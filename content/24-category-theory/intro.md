# Category theory

This volume teaches the category theory of a first graduate course and its form in Mathlib. A category has objects and morphisms between them, a composition of morphisms that is associative, and an identity morphism on each object. Groups with their homomorphisms, rings with ring homomorphisms, modules with linear maps, topological spaces with continuous maps and partial orders with monotone maps are categories. The earlier volumes of the handbook prove many theorems that have the same form in each of these settings: the free group and the free module, the product of groups and the product topology, the quotient group and the quotient topology. Category theory states such a theorem once, for an arbitrary category, and then reads off each case. The volume proves the general theorems in full and computes each of them in the categories of the earlier volumes.

Chapter 1 defines categories and gives the first examples: the category of types, a preorder as a category, a monoid as a category with one object, the opposite category, and the concrete categories of groups, rings, modules, topological spaces and orders. It studies isomorphisms, monomorphisms and epimorphisms, and shows that the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is an epimorphism of rings. Chapter 2 defines functors, natural transformations and the functor categories, and proves that a functor is an equivalence of categories if and only if it is fully faithful and essentially surjective. Chapter 3 proves the Yoneda lemma, studies representable functors and the category of elements, and computes the Yoneda embedding of a poset.

Chapter 4 defines limits and colimits as terminal cones and initial cocones. It computes products, equalizers and pullbacks in types, groups and topological spaces, proves that products and equalizers give all limits, and studies colimits, filtered colimits and the preservation, reflection and creation of limits by a functor. Chapter 5 defines adjunctions in their two equivalent forms, computes the adjunction between the free group and the forgetful functor, proves that right adjoints preserve limits, studies reflective subcategories and comma categories, and proves the general adjoint functor theorem. Chapter 6 defines monads and their algebras, the Kleisli category, and states Beck's monadicity theorem. Chapter 7 defines monoidal categories, states Mac Lane's coherence theorem, and studies braided, symmetric and closed monoidal categories and the monoid objects in them. Chapter 8 defines zero objects, kernels, cokernels and biproducts, and gives the definition of an abelian category with its main example, the modules over a ring.

The volume needs Volume 5 on groups, Volume 6 on rings, Volume 7 on modules and linear maps, and Volume 13 on topological spaces, for the examples. The tensor product of modules in Chapter 7 is in Volume 8, and the Galois connections that Chapter 5 reads as adjunctions are in Volume 4, Chapter 6. The level is that of a first graduate course, close to Mac Lane, *Categories for the Working Mathematician*, Chapters I to VII, and to Riehl, *Category Theory in Context*, Chapters 1 to 5. Read the chapters in order. Chapters 6, 7 and 8 need Chapters 1 to 5 but not each other, except that Chapter 7 uses monads from Chapter 6 in one example.

Mathlib writes `X ⟶ Y` for the type of morphisms from `X` to `Y`, `𝟙 X` for the identity of `X`, and `f ≫ g` for the composite "first `f`, then `g`". So `f ≫ g` is the composite $g \circ f$ of the textbook. The order of `≫` follows the arrows of a diagram, and the text says so where the order matters. A category in Mathlib is a type `C` with an instance `Category.{v, u} C`. The universe level `u` is the level of the objects and `v` the level of the morphisms. A small category has `u = v`, and the category of types `Type u` has objects in `Type (u + 1)` and morphisms in `Type u`. A functor is a structure `C ⥤ D` with the fields `obj` and `map`, and a natural transformation `α : F ⟶ G` has the components `α.app X`. Most constructions of the library, such as `limit F` and `pullback f g`, take a class like `HasLimit F` as a hypothesis, and give a chosen limit with its universal property. The text explains these choices where they appear.

The Mathlib folder `Mathlib/CategoryTheory` is large, and the volume teaches only its core. It leaves out bicategories, enriched categories, fibered categories, Kan extensions, the Grothendieck construction, profunctors, Markov categories and the many special shapes of limits that the library has. The modules of these topics have their home in this volume, and a later edition can add chapters on them. Other parts of the folder have their home in later volumes. Volume 25, Higher structures in categories, takes preadditive and abelian categories in full, triangulated categories, localization, shifts, sites and sheaves, presentable categories and Galois categories. Chapter 8 of this volume only opens that subject. Volume 26, Homological algebra, takes chain complexes, homology, exact sequences and derived functors, and the concrete categories of `Mathlib/Algebra/Category`, such as `GrpCat`, `RingCat` and `ModuleCat`. This volume uses those categories as examples and shows their Mathlib names in the boxes, but it does not explain their files. Volume 27 takes the category `TopCat` and simplicial sets. The volume has no Lean companion yet, so the sections show the Mathlib statements and give no Lean exercises.
