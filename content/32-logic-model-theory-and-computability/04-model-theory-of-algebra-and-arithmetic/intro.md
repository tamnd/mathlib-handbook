# Model theory of algebra and arithmetic

This chapter applies the general theory to two classical structures: fields and the natural numbers with addition. Section 1 defines the language of rings and compatible ring structures, turns elements of a free commutative ring into terms, and proves that the zero locus of finitely many polynomials is definable over their coefficients. It writes the field axioms, the theories of fields of characteristic $p$ and of algebraically closed fields, proves that $\mathrm{ACF}_p$ is categorical in each uncountable cardinal and so complete, and deduces the Lefschetz principle, with the Ax-Grothendieck theorem as an application. Section 2 defines the language of Presburger arithmetic, linear and semilinear sets, proves that the definable subsets of $\mathbb{N}^k$ are the semilinear sets, describes the definable subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ as the ultimately periodic sets, and concludes that the squares and multiplication are not definable from addition.

The chapter needs formulas and structures from Chapter 1, compactness, complete theories and the Łoś-Vaught test from Chapter 2, and definable sets from Chapter 3. Section 1 also uses rings, the characteristic and polynomials from Volume 6, and algebraic closures from Volume 11. Section 2 uses finite sets from Volume 3 and commutative monoids from Volume 5.

The next chapters leave model theory for computability. A set of natural numbers is ultimately periodic if and only if the words $a^n$ with $n$ in the set form a regular language, so Section 2 connects with the finite automata of Chapter 5. The recursive functions of Chapter 6 make precise the computably enumerable sets, which multiplication makes definable and addition alone does not. Volume 33 takes up the data structures and algorithms behind these computations.
