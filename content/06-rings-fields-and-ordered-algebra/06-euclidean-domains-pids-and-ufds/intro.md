# Euclidean domains, PIDs and UFDs

This chapter studies divisibility in an integral domain. In the integers every number factors into primes in one way, up to order and sign. The chapter asks which rings have the same property, and it proves a chain of implications that answers the question for many rings of interest: a Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain, and a principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain. Section 1 defines Euclidean domains and principal ideal domains, proves the first implication, and treats the greatest common divisor and the Euclidean algorithm. Section 2 studies the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: it proves that they form a Euclidean domain and uses this to prove the theorem of Fermat that a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is a sum of two squares. Section 3 defines irreducible and prime elements and unique factorization domains, and proves the second implication.

The chapter needs the ideals and quotient rings of Chapter 2, the integral domains of Chapter 3, and the division with remainder in $K[X]$ of Chapter 5. Section 2 uses Wilson's theorem from Chapter 4.

The chain does not reverse. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a unique factorization domain but not a principal ideal domain, and the ring $\mathbb{Z}[(1 + \sqrt{-19})/2]$ is a principal ideal domain but not a Euclidean domain. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a unique factorization domain at all. Volume 9 takes up factorization in polynomial rings, with the lemma of Gauss, and Volume 12 on number theory continues with the rings of integers of number fields, where unique factorization of elements fails and unique factorization of ideals takes its place. In Mathlib the three notions are the classes `EuclideanDomain`, `IsPrincipalIdealRing` and `UniqueFactorizationMonoid`, and the two implications are instances, so instance search finds them.
