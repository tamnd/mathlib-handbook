## Fractional ideals and invertibility

### Overview

The nonzero ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $(n)$ with $n \ge 1$, and they form a monoid under multiplication, isomorphic to the positive integers. To get a group, one adds the inverses $(1/n)$, the subgroups $\frac1n \mathbb{Z}$ of $\mathbb{Q}$. In a domain $A$ with fraction field $K$, the same step gives the fractional ideals: $A$-submodules of $K$ with a common denominator. In a Dedekind domain the nonzero fractional ideals form a group, and this property characterizes the Dedekind domains among all domains.

The group of fractional ideals is the place where the factorization of Section 3 lives, and its quotient by the principal fractional ideals is the class group of Chapter 5.

### Definitions

Let $A$ be a domain with field of fractions $K$.

**Definition (Fractional ideal).** A fractional ideal of $A$ is an $A$-submodule $I \subseteq K$ such that $dI \subseteq A$ for some $d \in A$, $d \ne 0$.

An ideal of $A$ is a fractional ideal with $d = 1$, an integral fractional ideal. Every fractional ideal is $d^{-1} J$ for the ideal $J = dI$ of $A$. For $x \in K$ the principal fractional ideal is $(x) = xA$, with $d$ the denominator of $x$. Every finitely generated $A$-submodule of $K$ is a fractional ideal, with $d$ the product of the denominators of the generators.

**Definition (Product and inverse).** For fractional ideals $I, J$, the product $IJ$ is the submodule generated by the products $xy$, $x \in I$, $y \in J$. For $I \ne 0$, the inverse is
$$I^{-1} = (A : I) = \{x \in K : xI \subseteq A\}.$$
The fractional ideal $I$ is invertible if $IJ = A$ for some fractional ideal $J$.

The product has the denominator $dd'$, and $(x)(y) = (xy)$. The inverse is a fractional ideal: if $a \in I$ is nonzero, written $a = b/c$ with $b, c \in A$, then $bI^{-1} = caI^{-1} \subseteq cA \subseteq A$. Always $II^{-1} \subseteq A$. If $IJ = A$, then $J \subseteq I^{-1}$, and $I^{-1} = I^{-1}IJ \subseteq J$, so $J = I^{-1}$. A principal fractional ideal $(x) \ne 0$ is invertible with inverse $(x^{-1})$.

::: mathlib
`FractionalIdeal.mul_inv_cancel_iff` · `Mathlib.RingTheory.FractionalIdeal.Inverse`

`I * I⁻¹ = 1 ↔ ∃ J, I * J = 1`: a fractional ideal is invertible if and only if its inverse in the sense of the definition is a two-sided inverse. In Mathlib `I⁻¹` is defined as `1 / I` (`FractionalIdeal.inv_eq`), with `1 / I` the colon ideal, and `FractionalIdeal.mem_inv_iff` says `x ∈ I⁻¹ ↔ ∀ y ∈ I, x * y ∈ 1` for `I ≠ 0`. The unit fractional ideal is `1`, the image of `A`. For principal ideals, `FractionalIdeal.spanSingleton_inv` and `FractionalIdeal.spanSingleton_mul_inv` give the rules above.

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (Invertible ideals are finitely generated).** An invertible fractional ideal is a finitely generated $A$-module.

*Proof.* From $II^{-1} = A$ write $1 = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$ with $x_i \in I$, $y_i \in I^{-1}$. For $z \in I$, $z = \sum x_i (y_i z)$, with $y_i z \in A$. So $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ generate $I$. $\square$

**Lemma (Invertible over a local ring).** Let $A$ be a local domain. An invertible fractional ideal of $A$ is principal.

*Proof.* Write $1 = \sum x_i y_i$ as above. Each $x_i y_i$ lies in $A$, and their sum is $1$, so one of them, say $x_1 y_1$, is not in the maximal ideal and is a unit $u$ of $A$. For $z \in I$, $z = x_1 \cdot (u^{-1} y_1 z)$ with $u^{-1} y_1 z \in A$. So $I = x_1 A$. $\square$

**Lemma (Inverse and localization).** Let $I$ be a finitely generated fractional ideal and $S \subseteq A$ a multiplicative subset with $0 \notin S$. Then $S^{-1}(I^{-1}) = (S^{-1}I)^{-1}$ in $K$, the inverse on the right being over $S^{-1}A$.

*Proof.* If $x \in I^{-1}$ and $s \in S$, then $(x/s) S^{-1}I \subseteq S^{-1}A$. Conversely, let $I = (a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ and $x \in (S^{-1}I)^{-1}$. Then $xa_i \in S^{-1}A$, say $xa_i = b_i/s_i$. With $s = s_1 \cdots s_n$, $sx a_i \in A$ for all $i$, so $sx \in I^{-1}$ and $x = (sx)/s \in S^{-1}(I^{-1})$. $\square$

**Theorem (Invertibility).** Let $A$ be a domain. Then $A$ is a Dedekind domain if and only if every nonzero fractional ideal of $A$ is invertible.

*Proof.* Assume $A$ is Dedekind, and let $I \ne 0$ be a fractional ideal. It is $d^{-1}J$ for an ideal $J$, finitely generated since $A$ is Noetherian, so $I$ is finitely generated. The product $II^{-1}$ is an ideal of $A$. If it is not $A$, it lies in a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, which is nonzero unless $A$ is a field, and for a field the claim is clear. The localization $A_{\mathfrak m}$ is a DVR (Section 1), so the fractional ideal $I_{\mathfrak m} = IA_{\mathfrak m}$ is principal, $I_{\mathfrak m} = xA_{\mathfrak m}$, and invertible. By the localization lemma,
$$(II^{-1})_{\mathfrak m} = I_{\mathfrak m} (I^{-1})_{\mathfrak m} = I_{\mathfrak m} (I_{\mathfrak m})^{-1} = A_{\mathfrak m}.$$
But $II^{-1} \subseteq \mathfrak m$ gives $(II^{-1})_{\mathfrak m} \subseteq \mathfrak m A_{\mathfrak m} \ne A_{\mathfrak m}$. This is a contradiction, so $II^{-1} = A$.

Conversely, assume every nonzero fractional ideal is invertible. Every nonzero ideal is invertible, so finitely generated by the first lemma, and $A$ is Noetherian. Let $\mathfrak p \ne 0$ be prime. Every nonzero ideal of $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is $JA_{\mathfrak p}$ for an ideal $J \ne 0$ of $A$, and from $JJ^{-1} = A$ follows $J_{\mathfrak p}(J^{-1})_{\mathfrak p} = A_{\mathfrak p}$. So it is invertible, and principal by the second lemma. So $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is a local PID, not a field since $\mathfrak p A_{\mathfrak p} \ne 0$, that is a DVR. By the theorem of Section 1, $A$ is Dedekind. $\square$

**Corollary (The group of fractional ideals).** For a Dedekind domain $A$, the nonzero fractional ideals form a commutative group under multiplication, with identity $A$. In particular, $IJ = IJ'$ with $I \ne 0$ implies $J = J'$.

::: mathlib
`FractionalIdeal.semifield` · `Mathlib.RingTheory.DedekindDomain.Ideal.Basic`

For a Dedekind domain, the instance `FractionalIdeal.semifield` makes `FractionalIdeal A⁰ K` a semifield: addition is the sum of submodules, multiplication is the product, and every nonzero element has the inverse `I⁻¹`. So the lemmas of a group with zero apply, such as `mul_inv_cancel₀`. The group of nonzero fractional ideals is `(FractionalIdeal A⁰ K)ˣ`.

### Examples

**Example (The integers).** The fractional ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $0$ and $q\mathbb{Z}$ for $q \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, since every finitely generated subgroup of $\mathbb{Q}$ is cyclic. The group of nonzero fractional ideals is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ under multiplication, and by unique factorization to the free abelian group on the primes. The inverse of $\frac{2}{3}\mathbb{Z}$ is $\frac{3}{2}\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example (An inverse in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$).** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ and $\mathfrak p = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$. Then $\mathfrak p^2 = (4, 2 + 2\sqrt{-5}, (1 + \sqrt{-5})^2)$, and $(1 + \sqrt{-5})^2 = -4 + 2\sqrt{-5}$. All three generators are multiples of $2$, and $2 = (2 + 2\sqrt{-5}) - (-4 + 2\sqrt{-5}) - 4$ lies in $\mathfrak p^2$. So $\mathfrak p^2 = (2)$, and $\mathfrak p^{-1} = \frac12 \mathfrak p$, since $\mathfrak p \cdot \frac12\mathfrak p = \frac12 (2) = A$. Explicitly, $\mathfrak p^{-1} = A + \frac{1 + \sqrt{-5}}{2} A$.

**Example (Not invertible).** Let $A = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ and $\mathfrak p = (2, 1 + \sqrt{-3})$. Then $\mathfrak p^2 = 2\mathfrak p$, computed as in the previous example with $(1 + \sqrt{-3})^2 = -2 + 2\sqrt{-3}$. If $\mathfrak p$ were invertible, cancellation would give $\mathfrak p = (2)$, but $1 + \sqrt{-3} \notin (2)$. So $\mathfrak p$ is not invertible, in agreement with the theorem, since $A$ is not Dedekind. Here $\mathfrak p^{-1} = \{x : x\mathfrak p \subseteq A\}$ contains $\omega = (1 + \sqrt{-3})/2$, and $\mathfrak p \mathfrak p^{-1} = \mathfrak p$.

**Example (Two variables).** In $A = k[X, Y]$, the ideal $\mathfrak m = (X, Y)$ has $\mathfrak m^{-1} = A$: if $f/g \cdot X$ and $f/g \cdot Y$ lie in $A$, then $g \mid fX$ and $g \mid fY$ with $\gcd(X, Y) = 1$, so $g \mid f$. So $\mathfrak m \mathfrak m^{-1} = \mathfrak m \ne A$, and $\mathfrak m$ is not invertible.

### The library around it

The fractional ideals are in the folder `Mathlib/RingTheory/FractionalIdeal/`: `Basic.lean` (the type, coercions, the semiring structure), `Operations.lean` (principal fractional ideals, maps, finiteness), `Inverse.lean` (the inverse and its rules, for any domain), and `Norm.lean` (the absolute norm of Volume 12). The facts that need a Dedekind domain are in `DedekindDomain/Ideal/Basic.lean`. The lemma `FractionalIdeal.exists_eq_spanSingleton_mul` writes every fractional ideal as `spanSingleton (a⁻¹) * J` with `J` an ideal, the form $d^{-1}J$ of the definition.

Common errors of a learner:

- The submonoid is part of the type: `FractionalIdeal (nonZeroDivisors A) K`, written `FractionalIdeal A⁰ K` with the notation `A⁰` open in the scope `nonZeroDivisors`.
- `I⁻¹` is defined for every domain, also when `I` is not invertible. The equation `I * I⁻¹ = 1` is the content of invertibility.
- The zero fractional ideal is `0`, also written `⊥`. The lemmas mix both forms; `FractionalIdeal.coeIdeal_eq_zero` translates.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find the inverse of the fractional ideal $\frac{3}{4}\mathbb{Z}[i]$ of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.
2. (A) Show that $(A : I) = A$ when $I = A$, and that $I \subseteq J$ implies $J^{-1} \subseteq I^{-1}$.
3. (A) Let $A$ be a PID. Show that every nonzero fractional ideal is principal and that the group of nonzero fractional ideals is $K^\times/A^\times$.
4. (B) Let $A = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ and $\mathfrak q = (3, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$. Show that $\mathfrak q \bar{\mathfrak q} = (3)$, where $\bar{\mathfrak q} = (3, 1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and find $\mathfrak q^{-1}$.
5. (B) Show that a fractional ideal $I$ is invertible if and only if it is finitely generated and $I_{\mathfrak m}$ is principal for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$.
6. (B) Show that an invertible fractional ideal is a projective $A$-module. Use $1 = \sum x_i y_i$ to split the map $A^n \to I$, $e_i \mapsto x_i$.
7. (C) Let $A$ be a Noetherian domain in which every nonzero prime ideal is invertible. Show that $A$ is a Dedekind domain.

### Notes and history

Dedekind proved that the ideals of a ring of integers can be inverted, and used it to prove unique factorization. The term "fractional ideal" and the characterization of Dedekind domains by invertibility are in Noether's 1927 paper on the abstract theory. The proof by localization follows Atiyah and Macdonald, Theorem 9.8.
