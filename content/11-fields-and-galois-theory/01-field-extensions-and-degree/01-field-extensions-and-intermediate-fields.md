## Field extensions and intermediate fields

### Overview

The field $\mathbb{Q}$ has no root of $X^2 - 2$. The field $\mathbb{R}$ has one, and the smallest subfield of $\mathbb{R}$ that contains $\sqrt 2$ is the set of numbers $a + b\sqrt 2$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. Field theory studies such pairs: a field $K$, a larger field $L$, and the fields between them.

This section defines extensions, intermediate fields and the field $K(S)$ generated by a subset. It describes the elements of $K(S)$, shows that adjunction can happen in steps, and finds the prime field of each field.

The section needs fields and subfields from Volume 6, Chapter 3, the characteristic from Volume 6, Chapter 4, and algebras and `Algebra.adjoin` from Volume 6, Chapter 8.

### Definitions

**Definition (Field extension).** Let $K$ and $L$ be fields. A field extension $L/K$ is a ring homomorphism $\iota \colon K \to L$. The field $K$ is the base and $L$ is the extension field.

A ring homomorphism from a field is injective: its kernel is an ideal of $K$ that does not contain $1$, so it is $0$. Thus $\iota$ identifies $K$ with the subfield $\iota(K)$ of $L$, and the text often writes $a$ for $\iota(a)$.

The map $\iota$ makes $L$ a $K$-algebra: $a \cdot x = \iota(a)\,x$ for $a \in K$ and $x \in L$. In particular $L$ is a vector space over $K$. Section 2 studies its dimension.

**Definition (Intermediate field).** Let $L/K$ be an extension. An intermediate field is a subfield $E$ of $L$ with $\iota(K) \subseteq E$. Equivalently, $E$ is a $K$-subalgebra of $L$ that is closed under inverses, since a subalgebra contains $\iota(a) = a \cdot 1$ for each $a \in K$.

The intermediate fields of $L/K$ are ordered by inclusion. An intersection of intermediate fields is an intermediate field, so the order is a complete lattice. Its bottom $\bot$ is $\iota(K)$ and its top $\top$ is $L$. The meet $E \sqcap F$ is $E \cap F$. The join $E \sqcup F$ is the smallest intermediate field that contains $E \cup F$, the compositum $EF$.

**Definition (Field generated by a subset).** Let $L/K$ be an extension and $S \subseteq L$ a subset. The field $K(S)$ is the intersection of all intermediate fields that contain $S$. For $S = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$ write $K(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$.

An extension $L/K$ is finitely generated if $L = K(S)$ for a finite $S$. It is simple if $L = K(\alpha)$ for one $\alpha$.

**Definition (Prime field).** The prime field of a field $L$ is the intersection of all subfields of $L$. It is the bottom element of the lattice of subfields.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Universal property of adjunction).** Let $L/K$ be an extension, $S \subseteq L$ and $E$ an intermediate field. Then $K(S) \subseteq E$ if and only if $S \subseteq E$.

*Proof.* The field $K(S)$ is an intersection of intermediate fields. Each of them contains $S$, so $S \subseteq K(S)$. If $K(S) \subseteq E$, then $S \subseteq E$. Conversely, if $S \subseteq E$, then $E$ is one of the fields in the intersection that defines $K(S)$, so $K(S) \subseteq E$. $\square$

So $S \mapsto K(S)$ is left adjoint to the map that sends an intermediate field to its set. It follows that $E \sqcup F = K(E \cup F)$.

**Theorem (Elements of $K(S)$).** Let $L/K$ be an extension and $S \subseteq L$. Let $K[S]$ be the $K$-subalgebra of $L$ generated by $S$. Then
$$K(S) = \{\, r s^{-1} : r, s \in K[S],\ s \neq 0 \,\}.$$

*Proof.* Call the right side $F$. Each element of $K[S]$ lies in $K(S)$, since $K(S)$ is a subalgebra that contains $S$. The field $K(S)$ is closed under inverses and products, so $F \subseteq K(S)$.

For the other inclusion, it is enough to prove that $F$ is an intermediate field that contains $S$. It contains $S$ and $\iota(K)$, since $r = r \cdot 1^{-1}$ for $r \in K[S]$. Let $r/s$ and $r'/s'$ be in $F$. Then
$$\frac{r}{s} - \frac{r'}{s'} = \frac{rs' - r's}{ss'}, \qquad \frac{r}{s} \cdot \frac{r'}{s'} = \frac{rr'}{ss'}.$$
The numerators and the denominator $ss'$ lie in $K[S]$, and $ss' \neq 0$ because $L$ has no zero divisors. If $r/s \neq 0$, then $r \neq 0$ and $(r/s)^{-1} = s/r \in F$. So $F$ is a subfield that contains $\iota(K)$, and the universal property gives $K(S) \subseteq F$. $\square$

So $K(S)$ is the field of fractions of the domain $K[S]$. For one element, $K(\alpha)$ is the set of values $p(\alpha)/q(\alpha)$ with $p, q \in K[X]$ and $q(\alpha) \neq 0$. In Mathlib this is `IntermediateField.mem_adjoin_iff_div`. It has no hypothesis $s \neq 0$, since `r / 0 = 0` in a Mathlib field.

**Theorem (Adjunction in steps).** Let $L/K$ be an extension and $S, T \subseteq L$. Then $K(S \cup T) = K(S)(T)$, where the right side is the field generated by $T$ over the base $K(S)$. In particular $K(\alpha, \beta) = K(\alpha)(\beta)$.

*Proof.* An intermediate field of $L/K(S)$ is a subfield of $L$ that contains $K(S)$. It is also an intermediate field of $L/K$. The field $K(S)(T)$ contains $K(S) \supseteq S$ and it contains $T$. So it contains $S \cup T$, and the universal property over $K$ gives $K(S \cup T) \subseteq K(S)(T)$.

Conversely, $K(S \cup T)$ contains $S$, so it contains $K(S)$ by the universal property over $K$. Thus it is an intermediate field of $L/K(S)$. It contains $T$, so the universal property over $K(S)$ gives $K(S)(T) \subseteq K(S \cup T)$. $\square$

**Theorem (Prime field).** Let $L$ be a field and $P$ its prime field. If $\operatorname{char} L = 0$, then $P \cong \mathbb{Q}$. If $\operatorname{char} L = p > 0$, then $p$ is prime and $P \cong \mathbb{F}_p$.

*Proof.* Let $\chi \colon \mathbb{Z} \to L$ be the unique ring homomorphism. Its image lies in each subfield, since a subfield contains $1$ and is closed under sums and negatives. Its kernel is $n\mathbb{Z}$ with $n = \operatorname{char} L$, and Volume 6, Chapter 4 proves that $n$ is $0$ or a prime.

Let $n = p$ be prime. The image $\chi(\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{F}_p$ is a field, so it is a subfield of $L$. It lies in every subfield, so it is the prime field.

Let $n = 0$. Then $\chi$ is injective, and $m/k \mapsto \chi(m)\chi(k)^{-1}$ is a well defined ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Q} \to L$, since $mk' = m'k$ gives $\chi(m)\chi(k') = \chi(m')\chi(k)$. Its image $Q$ is a subfield. Each subfield contains $\chi(\mathbb{Z})$ and the inverses of its nonzero elements, so it contains $Q$. Thus $P = Q \cong \mathbb{Q}$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$).** Take $L = \mathbb{R}$ and $K = \mathbb{Q}$. By Volume 6, Chapter 3, $F = \{a + b\sqrt 2 : a, b \in \mathbb{Q}\}$ is a subfield, and each of its elements lies in every field that contains $\sqrt 2$. So $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2) = F$. By adjunction in steps, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = F(\sqrt 3)$. The same argument gives $F(\sqrt 3) = \{u + v\sqrt 3 : u, v \in F\}$, once $\sqrt 3 \notin F$ is known. Suppose $\sqrt 3 = a + b\sqrt 2$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $3 = a^2 + 2b^2 + 2ab\sqrt 2$, and $ab = 0$ since $\sqrt 2$ is irrational. If $b = 0$, then $a^2 = 3$. If $a = 0$, then $(2b)^2 = 6$. Neither equation has a rational solution. So
$$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \{a + b\sqrt 2 + c\sqrt 3 + d\sqrt 6 : a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Q}\}.$$
For instance $(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)^{-1} = \sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2$, because $(\sqrt 3 + \sqrt 2)(\sqrt 3 - \sqrt 2) = 1$.

**Example (The ring $K[\alpha]$ can be smaller than $K(\alpha)$).** The quotients in the theorem on $K(S)$ are needed in general. Lindemann proved in 1882 that $\pi$ is transcendental: $p(\pi) \neq 0$ for each nonzero $p \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$. So $p \mapsto p(\pi)$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{Q}[X] \to \mathbb{Q}[\pi]$. Since $\deg(Xq) \geq 1$ for each $q \neq 0$, the element $X$ has no inverse, so $\pi^{-1} \notin \mathbb{Q}[\pi]$ and $\mathbb{Q}[\pi] \subsetneq \mathbb{Q}(\pi)$. For $\alpha = \sqrt 2$, in contrast, $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt 2]$ is already a field. Chapter 2 proves that $K[\alpha] = K(\alpha)$ exactly when $\alpha$ is algebraic over $K$.

### The library around it

An `IntermediateField K L` coerces to a type that is again a field, and the instances `Algebra K ↥E`, `Algebra ↥E L` and `IsScalarTower K ↥E L` exist. The isomorphisms `IntermediateField.botEquiv K L : ↥⊥ ≃ₐ[K] K` and `IntermediateField.topEquiv : ↥⊤ ≃ₐ[K] L` move a statement about `⊥` or `⊤` to one about `K` or `L`. An algebra map `f : E →ₐ[K] L` of fields has an image `f.fieldRange : IntermediateField K L`.

**Common errors.** `K(α)` with ordinary parentheses is a function application; write `K⟮α⟯`. Intermediate fields over `K⟮α⟯` and over `K` need `restrictScalars` before `=`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2) = \mathbb{Q}(1 + 3\sqrt 2)$. Hint: show that each field contains the generator of the other, and use the universal property twice.

2. (A) Write $(1 + \sqrt 2)^{-1}$ and $(3 - 2\sqrt 2)^{-1}$ in the form $a + b\sqrt 2$. Hint: multiply by the conjugate $a - b\sqrt 2$.

3. (A) Let $E$ and $F$ be intermediate fields of $L/K$. Show that $E \sqcup F = K(E \cup F) = E(F)$, with $E(F)$ viewed over $K$. Hint: adjunction in steps, and $K(E) = E$.

4. (B) Show that $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$. Hint: let $\theta = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$. Compute $\theta^{-1}$ from the first example, then $\sqrt 3 = (\theta + \theta^{-1})/2$.

5. (B) Let $L$ be a field of characteristic $p$. Show that the prime field of $L$ is $\{0, 1, \dots, p-1\}$, the multiples of $1$. Deduce that a field with $4$ elements has a prime field with $2$ elements. Hint: the order of $1$ in the additive group divides the order of the group.

6. (B) Let $L/K$ be an extension and $\alpha \in L$ with $\alpha^2 \in K$ and $\alpha \notin K$. Show that $K(\alpha) = \{a + b\alpha : a, b \in K\}$. Hint: for $a + b\alpha \neq 0$ show $a^2 - b^2\alpha^2 \neq 0$ and multiply by $a - b\alpha$. The proof also works in characteristic $2$.

7. (C) Let $t$ be transcendental over $K$. Show that $K(t^2) \subsetneq K(t)$ and that $K(t) = K(t^2)(t)$ with $t^2 \in K(t^2)$. Hint: if $t = p(t^2)/q(t^2)$, compare the parity of the degrees in $t q(t^2) = p(t^2)$.

### Notes and history

Dedekind introduced the word "field" (Körper) for fields of numbers. Steinitz wrote the first abstract theory of fields in 1910, in the article "Algebraische Theorie der Körper". The prime field and the classification by characteristic are part of that article.
