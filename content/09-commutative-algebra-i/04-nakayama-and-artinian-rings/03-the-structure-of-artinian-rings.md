## The structure of Artinian rings

### Overview

This section draws the consequences of the three facts of Section 2. First, $R$ is the product $R/m_1^k \times \cdots \times R/m_n^k$ of local Artinian rings, by the Chinese remainder theorem of Chapter 1. Second, the theorem of Akizuki and Hopkins: a ring is Artinian if and only if it is Noetherian and every prime ideal is maximal. So every Artinian ring is Noetherian, although an Artinian module need not be.

### Definitions

In this section $R$ is a commutative ring. A ring has dimension $0$ if every prime ideal is maximal. Volume 10 defines the Krull dimension in general.

**Definition (Local Artinian ring).** A local Artinian ring is an Artinian ring with exactly one maximal ideal.

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (Vector spaces).** For a vector space $V$ over a field $k$, the following are equivalent: $V$ satisfies the ACC on subspaces; $V$ has finite dimension; $V$ satisfies the DCC on subspaces.

*Proof.* If $\dim V = d$ is finite, then in a strictly ascending or strictly descending chain of subspaces the dimensions are strictly monotone integers between $0$ and $d$, so a chain has at most $d + 1$ distinct terms. If $V$ has infinite dimension, it contains a linearly independent sequence $v_1, v_2, \dots$ (Volume 7, Chapter 2). The chain $U_n = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ is strictly ascending, and the chain $W_n = \operatorname{span}(v_n, v_{n+1}, \dots)$ is strictly descending, since $v_n \in W_n$ and $v_n \notin W_{n+1}$ by linear independence. $\square$

**Lemma (A product of maximal ideals that is zero).** Assume $m_1 m_2 \cdots m_n = 0$ for maximal ideals $m_1, \dots, m_n$, not necessarily distinct. Then $R$ is Noetherian if and only if $R$ is Artinian.

*Proof.* Let $P_0 = R$ and $P_i = m_1 \cdots m_i$ for $1 \le i \le n$, so $R = P_0 \supseteq P_1 \supseteq \cdots \supseteq P_n = 0$. The quotient $Q_i = P_{i-1}/P_i$ is killed by $m_i$, since $m_i P_{i-1} = P_i$. So $Q_i$ is a vector space over $k_i = R/m_i$, and its $R$-submodules are exactly its $k_i$-subspaces. By the previous lemma, $Q_i$ is Noetherian if and only if it is Artinian.

Assume $R$ is Noetherian. Then every $P_i$ and every $Q_i$ is Noetherian (Chapter 3, Section 1), so every $Q_i$ is Artinian. Prove by downward induction on $i$ that $P_i$ is Artinian. For $i = n$, $P_n = 0$. If $P_i$ is Artinian, then the exact sequence $0 \to P_i \to P_{i-1} \to Q_i \to 0$ and Section 2 give that $P_{i-1}$ is Artinian. For $i = 0$ this says that $R$ is Artinian. The converse is the same argument with the two conditions exchanged. $\square$

**Theorem (Akizuki-Hopkins).** A ring $R$ is Artinian if and only if it is Noetherian and every prime ideal of $R$ is maximal.

*Proof.* Assume $R$ is Artinian. By Section 2 every prime is maximal, there are finitely many maximal ideals $m_1, \dots, m_n$, and $J = m_1 \cap \cdots \cap m_n$ satisfies $J^k = 0$ for some $k$. Since $m_1 \cdots m_n \subseteq J$, the product $(m_1 \cdots m_n)^k$ of $nk$ maximal ideals is $0$. The lemma shows that $R$ is Noetherian.

Conversely, assume $R$ is Noetherian and every prime is maximal. If $R = 0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, by Chapter 3, Section 3, the ideal $0$ has finitely many minimal primes $p_1, \dots, p_n$, and every prime contains one of them. By Chapter 1, Section 2, the nilradical $N$ is the intersection of all primes, so $N = p_1 \cap \cdots \cap p_n$. The ideal $N$ is finitely generated, $N = (a_1, \dots, a_m)$, and $a_i^e = 0$ for some common $e$. The ideal $N^{m(e-1)+1}$ is generated by the monomials $a_1^{j_1} \cdots a_m^{j_m}$ with $j_1 + \cdots + j_m = m(e - 1) + 1$. In each of them some $j_i \ge e$, so it is $0$. So $N^s = 0$ with $s = m(e-1) + 1$. Then $(p_1 \cdots p_n)^s \subseteq N^s = 0$. The $p_i$ are maximal by assumption, so the lemma shows that $R$ is Artinian. $\square$

**Theorem (Structure theorem).** Let $R$ be Artinian with distinct maximal ideals $m_1, \dots, m_n$, and let $k$ satisfy $J(R)^k = 0$. Then
$$R \cong R/m_1^k \times \cdots \times R/m_n^k,$$
and each $R/m_i^k$ is a local Artinian ring with maximal ideal $m_i/m_i^k$.

*Proof.* For $i \ne j$, $m_i + m_j = R$ since both are maximal and distinct. By Chapter 1, coprime ideals have coprime powers, so $m_i^k + m_j^k = R$. By the Chinese remainder theorem of Chapter 1, the map $R \to \prod_i R/m_i^k$ is surjective with kernel $\bigcap_i m_i^k = \prod_i m_i^k$. This product is $(m_1 \cdots m_n)^k \subseteq J(R)^k = 0$. So the map is an isomorphism. A maximal ideal of $R/m_i^k$ is $m/m_i^k$ for a maximal $m \supseteq m_i^k$. Since $m$ is prime, $m \supseteq m_i$, so $m = m_i$. So $R/m_i^k$ is local, and it is Artinian as a quotient of $R$. $\square$

The decomposition is unique up to isomorphism and order of the factors, a fact that this section does not prove.

**Corollary (Local rings).** Let $(R, m)$ be a Noetherian local ring. Then $R$ is Artinian if and only if $m^k = 0$ for some $k$.

*Proof.* If $R$ is Artinian, then $J(R) = m$ is nilpotent by Section 2. If $m^k = 0$, then the product of $k$ copies of the maximal ideal $m$ is $0$, and the lemma shows that $R$ is Artinian. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The ring $\mathbb{Z}/72$).** Here $72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2$, the maximal ideals are $(2)$ and $(3)$, and $J = (6)$. In $\mathbb{Z}/72$, $6^2 = 36 \ne 0$ and $6^3 = 216 = 3 \cdot 72 = 0$, so the least $k$ is $3$. The ideal $(2)^3 = (8)$ and the ideal $(3)^3 = (27) = (9)$, since $\gcd(27, 72) = 9$. The structure theorem gives
$$\mathbb{Z}/72 \cong \mathbb{Z}/8 \times \mathbb{Z}/9,$$
the factorization of the Chinese remainder theorem of $\mathbb{Z}$. The factor $\mathbb{Z}/8$ is local with maximal ideal $(2)$, and $\mathbb{Z}/9$ is local with maximal ideal $(3)$.

**Example (An idempotent).** Let $k$ be a field and $R = k[x]/(x^2(x - 1))$, a $k$-algebra of dimension $3$. Its maximal ideals are $(\bar x)$ and $(\bar x - 1)$, and $R \cong k[x]/(x^2) \times k[x]/(x - 1) \cong k[x]/(x^2) \times k$. The decomposition comes from the idempotent $e = 1 - \bar x^2$. Indeed $e^2 - e = e(e - 1) = -\bar x^2 (1 - \bar x^2) = \bar x^2(\bar x - 1)(\bar x + 1) = 0$. So $R = Re \times R(1 - e)$. The element $e$ is $1$ modulo $x^2$ and $0$ modulo $x - 1$, so $Re \cong k[x]/(x^2)$ and $R(1 - e) = R\bar x^2 \cong k$.

**Counterexample (Dimension $0$ is needed).** The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian and not Artinian. The prime $(0)$ is not maximal, so the theorem of Akizuki-Hopkins does not apply.

**Counterexample (The Noetherian hypothesis is needed).** Let $k$ be a field, $A = k[x_1, x_2, \dots]$ and $R = A/m^2$ with $m = (x_1, x_2, \dots)$. Write $\bar m = m/m^2$. Then $\bar m^2 = 0$, so every element of $\bar m$ is nilpotent and lies in every prime. Since $R/\bar m \cong k$ is a field, $\bar m$ is the only prime ideal, and every prime is maximal. But $\bar m$ is a $k$-vector space with basis $\bar x_1, \bar x_2, \dots$, and since $\bar m \cdot \bar m = 0$, every subspace of $\bar m$ is an ideal of $R$. By the lemma on vector spaces, $\bar m$ has strictly ascending and strictly descending chains of subspaces. So $R$ has dimension $0$ and is neither Noetherian nor Artinian.

### The library around it

Mathlib proves the comparison through semiprimary rings, which covers noncommutative rings. This section gives the commutative proof. A second form of the structure theorem uses localizations: when the prime spectrum is discrete, as for an Artinian ring, `MaximalSpectrum.toPiLocalizationEquiv` in `Mathlib.RingTheory.Spectrum.Prime.Topology` gives $R \cong \prod_m R_m$.

The class `IsSemiprimaryRing` is in `Mathlib.RingTheory.Jacobson.Semiprimary`. By `isSemiprimaryRing_iff`, a ring is semiprimary when $R / J(R)$ is semisimple and $J(R)$ is nilpotent. The same module proves that the Jacobson radical of a semisimple module is zero, `IsSemisimpleModule.jacobson_eq_bot`, and so `IsSemisimpleRing.jacobson_eq_bot` holds for a semisimple ring. The instance `instIsReducedOfIsSemisimpleRing` says that a commutative semisimple ring is reduced.

Common errors of a learner:

- `IsArtinianRing.quotNilradicalPowEquivPi` indexes the product by `MaximalSpectrum R`, not by a list of ideals. To get a product of `n` factors, compose with an equivalence of index types.
- An Artinian ring need not be reduced, so `IsArtinianRing.equivPi` needs `[IsReduced R]`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Write $\mathbb{Z}/600$ as a product of local Artinian rings.
2. (A) Find the idempotents of $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3 - x)$ and the decomposition that they give.
3. (A) Show that a finite-dimensional algebra over a field has only finitely many prime ideals, and that all of them are maximal.
4. (B) Let $(R, m)$ be local Artinian. Show that every element of $R$ is a unit or nilpotent.
5. (B) Let $(R, m)$ be a Noetherian local ring with $m^k \ne m^{k+1}$ for all $k$. Show that $R$ is not Artinian. Hint: by Nakayama's lemma of Section 1, $m^k = m^{k+1}$ implies $m^k = 0$.
6. (B) Let $R$ be Artinian. Show that $\operatorname{Spec} R$ with the Zariski topology of Chapter 2 is finite and discrete.
7. (C) Let $R$ be Artinian and $M$ a finitely generated $R$-module. Show that $M$ has a chain of submodules $M = M_0 \supsetneq M_1 \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq M_r = 0$ with each $M_{i-1}/M_i \cong R/m$ for a maximal ideal $m$. Hint: the lemma on a product of maximal ideals, applied to $M$ in place of $R$.

### Notes and history

Yasuo Akizuki proved the commutative case in 1935. Charles Hopkins and Jacob Levitzki proved in 1939 that a ring with the descending chain condition on left ideals satisfies the ascending one, for rings that need not be commutative. This section follows Atiyah and Macdonald, *Introduction to Commutative Algebra*, Chapter 8.
