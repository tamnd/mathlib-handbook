## The continuum and cardinalities in mathematics

### Overview

The cardinal $2^{\aleph_0}$ of the set of subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ is the continuum $\mathfrak{c}$. It is the cardinality of $\mathbb{R}$, of $\mathbb{C}$, of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and of the set of real sequences. Volume 3, Chapter 8 proved that $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. This section computes with $\mathfrak{c}$ by the rules of Section 2: $\mathfrak{c} \cdot \mathfrak{c} = \mathfrak{c}$ and $\mathfrak{c}^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$. The continuum hypothesis says $\mathfrak{c} = \aleph_1$. The axioms of set theory do not decide it, and the index of this edition has no declaration for that independence.

The second half counts the structures of algebra. A free group, a free ring or a subfield generated by a set $s$ has at most $\max(|s|, \aleph_0)$ elements. Finitely supported functions and multisets have the size of their domain. The algebraic elements over a ring $R$ number at most $\max(|R|, \aleph_0)$. So the algebraic numbers are countable, and Cantor's count of 1874 follows: almost all real numbers are transcendental.

### Definitions

**Definition (Continuum).** The *continuum* is the cardinal $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}$.

**Definition (Algebraic and transcendental elements).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $A$ an $R$-algebra. An element $x \in A$ is *algebraic* over $R$ if $p(x) = 0$ for some nonzero $p \in R[X]$. Otherwise $x$ is *transcendental*. A real number is an *algebraic number* if it is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$.

The free monoid, the free group, the free ring and the free commutative ring on a type $\alpha$ are the usual universal objects. The free monoid is the set of words in $\alpha$. The free group is the set of reduced words in the letters $a$ and $a^{-1}$ for $a \in \alpha$. The free ring is the monoid ring $\mathbb{Z}[M]$ of the free monoid $M$, the finitely supported functions $M \to \mathbb{Z}$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Proposition (Arithmetic of the continuum).**

1. $\aleph_0 < \mathfrak{c}$, $\aleph_1 \le \mathfrak{c}$ and $\mathfrak{c} = \beth_1$.
2. $\mathfrak{c} + \mathfrak{c} = \mathfrak{c} \cdot \mathfrak{c} = \mathfrak{c}$.
3. $x^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$ for each cardinal $x$ with $2 \le x \le \mathfrak{c}$. In particular $n^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_0^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$ for $n \ge 2$.

*Proof.* Item 1: Cantor's theorem gives $\aleph_0 < 2^{\aleph_0}$, and $\aleph_1$ is the least cardinal above $\aleph_0$ (Section 1). Also $\beth_1 = 2^{\beth_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$. Item 2 is Hessenberg's theorem, since $\mathfrak{c}$ is infinite. Item 3: $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0} \le x^{\aleph_0} \le \mathfrak{c}^{\aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0 \cdot \aleph_0} = 2^{\aleph_0}$. $\square$

The cardinality of $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathfrak{c}$. Mathlib proves it in the analysis library. Each real number is the limit of a Cauchy sequence $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Q}$, which gives $|\mathbb{R}| \le \aleph_0^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$. The map $f \mapsto \sum_n f(n) 3^{-n}$ injects $\{0, 1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ into $\mathbb{R}$, which gives $\mathfrak{c} \le |\mathbb{R}|$.

**Theorem (Free structures).** Let $\alpha$ be a nonempty type. The free monoid and the free group on $\alpha$ have cardinality $\max(|\alpha|, \aleph_0)$. The free ring and the free commutative ring on any type $\alpha$ have cardinality $\max(|\alpha|, \aleph_0)$.

*Proof.* The free monoid is the set of lists in $\alpha$, of cardinality $\max(\aleph_0, |\alpha|)$ by Section 2. Free group, upper bound: a reduced word is a list of pairs $(a, \varepsilon)$ with $a \in \alpha$ and a sign $\varepsilon$, so the group injects into the lists in $\alpha \times \{1, -1\}$. These number $\max(\aleph_0, 2|\alpha|) = \max(\aleph_0, |\alpha|)$. Lower bound: the generators give $|\alpha|$ distinct elements, and the powers $a^n$ of one generator are distinct, which gives $\aleph_0$ elements.

Free ring: the free monoid $M$ has $\max(|\alpha|, \aleph_0)$ elements, also for empty $\alpha$, where $M$ has one element. A finitely supported function $M \to \mathbb{Z}$ is given by a list of pairs in $M \times \mathbb{Z}$, so $|\mathbb{Z}[M]| \le \max(|M|, \aleph_0)$. The ring contains $\mathbb{Z}$ and the elements of $M$, which gives the lower bound. The free commutative ring is the same with multisets in place of lists. $\square$

**Corollary (Each nonempty type carries a commutative ring).** A type $\alpha$ has a commutative ring structure if and only if it is nonempty.

*Proof.* A ring has an element $0$. Conversely, a finite $\alpha$ with $n \ge 1$ elements is in bijection with $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. An infinite $\alpha$ satisfies $|\alpha| = \max(|\alpha|, \aleph_0)$, the cardinality of the free commutative ring on $\alpha$. Transport the ring structure along a bijection. $\square$

**Proposition (Finitely supported functions).** Let $\alpha$ be infinite and let $\beta$ have an element $0$ and another element. Then $|\alpha \to_0 \beta| = \max(|\alpha|, |\beta|)$. For infinite $\alpha$, the multisets in $\alpha$ number $|\alpha|$.

*Proof.* A finitely supported function $f$ is determined by its graph on its support, a finite subset of $\alpha \times \beta$. So $|\alpha \to_0 \beta| \le |\mathrm{Finset}(\alpha \times \beta)| = |\alpha \times \beta| = \max(|\alpha|, |\beta|)$, by Section 2. For the lower bounds, fix $b \ne 0$: the functions $\delta_a b$ that take the value $b$ at $a$ and $0$ elsewhere are distinct for distinct $a$. Fix $a_0$: the functions $\delta_{a_0} b$ are distinct for distinct $b$. A multiset is a finitely supported function $\alpha \to \mathbb{N}$, and $|\mathbb{N}| \le |\alpha|$. $\square$

**Theorem (Subfield generated by a set).** Let $K$ be a division ring and $s \subseteq K$. The subfield generated by $s$ has at most $\max(|s|, \aleph_0)$ elements, and exactly $|s|$ elements if $s$ is infinite.

*Proof.* Put $\kappa = \max(|s|, \aleph_0)$. Let $S_0 = s \cup \{0, 1\}$, and let $S_{n+1}$ be $S_n$ together with all $x + y$, $x y$, $-x$ and $x^{-1}$ for $x, y \in S_n$. By induction, $|S_n| \le \kappa$: the set $S_{n+1}$ is the image of $S_n \times S_n \times \{1, 2\} \sqcup S_n \times \{1, 2\}$ under the operations, a set of cardinality at most $\kappa \cdot \kappa = \kappa$. The union $S = \bigcup_n S_n$ is closed under the operations, since two elements lie in a common $S_n$. So $S$ is the subfield generated by $s$, and $|S| \le \aleph_0 \cdot \kappa = \kappa$. If $s$ is infinite, $s \subseteq S$ gives $|s| \le |S|$. $\square$

Mathlib proves the bound in another way. It maps the trees of the type `WType` of terms built from the field operations onto the subfield. The count of such trees is the same bound.

**Theorem (Algebraic elements).** Let $R$ and $A$ be domains, with $A$ an $R$-algebra that is torsion free as an $R$-module. The set of elements of $A$ algebraic over $R$ has cardinality at most $\max(|R|, \aleph_0)$, and exactly $|R|$ if $R$ is infinite. If $R$ is countable, the set is countable. If also $A$ has characteristic zero, the set has cardinality $\aleph_0$.

*Proof.* The polynomial ring $R[X]$ is the set of finitely supported functions $\mathbb{N} \to R$, so $|R[X]| \le \max(|R|, \aleph_0)$ by the argument of the proposition. For each algebraic $x$, choose a nonzero $p_x \in R[X]$ with $p_x(x) = 0$. The fibre of $x \mapsto p_x$ over $p$ lies in the set of roots of $p$ in $A$. Since $A$ is torsion free, the image of $p$ in $A[X]$ is nonzero, and a nonzero polynomial over a domain has finitely many roots. So the algebraic elements number at most $|R[X]| \cdot \aleph_0 \le \max(|R|, \aleph_0)$.

If $R$ is infinite, the elements $r \cdot 1$, $r \in R$, are algebraic, as roots of $X - r$. They are distinct since $A$ is torsion free. So the count is $|R|$. If $R$ is countable, the bound is $\aleph_0$. If $A$ has characteristic zero, the integers $n \cdot 1$ are distinct and algebraic, so the count is $\aleph_0$. $\square$

**Corollary (Transcendental numbers).** The algebraic numbers in $\mathbb{R}$ and in $\mathbb{C}$ form a countable set. The transcendental real numbers number $\mathfrak{c}$.

*Proof.* Apply the theorem with $R = \mathbb{Q}$. The set $T$ of transcendental reals is the complement of a countable set $Q$ in $\mathbb{R}$. Since $|Q| \le \aleph_0 < \mathfrak{c} = |\mathbb{R}|$, Section 2 gives $|\mathbb{R}| = \max(|Q|, |T|)$, so $|T| = \mathfrak{c}$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Computations).** The set $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}$ of real sequences has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$. A continuous function $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is determined by its values on the dense set $\mathbb{Q}$, so there are at most $\mathfrak{c}^{\aleph_0} = \mathfrak{c}$ of them, and the constants show that there are exactly $\mathfrak{c}$. All functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ number $\mathfrak{c}^{\mathfrak{c}} = 2^{\mathfrak{c}}$. The free group on $\mathbb{N}$ is countable, and the free group on $\mathbb{R}$ has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$. The algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{C}$ is countable. The subfield of $\mathbb{R}$ generated by $\mathbb{Q} \cup \{\pi, e\}$ is countable.

**Counterexample (Countability needs a countable base).** Take $R = A = \mathbb{R}$. Each $x \in \mathbb{R}$ is a root of $X - x$, so all reals are algebraic over $\mathbb{R}$, and the set is uncountable. The theorem still holds: the count is $|R| = \mathfrak{c}$.

**Counterexample (The characteristic matters).** Take $R = A = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime $p$. The ring $R$ is countable, so the algebraic elements are countable, but there are only $p$ of them. The count $\aleph_0$ needs characteristic zero. In the same way, the free group on the empty type is trivial, so the hypothesis that $\alpha$ is nonempty is needed for the free group. A function $\alpha \to_0 \beta$ into a one-point $\beta$ is $0$, so $\beta$ needs a second element.

Common errors of a learner:

- `𝔠` is notation in the scope `Cardinal`. Without `open Cardinal` it does not parse.
- The continuum hypothesis is not a theorem. A statement such as `𝔠 = ℵ₁` cannot be proved or refuted in Mathlib without new axioms.
- The algebraic count needs `Module.IsTorsionFree R A`. For an algebra with torsion, the image of a polynomial can be $0$ and the fibre infinite.
- `Algebraic.countable` is a statement about a `Set`, while the cardinal lemmas use a subtype. Convert with `Set.Countable` and `Cardinal.mk_le_aleph0`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $|\mathbb{R}^n| = |\mathbb{C}| = \mathfrak{c}$ for each $n \ge 1$.
2. (A) Show that the set of open subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$. Hint: each open set is a union of intervals with rational ends.
3. (A) Show that the set of polynomials with integer coefficients is countable, without the theorem on algebraic elements.
4. (B) Show that the set of irrational real numbers has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$, and that a countable union of sets of cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$ has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$.
5. (B) Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$ with a basis $B$. Show $|V| = \max(|B|, \aleph_0)$ for $B \ne \emptyset$. Deduce that a basis of $\mathbb{R}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}$.
6. (B) Show that a field $K$ that is algebraic over its prime field is countable. Show that $|K(x)| = \max(|K|, \aleph_0)$ for the field of rational functions.
7. (C) Show that the transcendence degree of $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathfrak{c}$. Hint: if $T$ is a transcendence basis, then $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}(T)$, and $|\mathbb{Q}(T)| \le \max(|T|, \aleph_0)$.

### Notes and history

Cantor proved in 1874 that the algebraic numbers are countable and that the real numbers are not, which gave a new proof that transcendental numbers exist. Liouville had constructed the first explicit transcendental numbers in 1844. Cantor stated the continuum hypothesis in 1878. Gödel in 1938 and Cohen in 1963 showed that the axioms of set theory neither prove nor refute it.
