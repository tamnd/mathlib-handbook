## Neighbourhoods, bases and generated topologies

### Overview

A topology has many open sets, and a proof rarely needs all of them. In $\mathbb{R}$ it is enough to know the open intervals: a set is open when it is a union of open intervals. A family of open sets with this property is a basis of the topology. A basis makes a topology concrete, and it lets a proof check a property on a small family. The neighbourhoods of a point are the local form of the same idea: a set is open when it is a neighbourhood of each of its points.

This section defines neighbourhoods, bases and the topology generated by any family of sets. It proves the criterion that says when a family is a basis, and builds the topology of $\mathbb{R}$ from the open intervals with rational end points. It defines the countability axioms and proves that a second countable space is separable. The Sorgenfrey line shows that the converse fails.

### Definitions

**Definition (Neighbourhood).** Let $X$ be a topological space and $x \in X$. A set $N \subseteq X$ is a neighbourhood of $x$ if there is an open set $U$ with $x \in U \subseteq N$. The family of all neighbourhoods of $x$ is written $\mathcal{N}(x)$.

*Remarks.* A neighbourhood need not be open: $[-1, 1]$ is a neighbourhood of $0$ in $\mathbb{R}$. Some older texts require neighbourhoods to be open. Mathlib, Bourbaki and this volume do not. The family $\mathcal{N}(x)$ has three properties: $X \in \mathcal{N}(x)$; if $N \in \mathcal{N}(x)$ and $N \subseteq M$ then $M \in \mathcal{N}(x)$; if $N, M \in \mathcal{N}(x)$ then $N \cap M \in \mathcal{N}(x)$. For the last one, take open $U \subseteq N$ and $V \subseteq M$ that contain $x$; then $U \cap V$ is open, contains $x$ and lies in $N \cap M$. These three properties say that $\mathcal{N}(x)$ is a filter, the subject of Chapter 2, where Mathlib's `nhds x` is defined.

**Definition (Basis).** Let $X$ be a topological space. A basis of the topology is a family $\mathcal{B}$ of open sets such that each open set is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$.

*Remarks.* The empty set is the union of the empty subfamily, so it needs no member of $\mathcal{B}$. A topology has many bases. The topology itself is a basis. In $\mathbb{R}$ the open intervals form a basis, and so do the open intervals with rational end points.

**Definition (Generated topology).** Let $\mathcal{S}$ be any family of subsets of a set $X$. The topology generated by $\mathcal{S}$ is the coarsest topology on $X$ in which each member of $\mathcal{S}$ is open: the intersection of all topologies that contain $\mathcal{S}$. The family $\mathcal{S}$ is then called a subbasis of this topology.

*Remarks.* An intersection of topologies is a topology, by a direct check of the axioms (Exercise 6 of Section 1). The discrete topology contains $\mathcal{S}$, so the intersection is over a nonempty family. The definition says what the open sets are only indirectly. The theorem on subbases below gives them explicitly.

::: mathlib
`TopologicalSpace.generateFrom` · `Mathlib.Topology.Order`

`generateFrom g` is the topology whose open sets are the sets `s` with `GenerateOpen g s`. This is an inductive predicate, not an intersection of topologies. Its constructors say that a member of `g` is open, that `univ` is open, and that the family is closed under binary intersections and arbitrary unions. The inductive form gives a principle of induction: to show that each open set of `generateFrom g` has a property, show it for the members of `g` and show that the property passes to intersections and unions. In the order of Section 1, `generateFrom g` is the largest topology (the coarsest) in which each set of `g` is open, and the lemma `le_generateFrom` says this.

**Definition (Countability axioms).** A topological space $X$ is

- first countable if each point $x$ has a countable family of neighbourhoods $N_1, N_2, \dots$ such that each neighbourhood of $x$ contains some $N_k$;
- second countable if the topology has a countable basis;
- separable if $X$ has a countable dense subset.

::: mathlib

The two are equivalent: the finite intersections of a countable family form a countable family, and by the theorem on subbases below they form a basis.

**Definition (Order topology).** Let $(X, \le)$ be a linear order. The order topology on $X$ is the topology generated by the open rays $(a, \infty) = \{x : a < x\}$ and $(-\infty, b) = \{x : x < b\}$ for $a, b \in X$. The usual topology of $\mathbb{R}$ is the order topology of its order.

::: mathlib

This form lets a type such as `ℝ` take its topology from its metric (Chapter 3) and then prove, as a theorem, that this topology is the order topology. ### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Open sets are the neighbourhoods of their points).** A set $U \subseteq X$ is open if and only if $U$ is a neighbourhood of each of its points.

*Proof.* If $U$ is open and $x \in U$, then $x \in U \subseteq U$, so $U$ is a neighbourhood of $x$. Conversely, assume that $U$ is a neighbourhood of each $x \in U$. For each $x \in U$ choose an open $V_x$ with $x \in V_x \subseteq U$. Then $U = \bigcup_{x \in U} V_x$: each $x \in U$ is in $V_x$, and each $V_x$ is in $U$. A union of open sets is open. $\square$

**Theorem (Criterion for a basis).** Let $X$ be a topological space and $\mathcal{B}$ a family of open sets. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis if and only if for each open $U$ and each $x \in U$ there is $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B \subseteq U$.

*Proof.* Assume $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis, and let $x \in U$ with $U$ open. Write $U = \bigcup_i B_i$ with $B_i \in \mathcal{B}$. Then $x \in B_i$ for some $i$, and $B_i \subseteq U$. Conversely, assume the condition, and let $U$ be open. For each $x \in U$ choose $B_x \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_x \subseteq U$. As in the last proof, $U = \bigcup_{x \in U} B_x$. $\square$

**Theorem (When a family is the basis of some topology).** Let $X$ be a set and $\mathcal{B}$ a family of subsets of $X$. There is a topology on $X$ with basis $\mathcal{B}$ if and only if

1. each $x \in X$ is in some $B \in \mathcal{B}$, and
2. for $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ there is $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.

The topology is then unique: its open sets are the unions of members of $\mathcal{B}$.

*Proof.* Assume that $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis of a topology $\mathcal{T}$. The set $X$ is open, so it is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$, and this gives 1. The set $B_1 \cap B_2$ is open, so the criterion gives $B_3$ for 2. The open sets of $\mathcal{T}$ are the unions of members of $\mathcal{B}$ by definition of a basis, so $\mathcal{T}$ is determined by $\mathcal{B}$.

Conversely, assume 1 and 2, and let $\mathcal{T}$ be the family of all unions of members of $\mathcal{B}$. Check the axioms. By 1, $X$ is the union of all members of $\mathcal{B}$, so $X \in \mathcal{T}$. A union of unions of members of $\mathcal{B}$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$, so $\mathcal{T}$ is closed under unions. For intersections, let $U = \bigcup_i B_i$ and $V = \bigcup_j C_j$ be in $\mathcal{T}$, and let $x \in U \cap V$. Then $x \in B_i \cap C_j$ for some $i$ and $j$. By 2 there is $D_x \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in D_x \subseteq B_i \cap C_j \subseteq U \cap V$. So $U \cap V = \bigcup_{x \in U \cap V} D_x$, and $U \cap V \in \mathcal{T}$. So $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology, each member of $\mathcal{B}$ is in $\mathcal{T}$, and $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis of $\mathcal{T}$ by construction. $\square$

**Theorem (Subbasis).** Let $\mathcal{S}$ be a family of subsets of $X$, and let $\mathcal{B}$ be the family of all finite intersections $S_1 \cap \dots \cap S_n$ of members of $\mathcal{S}$, where $n = 0$ gives $X$. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis of the topology generated by $\mathcal{S}$.

*Proof.* The family $\mathcal{B}$ satisfies conditions 1 and 2 of the last theorem: $X \in \mathcal{B}$, and the intersection of two finite intersections is a finite intersection. So $\mathcal{B}$ is the basis of a topology $\mathcal{T}$, whose open sets are the unions of members of $\mathcal{B}$. This topology contains $\mathcal{S}$. Each topology that contains $\mathcal{S}$ contains the finite intersections of members of $\mathcal{S}$ and their unions, so it contains $\mathcal{T}$. So $\mathcal{T}$ is the coarsest topology that contains $\mathcal{S}$, that is, the generated topology. $\square$

**Theorem (A second countable space is separable and first countable).** Let $X$ have a countable basis $\mathcal{B}$. Then $X$ has a countable dense subset, and each point has a countable family of neighbourhoods as in the definition of first countability.

*Proof.* For each nonempty $B \in \mathcal{B}$ choose a point $x_B \in B$. The set $D = \{x_B\}$ is countable. It is dense: a nonempty open set $U$ contains a nonempty $B \in \mathcal{B}$, by the definition of a basis, so $x_B \in U \cap D$. By the corollary on dense sets of Section 1, $D$ is dense. For the second claim, fix $x$. The members of $\mathcal{B}$ that contain $x$ form a countable family. If $N$ is a neighbourhood of $x$, there is an open $U$ with $x \in U \subseteq N$, and by the criterion there is $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B \subseteq U \subseteq N$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The topology of $\mathbb{R}$).** Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the family of the open intervals $(a, b)$ with $a < b$ rational. Condition 1 holds: $x \in (\lfloor x \rfloor - 1, \lfloor x \rfloor + 1)$. Condition 2 holds: $(a, b) \cap (c, d) = (\max(a, c), \min(b, d))$ is again such an interval when it is nonempty. So $\mathcal{B}$ is the basis of a topology. This topology is the usual one, in which $U$ is open when each $x \in U$ has an $\varepsilon$-interval in $U$. If $x \in (x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon) \subseteq U$, choose rationals $a \in (x - \varepsilon, x)$ and $b \in (x, x + \varepsilon)$, by the density of $\mathbb{Q}$; then $x \in (a, b) \subseteq U$. Conversely each $(a, b)$ is open in the $\varepsilon$ sense, with $\varepsilon = \min(x - a, b - x)$. The family $\mathcal{B}$ is in bijection with a subset of $\mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}$, so it is countable, and $\mathbb{R}$ is second countable. The open rays $(a, \infty)$ and $(-\infty, b)$ form a subbasis, since $(a, b) = (a, \infty) \cap (-\infty, b)$. So the usual topology is the order topology.

**Counterexample (The Sorgenfrey line).** Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the family of the half-open intervals $[a, b)$ with $a < b$ real. The two conditions hold, as for open intervals, so $\mathcal{B}$ is the basis of a topology on $\mathbb{R}$, the Sorgenfrey line $\mathbb{R}_\ell$. It is finer than the usual topology, since $(a, b) = \bigcup_n [a + 1/n, b)$. It is first countable: the sets $[x, x + 1/n)$ form a countable family of neighbourhoods of $x$ as required. It is separable: $\mathbb{Q}$ meets each $[a, b)$. It is not second countable. Suppose that $\mathcal{C}$ is a countable basis. For each $x \in \mathbb{R}$ the set $[x, x + 1)$ is open, so by the criterion there is $C_x \in \mathcal{C}$ with $x \in C_x \subseteq [x, x + 1)$. Then $x = \min C_x$. So $x \ne y$ gives $C_x \ne C_y$, and $x \mapsto C_x$ is an injection of $\mathbb{R}$ into the countable set $\mathcal{C}$. This is impossible, because $\mathbb{R}$ is uncountable. So the converse of the last theorem fails.

**Example (The cofinite topology on an uncountable set is not first countable).** Let $X$ be uncountable with the cofinite topology, and fix $x \in X$. Suppose that $N_1, N_2, \dots$ are neighbourhoods of $x$. Each contains an open set $U_k \ni x$, and $X \setminus U_k$ is finite. The union $E = \bigcup_k (X \setminus U_k)$ is countable, so there is $y \in X$ with $y \ne x$ and $y \notin E$. Then $y \in U_k \subseteq N_k$ for each $k$. The set $X \setminus \{y\}$ is open and contains $x$, so it is a neighbourhood of $x$, and it contains no $N_k$. So no countable family works, and $X$ is not first countable. On $\mathbb{N}$, by contrast, the cofinite topology is second countable: the sets $\mathbb{N} \setminus F$ with $F$ finite form a countable basis.

**Example (Bases of the discrete topology).** In a discrete space the singletons $\{x\}$ form a basis, and each basis contains all of them: the open set $\{x\}$ must contain a basic set that contains $x$. So a discrete space is second countable if and only if it is countable.

### The library around it

The file `Mathlib.Topology.Bases` has, for a basis `hB : IsTopologicalBasis B`, the lemmas `hB.isOpen_iff`, `hB.mem_nhds_iff`, `hB.open_eq_sUnion`, `hB.continuous_iff` (continuity can be checked on preimages of basic sets) and `hB.secondCountableTopology`. The countability classes come with instances: a second countable space is first countable and separable, and a countable product or subspace of second countable spaces is second countable. The file `Mathlib.Topology.Order` builds the lattice of topologies, the generated topology, and the induced and coinduced topologies of Section 3.

Common errors of a learner:

- `IsTopologicalBasis B` is about the topology of the instance. A family that is a basis of another topology on the same type is not a basis for the instance.
- `SecondCountableTopology` asks for a countable generating family, not a countable basis. The two are equivalent, but a proof that unfolds the class gets the subbasis form.
- The ordering of topologies is reversed, so `generateFrom g` is the largest topology in which each set of `g` is open.

The reference table at the end of the section lists the declarations of the two modules.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that the closed intervals $[a, b]$ with $a < b$ do not form a basis of any topology on $\mathbb{R}$. Hint: intersect $[0, 1]$ and $[1, 2]$.
2. (A) Show that the open discs with rational centre and rational radius form a countable basis of the usual topology of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
3. (A) Let $\mathcal{B}$ be a basis of $X$. Show that $x \in \overline{A}$ if and only if each $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B$ meets $A$.
4. (B) Show that each open cover of a second countable space has a countable subcover (the theorem of Lindelöf). Hint: for each basic set that lies in some member of the cover, choose one such member.
5. (B) Show that in the Sorgenfrey line the set $[0, 1)$ is clopen, and that $(-\infty, 0)$ and $[0, \infty)$ separate $\mathbb{R}_\ell$.
6. (B) Show that a metric space (Chapter 3) is first countable, and that a separable metric space is second countable. Hint: balls of radius $1/n$ around the points of a countable dense set.
7. (C) Show that $\mathbb{R}_\ell \times \mathbb{R}_\ell$, with the product topology of Section 3, has a closed discrete subset that is uncountable. Hint: the antidiagonal $\{(x, -x)\}$.

### Notes and history

Hausdorff (1914) took the neighbourhoods as the primitive notion and stated the axioms for a system of neighbourhoods. The countability axioms are also his. The Sorgenfrey line is from R. H. Sorgenfrey (1947), who used the square of Exercise 7 to show that a product of two normal spaces need not be normal. Standard references for the section are Munkres, *Topology*, §13 and §30, and Bourbaki, *Topologie générale* I, §1.

In Mathlib, `generateFrom` and the lattice of topologies were written by Johannes Hölzl and Mario Carneiro in Lean 3, and `Mathlib/Topology/Bases.lean` by Johannes Hölzl and Mario Carneiro, with later work by Yury Kudryashov on the countability classes.
