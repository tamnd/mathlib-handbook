# Set theory

This volume teaches the set theory that Mathlib formalizes: cardinal numbers and their arithmetic, ordinal numbers and transfinite recursion, cofinality and the regular cardinals, and a model of Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory inside Lean. Volume 3 counted finite sets and showed that some infinite sets are countable and others are not. This volume measures all infinite sets. A cardinal compares the sizes of sets, an ordinal compares the lengths of well-orders, and the two scales meet in the alephs and in the cofinality of an ordinal.

In Mathlib, a cardinal is a type up to bijection, and an ordinal is a well-ordered type up to order isomorphism. Both are quotients of a universe of types, so both have universe parameters: `Cardinal.{u}` is the type of the cardinals of the types in `Type u`, and `Cardinal.lift` moves a cardinal to a larger universe. The cardinal of a type `α` is `#α`, the least infinite cardinal is `ℵ₀`, the least infinite ordinal is `ω`, and `Cardinal.ord` and `Ordinal.card` pass between the two scales. Chapters 1 to 6 work with these types. Chapter 7 builds the sets of a textbook of set theory, with one global membership relation, and compares the two foundations.

The volume has seven chapters.

1. **Cardinal numbers.** Cardinals as types up to equivalence, their sum, product and power, the order by embeddings, the theorem of Cantor, Schröder and Bernstein and the comparability of cardinals. Cantor's theorem $\kappa < 2^\kappa$ and the inequality of König. The cardinal $\aleph_0$ and countable cardinals, and the conversions between cardinals, natural numbers and extended natural numbers.
2. **Well-orders and ordinals.** Well-orders, initial segments and order types. The ordinals as a well-ordered type, the paradox of Burali-Forti in the form of universes, suprema of families of ordinals, transfinite induction and recursion, and the rank of a well-founded relation.
3. **Ordinal arithmetic.** Successor and limit ordinals, addition, multiplication, exponentiation, subtraction, division and logarithm, the Cantor normal form and the ordinal notations below $\varepsilon_0$.
4. **Normal functions and fixed points.** Normal functions, their fixed points and derivatives, the enumeration of closed unbounded classes, the order topology on the ordinals, principal ordinals, the epsilon numbers and the Veblen hierarchy.
5. **Alephs and infinite cardinal arithmetic.** Initial ordinals, the alephs and the beths, the theorem $\kappa \cdot \kappa = \kappa$ for infinite $\kappa$ and its consequences, the continuum $2^{\aleph_0}$, and the cardinalities of the structures of other volumes, such as free modules, fields generated by a set and the algebraic numbers.
6. **Cofinality and regular cardinals.** The cofinality of an order and of an ordinal, regular, singular and inaccessible cardinals and the infinite pigeonhole principle.
7. **ZFC inside Lean.** Pre-sets and ZFC sets, the axioms of ZFC in the type `ZFSet`, classes, von Neumann ordinals, rank and the von Neumann hierarchy, and the relation between the type theory of Lean and ZFC.

Chapter 1 is the base of the volume. Chapter 2 needs only Volume 4 and the definition of cardinals in Chapter 1. Chapter 3 needs Chapter 2, and Chapter 4 needs Chapter 3. Chapter 5 needs Chapters 1 to 4, and Chapter 6 needs Chapter 5. Chapter 7 needs Chapters 2, 5 and 6 at a few places. A reader who wants only cardinal arithmetic can read Chapter 1, the first section of Chapter 2 and Chapter 5.

The reader needs Volume 1 for the notation of types, sets, functions, quotients and the axiom of choice, Volume 3 for finite and countable types, and Volume 4 for orders, well-founded relations, Zorn's lemma and the theorem of Knaster and Tarski, which proves the theorem of Cantor, Schröder and Bernstein. The volume does not prove these results again. Each section gives the definitions in mathematical form, proves the theorems, computes examples, and then shows the form of the definitions and theorems in Mathlib. The volume uses the axiom of choice freely, as Mathlib does, and says so where a proof needs it.

The volume treats only what the index of this edition contains. The index of this edition has no declaration for combinatorial games, nimbers or surreal numbers, and no declaration for forcing, for the constructible universe or for the independence of the continuum hypothesis. Chapter 5 states the continuum hypothesis, and Chapter 7 says what is known about it outside Mathlib.

The standard references for the subject are the books of Jech, *Set Theory*, and Kunen, *Set Theory*.
