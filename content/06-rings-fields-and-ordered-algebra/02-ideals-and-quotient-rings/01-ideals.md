## Ideals

### Overview

The normal subgroups of a group are the kernels of homomorphisms, and they are the subgroups by which a quotient group exists. For rings the same part is played by ideals. An ideal is an additive subgroup that absorbs products: if $a$ is in the ideal, then $r a$ is in the ideal for each $r$ in the ring. The even integers form an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$: an even number times any integer is even. They are the kernel of the reduction $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2$.

This section defines left and two-sided ideals, the ideal generated by a set, and the sum, the intersection and the product of ideals. It proves that the kernel of a ring homomorphism is an ideal, that each ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ has the form $n \mathbb{Z}$, that an ideal with a unit is the whole ring, and that a commutative ring is a field if and only if it has exactly two ideals. The next section builds the quotient ring $R/I$ from an ideal $I$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Ideal).** Let $R$ be a ring. A left ideal of $R$ is an additive subgroup $I \subseteq R$ such that $r a \in I$ for all $r \in R$ and $a \in I$. A right ideal is an additive subgroup with $a r \in I$ for all $r \in R$ and $a \in I$. A two-sided ideal is a left ideal that is also a right ideal.

In a commutative ring the three notions agree. For a ring that is not commutative, "ideal" means a two-sided ideal in this chapter. Each ring $R$ has the zero ideal $0 = \{0\}$ and the unit ideal $R$. An ideal $I \ne R$ is proper.

**Definition (Generated ideal).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $s \subseteq R$. The ideal generated by $s$, written $(s)$, is the intersection of all ideals of $R$ that contain $s$. For a finite set the notation is $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$. An ideal of the form $(a)$ is principal.

An intersection of ideals is an ideal: each condition of the definition holds in each ideal of the family, so it holds in the intersection. So $(s)$ is the smallest ideal that contains $s$.

**Definition (Sum, intersection and product).** Let $I$ and $J$ be ideals of $R$. Their sum is $I + J = \{ a + b : a \in I, b \in J \}$. Their intersection is $I \cap J$. Their product $I J$ is the set of all finite sums $a_1 b_1 + \dots + a_n b_n$ with $a_k \in I$ and $b_k \in J$. The ideals $I$ and $J$ are coprime if $I + J = R$.

The product $I J$ is not the set of the products $a b$. That set is often not closed under the sum (Exercise 6).

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Ideal test).** A subset $I$ of a ring $R$ is a left ideal if and only if $0 \in I$, $a - b \in I$ for all $a, b \in I$, and $r a \in I$ for all $r \in R$ and $a \in I$.

*Proof.* The first two conditions say that $I$ is an additive subgroup (Volume 5). The third is the absorption rule. $\square$

**Theorem (Units and the unit ideal).** Let $I$ be a left ideal of $R$. If $I$ contains a unit $u$, then $I = R$. In particular $I = R$ if and only if $1 \in I$.

*Proof.* Let $r \in R$. Then $r = (r u^{-1}) u \in I$, since $u \in I$ and $I$ absorbs products from the left. If $1 \in I$, then $I = R$ since $1$ is a unit. Conversely $1 \in R$. $\square$

So a proper ideal contains no unit.

**Theorem (Kernels are ideals).** Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism. Then $\ker f = \{ a \in R : f(a) = 0 \}$ is a two-sided ideal of $R$, and $f$ is injective if and only if $\ker f = 0$.

*Proof.* The kernel is the kernel of the additive group homomorphism $f$, so it is an additive subgroup. For $a \in \ker f$ and $r \in R$, $f(r a) = f(r) f(a) = f(r) \cdot 0 = 0$ and $f(a r) = f(a) f(r) = 0$. So $r a, a r \in \ker f$. The statement on injectivity is the one for group homomorphisms: $f(a) = f(b)$ if and only if $a - b \in \ker f$. $\square$

For a nonzero ring $S$ the kernel does not contain $1$, so it is a proper ideal and not a subring.

**Theorem (Elements of a generated ideal).** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $s \subseteq R$. Then $(s)$ is the set $T$ of all finite sums $r_1 x_1 + \dots + r_n x_n$ with $n \ge 0$, $r_k \in R$ and $x_k \in s$. In particular $(a) = R a = \{ r a : r \in R \}$ and $(a, b) = \{ r a + t b : r, t \in R \}$.

*Proof.* Each ideal that contains $s$ contains each $r_k x_k$, by absorption, and so each finite sum of them. So $T \subseteq (s)$. Conversely $T$ is an ideal: it contains $0$ (the empty sum), the difference of two such sums is again such a sum, and $r \sum r_k x_k = \sum (r r_k) x_k$. It contains each $x \in s$ as $1 \cdot x$. So $(s) \subseteq T$. $\square$

Hence $b \in (a)$ if and only if $a \mid b$, and $(b) \subseteq (a)$ if and only if $a \mid b$.

**Theorem (Sum, intersection, product).** Let $I$ and $J$ be ideals of a commutative ring $R$.

1. $I + J$, $I \cap J$ and $I J$ are ideals.
2. $I + J$ is the smallest ideal that contains $I$ and $J$, and $I \cap J$ is the largest ideal contained in both.
3. $I J \subseteq I \cap J$.
4. If $I + J = R$, then $I J = I \cap J$.

*Proof.* (1) For the sum: $0 = 0 + 0$, $(a + b) - (a' + b') = (a - a') + (b - b')$ and $r (a + b) = r a + r b$, with $a, a' \in I$ and $b, b' \in J$. For the intersection, see the definitions. For the product: the difference of two finite sums $\sum a_k b_k$ is again such a sum, since $-(a b) = (-a) b$ with $-a \in I$, and $r \sum a_k b_k = \sum (r a_k) b_k$ with $r a_k \in I$.

(2) An ideal that contains $I$ and $J$ contains each $a + b$. And $I + J$ contains $I$ (take $b = 0$) and $J$. The claim on $I \cap J$ holds because an ideal inside $I$ and inside $J$ is inside $I \cap J$.

(3) For $a \in I$ and $b \in J$, $a b \in I$ since $I$ absorbs, and $a b \in J$ since $J$ absorbs. So $a b \in I \cap J$, and $I \cap J$ contains the sums of such products.

(4) By (3) it remains to show $I \cap J \subseteq I J$. Write $1 = x + y$ with $x \in I$ and $y \in J$. For $a \in I \cap J$,
$$a = a \cdot 1 = x a + a y.$$
Here $x a \in I J$ since $x \in I$ and $a \in J$, and $a y \in I J$ since $a \in I$ and $y \in J$. So $a \in I J$. $\square$

**Theorem (Ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$).** Each ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n \mathbb{Z} = (n)$ for exactly one integer $n \ge 0$.

*Proof.* Let $I$ be an ideal. If $I = 0$, then $I = (0)$. Otherwise $I$ contains a nonzero $a$, and also $-a$, so it contains a positive integer. Let $n$ be the smallest positive integer in $I$. Then $(n) \subseteq I$. Let $a \in I$. Division with remainder gives $a = q n + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. Then $r = a - q n \in I$. Since $n$ is the smallest positive element of $I$, $r = 0$, so $a = q n \in (n)$. Hence $I = (n)$. For uniqueness: if $(n) = (m)$ with $n, m \ge 0$, then $n \mid m$ and $m \mid n$, so $n = m$. $\square$

The same proof works in each ring with a division with remainder, the Euclidean domains of Chapter 6.

**Theorem (Fields and ideals).** A commutative ring $R$ is a field if and only if it has exactly two ideals, $0$ and $R$.

*Proof.* Let $R$ be a field. Then $0 \ne 1$, so $0 \ne R$ and there are at least two ideals. Let $I \ne 0$ be an ideal and $a \in I$ with $a \ne 0$. Then $a$ is a unit, so $I = R$ by the theorem on units.

Conversely, let $0$ and $R$ be the only ideals, with $0 \ne R$. Then $1 \ne 0$. Let $a \ne 0$. The ideal $(a)$ contains $a$, so $(a) \ne 0$, and so $(a) = R$. Then $1 \in (a) = R a$, so $1 = b a$ for some $b$. Hence $a$ is a unit. So each nonzero element is a unit, and $R$ is a field. $\square$

**Theorem (Preimages and images).** Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism of commutative rings. If $J$ is an ideal of $S$, then $f^{-1}(J)$ is an ideal of $R$ that contains $\ker f$. If $f$ is surjective and $I$ is an ideal of $R$, then $f(I)$ is an ideal of $S$.

*Proof.* The preimage is an additive subgroup. For $a \in f^{-1}(J)$ and $r \in R$, $f(r a) = f(r) f(a) \in J$. And $\ker f = f^{-1}(0) \subseteq f^{-1}(J)$. The image $f(I)$ is an additive subgroup. For $s \in S$ and $a \in I$, write $s = f(r)$ by surjectivity. Then $s f(a) = f(r a) \in f(I)$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Operations in $\mathbb{Z}$).** Take $I = (4)$ and $J = (6)$. The sum $(4) + (6)$ contains $6 - 4 = 2$, and each of its elements $4 r + 6 t$ is even, so $(4) + (6) = (2)$. The intersection consists of the common multiples of $4$ and $6$, so $(4) \cap (6) = (12)$. The product consists of the sums of products $4 r \cdot 6 t$, so $(4)(6) = (24)$. In general $(m) + (n) = (\gcd(m, n))$, $(m) \cap (n) = (\operatorname{lcm}(m, n))$ and $(m)(n) = (m n)$. The ideals $(4)$ and $(9)$ are coprime, since $1 = 9 - 2 \cdot 4$, and then $(4)(9) = (36) = (4) \cap (9)$, as part (4) of the theorem says.

**Example (The ideals of $\mathbb{Z}/12$).** Let $\pi : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/12$ be the reduction. For an ideal $I$ of $\mathbb{Z}/12$, the preimage $\pi^{-1}(I)$ is an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ that contains $\ker \pi = (12)$. So $\pi^{-1}(I) = (d)$ with $d \ge 1$ and $d \mid 12$. Since $\pi$ is surjective, $I = \pi(\pi^{-1}(I)) = ([d])$. So $\mathbb{Z}/12$ has exactly six ideals, one for each divisor $d \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\}$. For example $([3]) = \{[0], [3], [6], [9]\}$ and $([8]) = ([4]) = \{[0], [4], [8]\}$, since $\gcd(8, 12) = 4$.

**Example (An ideal of the Gaussian integers).** Let $I = (2 + i)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Its elements are $(2 a - b) + (a + 2 b) i$, and it contains $(2 + i)(2 - i) = 5$. It does not contain $1$: the element $(2 a - b) + (a + 2 b) i$ equals $1$ only if $2 a - b = 1$ and $a + 2 b = 0$, so $a = -2 b$ and $-5 b = 1$, which has no integer solution. So $I$ is a proper ideal. Since $i \equiv -2$ modulo $I$, each $a + b i$ is congruent to the integer $a - 2 b$. Section 2 uses this to show $\mathbb{Z}[i]/(2 + i) \cong \mathbb{Z}/5$.

**Counterexample (A left ideal that is not two-sided).** In $M_2(\mathbb{R})$, let $L$ be the set of the matrices with second column zero. If $A \in L$, then the second column of $B A$ is $B$ times the second column of $A$, which is zero. So $L$ is a left ideal. It is not a right ideal: $E_{11} \in L$ but $E_{11} E_{12} = E_{12} \notin L$. The ring $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ has no two-sided ideals other than $0$ and itself (Exercise 9), and it is not a division ring. So the theorem on fields needs the commutativity.

**Counterexample (An image that is not an ideal).** The inclusion $f : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is a ring homomorphism, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is an ideal of itself. But $f(\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ is not an ideal of $\mathbb{Q}$, since $\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \notin \mathbb{Z}$. The theorem on images needs the surjectivity.

### The library around it

The ideals of Mathlib are the submodules of `R` as a module over itself, so the lattice structure, the span, the map and the comap come from linear algebra (Volume 7). The theory of ideals and the theory of modules share one set of lemmas. The library on ideals is in the folder `Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal`, and Volume 9 treats it in full.

Common errors of a learner:

- `Ideal.span {a}` takes a set. Write `Ideal.span {a}`, not `Ideal.span a`. For two elements write `Ideal.span {a, b}`.
- `I + J` and `I ⊔ J` are the same ideal, but they are different terms. A lemma stated with `⊔` does not rewrite a goal stated with `+`. Use `Ideal.add_eq_sup` first.
- The product `I * J` is a span, so a membership goal `x ∈ I * J` for a sum needs `Ideal.add_mem` and `Ideal.mul_mem_mul`, not a single witness pair.
- The zero ideal is `⊥`, not the subset `{0}`; use `Ideal.mem_bot` for membership.

### Exercises

1. (A) List the elements of each ideal of $\mathbb{Z}/8$, and draw the inclusions between them.
2. (A) Compute $(12) + (18)$, $(12) \cap (18)$ and $(12)(18)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, each as $(n)$ with $n \ge 0$.
3. (A) Show that the set of the real functions $f$ on $[0, 1]$ with $f(1/2) = 0$ is an ideal of the ring of all functions $[0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$, and that it is the kernel of a homomorphism.
4. (A) Show that $(2, X)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is the set of the polynomials with even constant term, and that it is a proper ideal.
5. (B) Let $I \subseteq J$ be ideals of $R$ and $f : R \to S$ a surjective homomorphism. Show $f(I) \subseteq f(J)$, and show $f^{-1}(f(I)) = I + \ker f$.
6. (B) In $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]$ let $I = J = (2, X)$. Show that $4 + X^2 \in I J$ but that $4 + X^2$ is not a product $a b$ with $a, b \in I$. Hint: if $a b = 4 + X^2$, compare degrees and constant terms; an element of $I$ of degree $0$ is even.
7. (B) Let $R$ be commutative and $I, J, K$ ideals. Show $I (J + K) = I J + I K$. Then show that in $\mathbb{Z}$ the equality $I \cap (J + K) = (I \cap J) + (I \cap K)$ always holds. Hint: write the generators with their prime factorizations; $\gcd$ takes the minimum of the exponents and $\operatorname{lcm}$ the maximum.
8. (B) An element $a$ of a commutative ring is nilpotent if $a^n = 0$ for some $n \ge 1$. Show that the nilpotent elements form an ideal. Hint: for $a^m = 0$ and $b^n = 0$, expand $(a + b)^{m + n - 1}$ with the binomial theorem.
9. (C) Show that the only two-sided ideals of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ are $0$ and $M_2(\mathbb{R})$. Hint: if $A = (a_{jk}) \ne 0$ with $a_{pq} \ne 0$, then $E_{jp} A E_{qk} = a_{pq} E_{jk}$.

### Notes and history

Kummer introduced "ideal numbers" in the 1840s to save unique factorization in the rings $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_p]$, where it fails for elements. Dedekind replaced the ideal numbers by sets of actual numbers, closed under sums and under multiplication by ring elements, in his supplements to Dirichlet's lectures on number theory (1871). The definition for an arbitrary commutative ring is due to Emmy Noether and her school in the 1920s, and this section follows van der Waerden's *Moderne Algebra* (1930).

In Mathlib, `Ideal R` was defined as `Submodule R R` in the first algebra library of Lean 3, written by Kenny Lau, Chris Hughes and Mario Carneiro in 2018. The class `Ideal.IsTwoSided` came later, for quotients of noncommutative rings.
