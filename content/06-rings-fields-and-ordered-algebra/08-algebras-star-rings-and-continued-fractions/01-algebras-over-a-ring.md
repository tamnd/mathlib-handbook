## Algebras over a ring

### Overview

Many rings come with a ring of scalars. The complex numbers contain the real numbers, the matrices $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ contain the scalar matrices $r I$, and the polynomials $\mathbb{R}[X]$ contain the constants. In each case the scalars commute with every element, and multiplication by a scalar is both a ring operation and a module operation. An algebra over a commutative ring $R$ is a ring $A$ with this extra structure: a ring homomorphism $R \to A$ whose image lies in the center of $A$. This section defines algebras and their homomorphisms, proves that the two usual definitions agree, and gives the main examples. It proves the universal property of the polynomial ring as an algebra, which gives evaluation as an algebra homomorphism, and it describes the subalgebra generated by one element. The last part explains why Mathlib makes `Algebra` a class that holds both the map and the scalar multiplication.

### Definitions

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring and $A$, $B$ are rings, not necessarily commutative. All of this works for semirings too, and Mathlib states it for semirings.

**Definition (Algebra).** An $R$-algebra is a ring $A$ with a ring homomorphism $\varphi : R \to A$ such that $\varphi(r) x = x \varphi(r)$ for all $r \in R$ and $x \in A$. The map $\varphi$ is the structure map. The scalar multiplication of the algebra is $r \cdot x = \varphi(r) x$.

**Definition (Algebra homomorphism).** Let $A$ and $B$ be $R$-algebras with structure maps $\varphi_A$ and $\varphi_B$. An $R$-algebra homomorphism is a ring homomorphism $f : A \to B$ with $f(\varphi_A(r)) = \varphi_B(r)$ for all $r \in R$. It is an isomorphism of $R$-algebras if it is bijective.

**Definition (Subalgebra).** A subalgebra of an $R$-algebra $A$ is a subring $S \subseteq A$ that contains $\varphi(r)$ for every $r \in R$. For a subset $T \subseteq A$, the subalgebra generated by $T$, written $R[T]$, is the intersection of all subalgebras that contain $T$.

The intersection of subalgebras is a subalgebra, so $R[T]$ is the smallest subalgebra that contains $T$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Algebras as modules).** Let $A$ be a ring and an $R$-module. Suppose that
$$(r \cdot x) y = r \cdot (x y) = x (r \cdot y) \quad \text{for all } r \in R, \ x, y \in A.$$
Then $\varphi(r) = r \cdot 1$ is a ring homomorphism with central image, and $r \cdot x = \varphi(r) x$. Conversely each $R$-algebra is such a module, with $r \cdot x = \varphi(r) x$.

*Proof.* Assume the module conditions. The map $\varphi$ is additive, since $(r + s) \cdot 1 = r \cdot 1 + s \cdot 1$, and $\varphi(1) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$. For the product,
$$\varphi(r) \varphi(s) = (r \cdot 1)(s \cdot 1) = r \cdot (1 (s \cdot 1)) = r \cdot (s \cdot 1) = (r s) \cdot 1 = \varphi(r s).$$
The second step is the first condition with $x = 1$. Next, for $x \in A$,
$$\varphi(r) x = (r \cdot 1) x = r \cdot (1 x) = r \cdot x, \qquad x \varphi(r) = x (r \cdot 1) = r \cdot (x 1) = r \cdot x.$$
So $\varphi(r) x = r \cdot x = x \varphi(r)$. This shows that the image is central and that the scalar multiplication is $\varphi(r) x$.

Conversely, let $\varphi : R \to A$ be a ring homomorphism with central image, and set $r \cdot x = \varphi(r) x$. The module axioms follow from the ring axioms of $A$ and the fact that $\varphi$ is a ring homomorphism. For example $(r s) \cdot x = \varphi(r) \varphi(s) x = r \cdot (s \cdot x)$. The conditions hold: $(r \cdot x) y = \varphi(r) x y = r \cdot (x y)$, and $x (r \cdot y) = x \varphi(r) y = \varphi(r) x y = r \cdot (x y)$, where the middle step uses that $\varphi(r)$ is central. $\square$

**Proposition (Algebra homomorphisms are linear).** An $R$-algebra homomorphism $f : A \to B$ satisfies $f(r \cdot x) = r \cdot f(x)$.

*Proof.* $f(r \cdot x) = f(\varphi_A(r) x) = f(\varphi_A(r)) f(x) = \varphi_B(r) f(x) = r \cdot f(x)$. $\square$

Conversely a ring homomorphism that is $R$-linear is an algebra homomorphism: apply linearity to $x = 1$.

**Theorem (Every ring is a $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra).** Each ring $A$ has exactly one $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra structure. Each ring homomorphism between rings is a $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra homomorphism.

*Proof.* A $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra structure is a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to A$ with central image. There is exactly one ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to A$: it sends $n$ to $n \cdot 1_A$, by Chapter 1. Its image is central, since $(n \cdot 1) x = n \cdot x = x (n \cdot 1)$ by distributivity. A ring homomorphism $f : A \to B$ satisfies $f(n \cdot 1_A) = n \cdot 1_B$, which is the condition. $\square$

**Theorem (Division rings of characteristic zero are $\mathbb{Q}$-algebras).** Let $D$ be a division ring of characteristic zero. There is exactly one $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra structure on $D$.

*Proof.* The integer $n \ne 0$ maps to $n \cdot 1 \ne 0$, which is invertible in $D$. Define $\varphi(m/n) = (m \cdot 1)(n \cdot 1)^{-1}$. The proof that this is a well-defined ring homomorphism is the same as in Chapter 7, where it was given for fields. Its image is central: $n \cdot 1$ commutes with each $x$, so its inverse does too, since $x (n \cdot 1)^{-1} = (n \cdot 1)^{-1} (n \cdot 1) x (n \cdot 1)^{-1} = (n \cdot 1)^{-1} x$. Uniqueness: a ring homomorphism must send $n$ to $n \cdot 1$ and so $1/n$ to its inverse. $\square$

**Theorem (Universal property of $R[X]$).** Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra and $a \in A$. There is exactly one $R$-algebra homomorphism $\operatorname{ev}_a : R[X] \to A$ with $\operatorname{ev}_a(X) = a$. It is
$$\operatorname{ev}_a\Big(\sum_i c_i X^i\Big) = \sum_i \varphi(c_i) a^i.$$

*Proof.* The formula is additive and sends $1$ to $1$ and each constant $c$ to $\varphi(c)$. For products, let $p = \sum_i c_i X^i$ and $q = \sum_j d_j X^j$. Then $p q = \sum_k \big(\sum_{i + j = k} c_i d_j\big) X^k$, so
$$\operatorname{ev}_a(p q) = \sum_k \sum_{i + j = k} \varphi(c_i) \varphi(d_j) a^{i + j}.$$
On the other side,
$$\operatorname{ev}_a(p) \operatorname{ev}_a(q) = \sum_{i, j} \varphi(c_i) a^i \varphi(d_j) a^j = \sum_{i, j} \varphi(c_i) \varphi(d_j) a^{i + j}.$$
The second step moves $\varphi(d_j)$ to the left of $a^i$. This is allowed because $\varphi(d_j)$ is central. The two sums agree. So $\operatorname{ev}_a$ is an algebra homomorphism with $\operatorname{ev}_a(X) = a$.

For uniqueness, let $f$ be an algebra homomorphism with $f(X) = a$. Then $f(c) = \varphi(c)$ for constants, $f(X^i) = a^i$, and $f(\sum c_i X^i) = \sum \varphi(c_i) a^i$ by additivity and multiplicativity. $\square$

The centrality of the image of $\varphi$ is the reason for the definition. Without it, the map $p \mapsto p(a)$ is not multiplicative. The element $a$ need not commute with other elements of $A$: only the scalars must be central.

**Proposition (The subalgebra generated by one element).** Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra and $a \in A$. Then
$$R[a] = \{ p(a) : p \in R[X] \} = \operatorname{range}(\operatorname{ev}_a).$$

*Proof.* The range of a ring homomorphism is a subring. It contains $\varphi(r) = \operatorname{ev}_a(r)$ and $a = \operatorname{ev}_a(X)$. So it is a subalgebra that contains $a$, and $R[a] \subseteq \operatorname{range}(\operatorname{ev}_a)$. Conversely, each subalgebra $S$ that contains $a$ contains $\varphi(c_i)$ and $a^i$, and so each $\sum \varphi(c_i) a^i$. So the range lies in $S$, and in particular in $R[a]$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Standard algebras).** (1) The field $\mathbb{C}$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra by the inclusion $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$. Since $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[i]$, the subalgebra generated by $i$ is all of $\mathbb{C}$. (2) The ring $M_n(R)$ is an $R$-algebra by $r \mapsto r I$. A scalar matrix commutes with each matrix, since $(r I) M = r M = M (r I)$. In Mathlib this is the instance `Matrix.instAlgebra` of Volume 7. (3) The ring $R[X]$ is an $R$-algebra by the constant polynomials. (4) A product $A \times B$ of $R$-algebras is an $R$-algebra by $r \mapsto (\varphi_A(r), \varphi_B(r))$. (5) The quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ are an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra by $r \mapsto r + 0 i + 0 j + 0 k$. A real quaternion commutes with $i$, $j$ and $k$, so the image is central.

**Example (The subalgebra generated by a matrix).** Let
$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in M_2(\mathbb{Q}).$$
A computation gives $J^2 = -I$. So $J^3 = -J$ and $J^4 = I$, and each $p(J)$ has the form $a I + b J$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$. By the proposition, $\mathbb{Q}[J] = \{ a I + b J \}$. The product of two such elements is
$$(a I + b J)(c I + d J) = (a c - b d) I + (a d + b c) J.$$
This is the rule for $(a + b i)(c + d i)$. So $a + b i \mapsto a I + b J$ is an isomorphism of $\mathbb{Q}$-algebras $\mathbb{Q}(i) \to \mathbb{Q}[J]$. The kernel of $\operatorname{ev}_J : \mathbb{Q}[X] \to M_2(\mathbb{Q})$ is the ideal $(X^2 + 1)$. For instance $\operatorname{ev}_J(X^3 + 2X^2 + X) = -J - 2 I + J = -2 I$.

**Counterexample (The quaternions are not a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra).** The map $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{H}$, $a + b i \mapsto a + b i$, is an injective ring homomorphism. Its image is not central: $i j = k$ and $j i = -k$. So it does not make $\mathbb{H}$ a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra in the sense of this section. Left multiplication still makes $\mathbb{H}$ a $\mathbb{C}$-vector space of dimension $2$, but the condition $x (r \cdot y) = r \cdot (x y)$ fails for $x = j$, $r = i$, $y = 1$: the left side is $j i = -k$ and the right side is $i j = k$. The same failure breaks evaluation. In $\mathbb{C}[X]$ the polynomials $i X$ and $X i$ are equal, but substituting $j$ for $X$ gives $i j = k$ and $j i = -k$.

### The library around it

Mathlib makes `Algebra R A` a class that holds the scalar multiplication as data, together with the map. The reason is the problem of diamonds. Many rings already have a scalar multiplication: `ℝ` acts on `ℂ`, and `ℕ` and `ℤ` act on every ring by repeated addition. If `Algebra` held only the map and defined `•` from it, Lean would see two scalar multiplications on the same type, equal by a theorem but not by definition. Lemmas stated with one would not apply to the other. With `smul` as a field, each instance can reuse the existing `•` and prove `smul_def` once. The `ℤ`-algebra instance does this with the action of `ℤ` by repeated addition.

A second design choice is that `Algebra R A` takes a commutative `R` but a general `A`. This covers matrices and quaternions. For an algebra over a noncommutative ring, Mathlib uses a ring homomorphism and module classes directly. Towers of algebras $R \to S \to A$ use the class `IsScalarTower R S A`, which says that the two ways to act by $R$ agree. The class `Algebra.compHom` builds an $R$-algebra from an $S$-algebra and a homomorphism $R \to S$.

**Common errors of a learner.**

- Writing `algebraMap r` without the types. The map needs `algebraMap R A r`, since Lean cannot guess `R`.
- Expecting `RingHom.toAlgebra` to be found by instance search. It is a definition. Use `letI := f.toAlgebra` inside a proof.
- Thinking that the generator $a$ of $R[a]$ must commute with all of $A$. Only the scalars must be central. The subalgebra $R[a]$ is commutative in any case.
- Giving a ring a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra structure when its characteristic is not zero. In $\mathbb{Z}/p$ the element $p \cdot 1 = 0$ has no inverse, so no ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}/p$ exists.
- Confusing an algebra homomorphism with a ring homomorphism. The map $z \mapsto \bar z$ on $\mathbb{C}$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphism. It is not a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra homomorphism, since it moves $i \cdot 1$.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that the only $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphisms $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ are the identity and complex conjugation. Hint: $f(i)^2 = -1$.
2. (A) Show that a field $K$ that contains a field $F$ is an $F$-algebra, and that the subalgebra generated by $a \in K$ is $F[a]$.
3. (A) Show that $\mathbb{Z}/6$ has no $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra structure.
4. (B) Compute the subalgebra of $M_2(\mathbb{Q})$ generated by $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Show that it is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}[X]/(X^2)$.
5. (B) Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra. Show that the center of $A$ is a subalgebra.
6. (B) Show that each $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[X] \to \mathbb{R}$ is evaluation at a point, and that there are no $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphisms $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{R}$.
7. (B) Let $A = \mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2 - 1)$. Show that $A \cong \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ as $\mathbb{R}$-algebras. Hint: send $X$ to $(1, -1)$.
8. (C) Show that the $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra of $\mathbb{H}$ generated by $i$ and $j$ is all of $\mathbb{H}$, and that the subalgebra generated by $i$ alone is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}$. Show that $\mathbb{H}$ is not generated by one element.
9. (C) Show that the $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphisms $\mathbb{R}[X, Y] \to M_2(\mathbb{R})$ correspond to pairs of commuting matrices. Hint: imitate the proof of the universal property.

### Notes and history

The word "algebra" for a ring with a field of scalars comes from Benjamin Peirce's *Linear associative algebra* (1870), which classified small algebras over $\mathbb{C}$. Hamilton's quaternions (1843) and Cayley's matrices (1858) were the first examples. Wedderburn's structure theorem (1907) described finite-dimensional algebras over a field in terms of matrix rings over division algebras. The definition over a commutative ring, with a central structure map, became standard with the commutative algebra of Noether and her school in the 1920s. In Mathlib the class `Algebra` was in the library from its early days, and its design with `smul` as a field was set to avoid diamonds with the existing actions of `ℕ` and `ℤ`.
