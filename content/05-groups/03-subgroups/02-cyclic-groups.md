## Cyclic groups and images of subgroups

### Overview

The smallest subgroups are the ones that one element generates. A group that one element generates is cyclic. The cyclic groups are completely known: up to isomorphism they are $\mathbb{Z}$ and the groups $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Their subgroups are known too. Each subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$, and a cyclic group of order $n$ has exactly one subgroup of order $d$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$.

This section proves these facts. It also gives the image and the preimage of a subgroup along a homomorphism, which carry subgroups from one group to another. Chapter 4 uses cyclic subgroups to define the order of an element, and Chapter 8 uses them in the proof of Cauchy's theorem.

### Definitions

**Definition (Cyclic subgroup).** Let $G$ be a group and $a \in G$. The cyclic subgroup generated by $a$ is $\langle a \rangle = \{a^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$.

It is a subgroup, because $a^0 = 1$, $a^k a^l = a^{k + l}$ and $(a^k)^{-1} = a^{-k}$. It is the closure of $\{a\}$, by the theorem on words of the previous section.

**Definition (Cyclic group).** A group $G$ is cyclic if $G = \langle a \rangle$ for some $a \in G$. Such an $a$ is a generator of $G$.

**Definition (Image and preimage).** Let $f : G \to H$ be a homomorphism. For a subgroup $K \le G$, the image is $f(K) = \{f(x) : x \in K\}$. For a subgroup $L \le H$, the preimage is $f^{-1}(L) = \{x \in G : f(x) \in L\}$.

::: mathlib

<?decl Subgroup.map?>
:::

::: mathlib

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Image and preimage are subgroups).** In the definition above, $f(K)$ is a subgroup of $H$ and $f^{-1}(L)$ is a subgroup of $G$.

*Proof.* The image contains $f(1) = 1$. If $y = f(a)$ and $z = f(b)$ with $a, b \in K$, then $y z = f(a b)$ and $y^{-1} = f(a^{-1})$, with $a b, a^{-1} \in K$. The preimage contains $1$, since $f(1) = 1 \in L$. If $f(a), f(b) \in L$, then $f(a b) = f(a) f(b) \in L$ and $f(a^{-1}) = f(a)^{-1} \in L$. $\square$

**Theorem (Cyclic groups are commutative).** A cyclic group is commutative.

*Proof.* Let $G = \langle a \rangle$ and $x, y \in G$. Write $x = a^k$ and $y = a^l$. Then $x y = a^{k + l} = a^{l + k} = y x$. $\square$

**Theorem (Subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$).** Each subgroup $H$ of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for exactly one $n \ge 0$.

*Proof.* If $H = \{0\}$, then $H = 0\mathbb{Z}$. Otherwise $H$ has a nonzero element $x$, and also $-x$, so it has a positive element. Let $n$ be the least positive element of $H$. Then $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq H$, since $H$ is closed under sums and negatives. Let $x \in H$. Divide with remainder: $x = q n + r$ with $0 \le r < n$. Then $r = x - q n \in H$. By the choice of $n$, $r$ is not positive, so $r = 0$ and $x \in n\mathbb{Z}$. So $H = n\mathbb{Z}$. The number $n$ is unique, because $n$ is the least positive element of $n\mathbb{Z}$ when $n > 0$. $\square$

**Theorem (Structure of cyclic groups).** Let $G = \langle a \rangle$. If the powers $a^k$ are distinct, then $k \mapsto a^k$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to G$. Otherwise there is a least $n \ge 1$ with $a^n = 1$, the elements $1, a, \ldots, a^{n-1}$ are distinct, $G = \{1, a, \ldots, a^{n - 1}\}$, and $a^k = a^l$ if and only if $n \mid k - l$.

*Proof.* The map $\varphi(k) = a^k$ is a homomorphism $\mathbb{Z} \to G$, by the laws of exponents, and it is onto, since $G = \langle a \rangle$. Its kernel is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$, so it is $n\mathbb{Z}$ for some $n \ge 0$. Also $a^k = a^l$ if and only if $a^{k - l} = 1$, that is, $k - l \in n\mathbb{Z}$. If $n = 0$, then $\varphi$ is injective, so it is an isomorphism. If $n \ge 1$, then $n$ is the least positive element of the kernel, so it is the least $n \ge 1$ with $a^n = 1$. Two of the numbers $0, 1, \ldots, n - 1$ differ by less than $n$, so their powers are distinct. Each $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ is $q n + r$ with $0 \le r < n$, and $a^k = a^r$. So $G = \{1, a, \ldots, a^{n-1}\}$. $\square$

So a cyclic group with $n$ elements is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n$, by $a^k \mapsto k \bmod n$. This map is well defined and injective by the last claim, and it is a homomorphism by the laws of exponents.

**Theorem (Subgroups of a cyclic group).** Each subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. If $G = \langle a \rangle$ has $n$ elements, then for each divisor $d$ of $n$ there is exactly one subgroup of order $d$, namely $\langle a^{n/d} \rangle$.

*Proof.* Let $H \le G = \langle a \rangle$, and let $S = \{k \in \mathbb{Z} : a^k \in H\}$. This is the preimage of $H$ under $k \mapsto a^k$, so it is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$, and $S = m\mathbb{Z}$ for some $m \ge 0$. Each element of $H$ is $a^k$ with $k \in S$, so it is $a^{m j} = (a^m)^j$. So $H = \langle a^m \rangle$ is cyclic.

Now let $|G| = n$. Then $a^n = 1 \in H$, so $n \in S = m\mathbb{Z}$, and $m$ is a positive divisor of $n$. The elements $(a^m)^j$ for $0 \le j < n/m$ are distinct, because $a^{m j} = a^{m j'}$ needs $n \mid m(j - j')$, that is, $n/m \mid j - j'$. And $(a^m)^{n/m} = 1$. So $|H| = n/m$, by the theorem on structure applied to $\langle a^m \rangle$. A subgroup of order $d$ thus has $m = n/d$ and is $\langle a^{n/d} \rangle$. That subgroup has order $d$ by the same count, so it exists. $\square$

**Theorem (Induction on the closure).** Let $s \subseteq G$ and let $P$ be a property of elements of $G$. If $P$ holds for each element of $s$ and for $1$, and $P(x)$ and $P(y)$ give $P(x y)$ and $P(x^{-1})$, then $P$ holds on $\langle s \rangle$.

*Proof.* The set $\{x \in G : P(x)\}$ is a subgroup that contains $s$, by the hypotheses. So it contains $\langle s \rangle$, by the minimal property of the closure. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/12$).** The group $\mathbb{Z}/12$ is cyclic with generator $1$. Its divisors $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$ give six subgroups: $\{0\}$, $\langle 6 \rangle = \{0, 6\}$, $\langle 4 \rangle = \{0, 4, 8\}$, $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3, 6, 9\}$, $\langle 2 \rangle = \{0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$ and the whole group. The element $10$ generates $\langle 2 \rangle$, since $5 \cdot 10 = 50 = 2$. In general $\langle k \rangle = \langle \gcd(k, 12) \rangle$. The generators of $\mathbb{Z}/12$ are the $k$ with $\gcd(k, 12) = 1$: $1, 5, 7, 11$.

**Example (Roots of unity).** The set $\mu_n = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : z^n = 1\}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{C}^\times$ with the $n$ elements $e^{2 \pi i k / n}$, $0 \le k < n$. It is cyclic with generator $e^{2 \pi i / n}$. The subgroup of order $d$ for $d \mid n$ is $\mu_d$.

**Example (An image and a preimage).** Let $f : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6$ be the reduction. The image of $4\mathbb{Z}$ is $\{0, 4, 2\} = \langle 2 \rangle$. The preimage of $\langle 3 \rangle = \{0, 3\}$ is $3\mathbb{Z}$. So $f^{-1}(f(4\mathbb{Z})) = 2\mathbb{Z}$ is larger than $4\mathbb{Z}$: the preimage of the image adds the kernel $6\mathbb{Z}$. Chapter 5 makes this precise.

**Counterexample (Commutative but not cyclic).** The Klein four group $V = \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$ is commutative, and each of its proper subgroups is cyclic. It is not cyclic: each element $x$ has $2 x = 0$, so $\langle x \rangle$ has at most $2$ elements. It has three subgroups of order $2$, so the uniqueness part of the theorem fails for groups that are not cyclic. The group $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ is not cyclic either: if $\mathbb{Q} = \langle q \rangle$ with $q \ne 0$, then $q/2$ would be $k q$ for an integer $k$, so $k = 1/2$, which is false.

### The library around it

The finite cyclic groups have the lemmas `isCyclic_of_prime_card` and `orderOf_eq_card_of_forall_mem_zpowers` of Chapter 4, and `IsCyclic.card_orderOf_eq_totient`, which counts the elements of each order. The image of a cyclic group is cyclic, by `isCyclic_of_surjective`. The lemma `Int.zmultiples_natAbs` and the instance `IsAddCyclic ℤ` give the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}$; the older lemma `Int.subgroup_cyclic` is deprecated. For the image and the preimage, the lemmas `Subgroup.map_map`, `Subgroup.comap_comap` and `Subgroup.map_le_iff_le_comap` are the basic rewriting rules.

In a linearly ordered commutative group, the cyclic subgroups have a description by the order. The module `Mathlib.GroupTheory.Archimedean` proves that a subgroup $H$ of an archimedean group, with a least element $a$ among its elements $> 1$, is $\langle a \rangle$: this is `Subgroup.zpowers_eq_of_isLeast_mem_and_one_lt`. The proof is the division algorithm. For $h \in H$, the archimedean property gives $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $a^k \le h < a^{k+1}$. Then $1 \le a^{-k} h < a$ and $a^{-k} h \in H$, so the minimality of $a$ gives $a^{-k} h = 1$. The lemma `Subgroup.exists_isLeast_one_lt` gives such a least element when $H \ne \bot$ and $H$ misses an interval $(1, a)$. The module `Mathlib.GroupTheory.ArchimedeanDensely` then proves a dichotomy: an archimedean linearly ordered commutative group is either isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ as an ordered group or densely ordered. This is `LinearOrderedCommGroup.discrete_or_denselyOrdered`, with the additive form `LinearOrderedAddCommGroup.discrete_or_denselyOrdered`. For a nontrivial group, `LinearOrderedCommGroup.isCyclic_iff_not_denselyOrdered` says that the group is cyclic if and only if it is not densely ordered, and `Subgroup.isCyclic_of_disjoint_Ioo_one` says that a subgroup that misses an interval $(1, a)$ is cyclic. The same dichotomy holds for a linearly ordered group with zero, with `WithZero (Multiplicative ℤ)` in the place of $\mathbb{Z}$, by `LinearOrderedCommGroupWithZero.discrete_or_denselyOrdered`. The module also proves that $\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly two additive automorphisms, the identity and the negation, by `Int.addEquiv_eq_refl_or_neg`.

The module `Mathlib.GroupTheory.SpecificGroups.Cyclic` holds the deeper results on cyclic groups, which use the order of an element and the index of Chapter 4. A cyclic group of order $n$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/n$ by `zmodCyclicMulEquiv`, and an infinite one is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ by `intCyclicMulEquiv`; both use the multiplicative copies `Multiplicative (ZMod n)` and `Multiplicative ℤ`. Two cyclic groups of the same order are isomorphic by `mulEquivOfCyclicCardEq`, and two groups of the same prime order by `mulEquivOfPrimeCardEq`. The subgroups of a cyclic group are determined by their order or by their index: `IsCyclic.subgroup_eq_iff_card_eq`, `IsCyclic.subgroup_le_iff_card_dvd` and `IsCyclic.subgroup_le_iff_index_dvd`. For a generator $g$, the index of $\langle g^i \rangle$ is $\gcd(i, \operatorname{ord} g)$, by `Subgroup.index_zpowers_zpow`. A finite commutative group is cyclic if and only if its exponent equals its order (`IsCyclic.iff_exponent_eq_card`). A finite group with at most $n$ solutions of $x^n = 1$ for each $n$ is cyclic (`isCyclic_of_card_pow_eq_one_le`), the criterion that makes the unit group of a finite field cyclic. A product $M \times N$ is cyclic if and only if both factors are cyclic with coprime orders (`Group.isCyclic_prod_iff`). The automorphism group of a cyclic group of order $n$ is $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$ by `IsCyclic.mulAutMulEquiv`, so it has $\varphi(n)$ elements (`IsCyclic.card_mulAut`). A commutative group is simple if and only if its order is prime (`CommGroup.is_simple_iff_prime_card`). If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is commutative, by `isMulCommutative_of_isCyclic_quotient_center_self`. The proof: pick $g$ whose class generates $G/Z(G)$. Each element is $g^i z$ with $z$ central, and two such elements commute.

Common errors of a learner:

- `zpowers a` uses integer powers. In a finite group the natural powers are enough, but the definition and its lemmas use `ℤ`, so a goal can need `zpow_natCast` to change `a ^ (n : ℤ)` into `a ^ n`.
- `IsCyclic G` does not give a chosen generator. To name one, destructure `IsCyclic.exists_generator`.
- A cyclic group is commutative, but the type does not get a `CommGroup` instance from `IsCyclic`. Use `IsCyclic.commGroup` by hand.

### Exercises

1. (A) List the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/18$ and a generator of each.
2. (A) Show that $\langle a \rangle = \langle a^{-1} \rangle$.
3. (A) Show that $(\mathbb{Z}/7)^\times$ is cyclic, with generator $3$.
4. (B) Show that $\langle k \rangle = \langle \gcd(k, n) \rangle$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Hint: write $\gcd(k, n) = u k + v n$.
5. (B) Show that the image of a cyclic group under a homomorphism is cyclic, and that a group with a cyclic quotient need not be cyclic. Hint: for the second part, map $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$ onto $\mathbb{Z}/2$.
6. (B) Show that $m\mathbb{Z} \cap n\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{lcm}(m, n)\mathbb{Z}$ and $m\mathbb{Z} + n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m, n)\mathbb{Z}$.
7. (C) Show that $(\mathbb{Z}/8)^\times$ is not cyclic, and that a finite group with exactly one subgroup of each order $d \mid n$ is cyclic, when it is commutative. Hint: for the second claim, count the elements that generate each cyclic subgroup.

### Notes and history

The cyclic groups appear in the work of Gauss on the roots of unity and on the multiplicative group modulo a prime, which he showed is cyclic in the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae of 1801. The generators of that group are the primitive roots. The theorem that each subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$ is a form of the existence of the greatest common divisor, and the proof above is the proof of Euclid's algorithm.

In Mathlib the class `IsCyclic` lives in a basic file so that the instances for `ℤ` and `ZMod n` can be stated early. The theorems about the subgroups of a cyclic group are in `Mathlib.GroupTheory.SpecificGroups.Cyclic`, with the counting results that use Euler's function.
