## π-systems and Dynkin systems

### Overview

A σ-algebra generated by a family $\mathcal{C}$ has no explicit description: Section 1 defines it as an intersection. So a property of all members of $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is proved in two steps. First the property holds on $\mathcal{C}$. Then the family of the sets with the property is closed under the operations that generate $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$. The second step often fails for intersections. Two measures that agree on $A$ and on $B$ need not agree on $A \cap B$, but they agree on complements and on disjoint countable unions.

The π-λ theorem says that this is enough when $\mathcal{C}$ is closed under finite intersections. The families closed under complements and disjoint countable unions are the Dynkin systems. Chapter 2 uses it to prove that a finite measure is determined by its values on a π-system, and Chapter 6 uses it for the product measure.

### Definitions

**Definition (π-system).** A family $\mathcal{C}$ of subsets of $X$ is a *π-system* if $A \cap B \in \mathcal{C}$ for all $A, B \in \mathcal{C}$.

::: mathlib
`IsPiSystem` · `Mathlib.MeasureTheory.PiSystem`

`IsPiSystem C` says: for `s, t ∈ C`, if `(s ∩ t).Nonempty` then `s ∩ t ∈ C`. So Mathlib asks only for the nonempty intersections, and the family of the open intervals of `ℝ` is a π-system although the empty set is not an open interval. The empty family is a π-system too. The difference with the definition above is harmless, because each Dynkin system contains `∅`; `IsPiSystem.insert_empty` adds it.

<?decl IsPiSystem?>
:::

**Definition (Dynkin system).** A family $\mathcal{D}$ of subsets of $X$ is a *Dynkin system* (or *λ-system*) if $\emptyset \in \mathcal{D}$, $A^c \in \mathcal{D}$ for each $A \in \mathcal{D}$, and $\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{D}$ for each sequence $(A_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of pairwise disjoint members of $\mathcal{D}$.

Each σ-algebra is a Dynkin system. An intersection of Dynkin systems is a Dynkin system, by the argument of Section 1 for σ-algebras. So each family $\mathcal{C}$ generates a Dynkin system $\delta(\mathcal{C})$, the intersection of the Dynkin systems that contain $\mathcal{C}$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (differences).** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a Dynkin system. Then $X \in \mathcal{D}$, $\mathcal{D}$ is closed under disjoint finite unions, and $A \setminus B \in \mathcal{D}$ for $A, B \in \mathcal{D}$ with $B \subseteq A$.

*Proof.* $X = \emptyset^c$. A disjoint union $A \cup B$ is the union of the disjoint sequence $A, B, \emptyset, \emptyset, \dots$. For $B \subseteq A$, the sets $A^c$ and $B$ are disjoint, so $A^c \cup B \in \mathcal{D}$, and $A \setminus B = (A^c \cup B)^c \in \mathcal{D}$. $\square$

::: mathlib
`MeasurableSpace.DynkinSystem.has_sdiff` · `Mathlib.MeasureTheory.PiSystem`

**Proposition (a Dynkin π-system is a σ-algebra).** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a Dynkin system that is closed under finite intersections. Then $\mathcal{D}$ is a σ-algebra.

*Proof.* It suffices to show that $\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{D}$ for each sequence $(A_n)$ in $\mathcal{D}$. Put $B_0 = A_0$ and $B_n = A_n \cap A_0^c \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1}^c$ for $n \ge 1$. Each $B_n$ is in $\mathcal{D}$, as a finite intersection of members and of complements of members. The sets $B_n$ are pairwise disjoint: for $m < n$, $B_m \subseteq A_m$ and $B_n \subseteq A_m^c$. Each $x \in \bigcup_n A_n$ is in $B_n$ for the least $n$ with $x \in A_n$, so $\bigcup_n B_n = \bigcup_n A_n$. This union is in $\mathcal{D}$ because the $B_n$ are disjoint. $\square$

::: mathlib

**Lemma (restriction to a member).** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a Dynkin system and $A \in \mathcal{D}$. Then $\mathcal{D}_A = \{B \subseteq X : B \cap A \in \mathcal{D}\}$ is a Dynkin system.

*Proof.* $\emptyset \cap A = \emptyset \in \mathcal{D}$. If $B \cap A \in \mathcal{D}$, then $B^c \cap A = A \setminus (B \cap A)$, a difference of members of $\mathcal{D}$ with $B \cap A \subseteq A$, so it is in $\mathcal{D}$ by the lemma on differences. If $(B_n)$ are pairwise disjoint with $B_n \cap A \in \mathcal{D}$, then the sets $B_n \cap A$ are pairwise disjoint and $\big(\bigcup_n B_n\big) \cap A = \bigcup_n (B_n \cap A) \in \mathcal{D}$. $\square$

::: mathlib

**Theorem (π-λ theorem).** Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a π-system on $X$. Then $\delta(\mathcal{C}) = \sigma(\mathcal{C})$. So each Dynkin system that contains $\mathcal{C}$ contains $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$.

*Proof.* The σ-algebra $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is a Dynkin system that contains $\mathcal{C}$, so $\delta(\mathcal{C}) \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{C})$. For the converse inclusion it suffices to show that $\delta(\mathcal{C})$ is closed under finite intersections: then $\delta(\mathcal{C})$ is a σ-algebra by the proposition, it contains $\mathcal{C}$, and so it contains $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$. Write $\mathcal{D} = \delta(\mathcal{C})$ and use the systems $\mathcal{D}_A$ of the lemma.

Step 1. Let $A \in \mathcal{C}$. For each $B \in \mathcal{C}$, the set $B \cap A$ is in $\mathcal{C}$ if it is nonempty and equals $\emptyset$ otherwise, so it is in $\mathcal{D}$ in both cases. Thus $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{D}_A$. The family $\mathcal{D}_A$ is a Dynkin system, so $\mathcal{D} \subseteq \mathcal{D}_A$. That is, $B \cap A \in \mathcal{D}$ for all $B \in \mathcal{D}$ and $A \in \mathcal{C}$.

Step 2. Let $B \in \mathcal{D}$. By Step 1, $A \cap B \in \mathcal{D}$ for each $A \in \mathcal{C}$, so $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{D}_B$. The family $\mathcal{D}_B$ is a Dynkin system, so $\mathcal{D} \subseteq \mathcal{D}_B$. That is, $E \cap B \in \mathcal{D}$ for all $E, B \in \mathcal{D}$. $\square$

The proof applies the minimality of $\delta(\mathcal{C})$ twice, first with a set of $\mathcal{C}$ fixed, then with a set of $\mathcal{D}$ fixed.

**Corollary (induction on a π-system).** Let $\Sigma = \sigma(\mathcal{C})$ with $\mathcal{C}$ a π-system, and let $P$ be a property of the members of $\Sigma$. Suppose that $P(\emptyset)$ holds, $P(A)$ holds for each $A \in \mathcal{C}$, $P(A)$ implies $P(A^c)$, and $P(A_n)$ for all $n$ implies $P\big(\bigcup_n A_n\big)$ for each pairwise disjoint sequence in $\Sigma$. Then $P(A)$ holds for each $A \in \Sigma$.

*Proof.* The family $\{A \in \Sigma : P(A)\}$ is a Dynkin system that contains $\mathcal{C}$. By the theorem it contains $\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \Sigma$. $\square$

::: mathlib

### Examples

**Example (intervals).** In $\mathbb{R}$, the intersection of $(a, b]$ and $(c, d]$ is $(\max(a, c), \min(b, d)]$, which is empty or a half-open interval. So the half-open intervals form a π-system, and by Exercise 4 of Section 1 they generate $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$. In the same way the rays $(-\infty, a)$ form a π-system, since $(-\infty, a) \cap (-\infty, b) = (-\infty, \min(a, b))$, and Section 1 shows that the rays with rational end points generate $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$. Mathlib has these facts as `isPiSystem_Ioc`, `isPiSystem_Iio` and `Real.isPiSystem_Iio_rat`.

::: mathlib
`isPiSystem_Ioc` · `Mathlib.MeasureTheory.PiSystem`

For maps `f : ι → α` and `g : ι' → α` into a linear order, the family of the intervals `Ioc (f i) (g j)` with `f i < g j` is a π-system.

<?decl isPiSystem_Ioc?>
:::

**Counterexample (the π-system hypothesis is necessary).** On $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ let $\mathcal{C} = \{\{1, 2\}, \{2, 3\}\}$, which is not a π-system. The family $\{\emptyset, X, \{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}, \{2, 3\}, \{1, 4\}\}$ is a Dynkin system: it is closed under complements, and two disjoint nonempty members are complements of each other, with union $X$. Each Dynkin system that contains $\mathcal{C}$ contains these six sets, so they form $\delta(\mathcal{C})$. But $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ contains $\{2\} = \{1, 2\} \cap \{2, 3\}$, so $\delta(\mathcal{C}) \ne \sigma(\mathcal{C})$. Chapter 2 turns this into two measures that agree on $\mathcal{C}$ and differ on $\{2\}$: the weights $\tfrac14$ on each point, and the weights $\tfrac12$ on $1$ and on $3$.

### The library around it

Mathlib has three more classes of families of sets, used to build measures in Chapter 2. A *semiring of sets* contains $\emptyset$, is closed under $\cap$, and writes each difference $A \setminus B$ as a finite disjoint union of members. The half-open intervals form a semiring. A *ring of sets* is closed under $\cup$ and $\setminus$, and an *algebra of sets* is a ring that contains $X$.

::: mathlib
`MeasureTheory.IsSetSemiring` · `Mathlib.MeasureTheory.SetSemiring`

The fields are `empty_mem`, `inter_mem` and `sdiff_eq_sUnion'`, the last a finite set of pairwise disjoint members with union `s \ t`. The class `MeasureTheory.IsSetRing` is in the same file.

Common errors of a learner:

- Applying the π-λ theorem to a generating family that is not closed under intersections, such as the family of the open balls of radius 1 in a metric space. Use `generatePiSystem` first.
- Checking only finite disjoint unions in the `iUnion` case.
- Forgetting the `empty` case of `induction_on_inter`. Mathlib's `IsPiSystem` does not put `∅` in the family, so the case is not covered by `basic`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that the family of the singletons of $X$, together with $\emptyset$, is a π-system.
2. (A) Show that a Dynkin system that is closed under finite unions is a σ-algebra.
3. (A) Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a Dynkin system and $A, B \in \mathcal{D}$ with $A \cap B = \emptyset$. Show that $A \cup B \in \mathcal{D}$ and $(A \cup B)^c \in \mathcal{D}$.
4. (B) Find a π-system $\mathcal{C}$ on $\{1, 2, 3\}$ with $\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{P}(\{1, 2, 3\})$ and with as few members as possible. Hint: show that one member is not enough, and that two singletons and $\emptyset$ are enough.
5. (B) Show that the family of the sets $\prod_i (-\infty, a_i]$ with $a \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is a π-system that generates $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. Hint: write an open box with rational corners as a countable union of differences.
6. (B) Show that an intersection of Dynkin systems is a Dynkin system, but that a union of two Dynkin systems need not be one. Hint: use $\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2, 3\}, X\}$ and $\{\emptyset, \{2\}, \{1, 3\}, X\}$.
7. (C) A *monotone class* is a family closed under increasing countable unions and decreasing countable intersections. Show that the monotone class generated by an algebra of sets $\mathcal{A}$ is $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$. Hint: copy the two steps of the proof of the π-λ theorem with complements replaced by monotone limits.

### Notes and history

The theorem carries the name of Eugene Dynkin, who used it in probability theory. An earlier form of the theorem is due to Wacław Sierpiński. The Mathlib file `Mathlib.MeasureTheory.PiSystem` has the authors Johannes Hölzl, Martin Zinkevich and Rémy Degenne, and `Mathlib.MeasureTheory.SetSemiring` has the authors Rémy Degenne and Peter Pfaffelhuber.
