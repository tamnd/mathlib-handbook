## The Hilbert basis theorem

### Overview

Section 1 gave the first Noetherian rings: fields, principal ideal domains, and their quotients and localizations. The polynomial ring $k[X, Y]$ over a field is not a principal ideal domain, since the ideal $(X, Y)$ needs two generators. The Hilbert basis theorem proves that it is still Noetherian. More generally, if $R$ is Noetherian, then $R[X]$ is Noetherian. So every finitely generated algebra over a field or over $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.

The proof uses the chain of leading coefficient ideals of an ideal $I \subseteq R[X]$, which stops because $R$ is Noetherian.

### Definitions

In this section $R$ is a commutative ring and $R[X]$ is the polynomial ring of Volume 6, Chapter 5. For $f \in R[X]$ and $n \ge 0$, write $[X^n] f$ for the coefficient of $X^n$ in $f$.

**Definition (Leading coefficient ideals).** Let $I$ be an ideal of $R[X]$ and $n \ge 0$. The $n$-th leading coefficient ideal of $I$ is
$$L_n(I) = \{ [X^n] f : f \in I, \ \deg f \le n \}.$$

So $L_n(I)$ consists of $0$ and of the leading coefficients of the elements of $I$ of degree exactly $n$.

**Lemma (Leading coefficient ideals form a chain).** Each $L_n(I)$ is an ideal of $R$, and $L_n(I) \subseteq L_{n+1}(I)$.

*Proof.* The set $\{ f \in I : \deg f \le n \}$ is an $R$-submodule of $R[X]$: it contains $0$, and if $\deg f \le n$ and $\deg g \le n$, then $\deg(f + g) \le n$ and $\deg(rf) \le n$. The map $f \mapsto [X^n] f$ is $R$-linear. So $L_n(I)$ is the image of a submodule under a linear map, and it is an ideal of $R$. If $a = [X^n] f$ with $f \in I$ and $\deg f \le n$, then $Xf \in I$, $\deg(Xf) \le n + 1$ and $[X^{n+1}](Xf) = [X^n] f = a$. So $a \in L_{n+1}(I)$. $\square$

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Hilbert basis theorem).** If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then $R[X]$ is a Noetherian ring.

*Proof.* Let $I$ be an ideal of $R[X]$. Write $L_n$ for $L_n(I)$. The chain $L_0 \subseteq L_1 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals of $R$ stops, because $R$ is Noetherian: there is an $N$ with $L_n = L_N$ for all $n \ge N$.

For each $n \le N$, the ideal $L_n$ is finitely generated: $L_n = (a_{n,1}, \dots, a_{n,k_n})$. For each $a_{n,j}$ choose $f_{n,j} \in I$ with $\deg f_{n,j} \le n$ and $[X^n] f_{n,j} = a_{n,j}$. Let $J$ be the ideal of $R[X]$ generated by the finitely many $f_{n,j}$ with $n \le N$ and $1 \le j \le k_n$. Then $J \subseteq I$. The claim is $I \subseteq J$, so that $I = J$ is finitely generated.

The proof is by strong induction on the degree. Let $f \in I$. If $f = 0$, then $f \in J$. Otherwise let $d = \deg f$ and $a = [X^d] f$, the leading coefficient of $f$. Then $a \in L_d$. Let $n = \min(d, N)$. Then $L_d = L_n$: if $d \le N$ this is trivial, and if $d > N$ it is the choice of $N$. So $a = \sum_j r_j a_{n,j}$ for some $r_j \in R$. Let
$$g = \sum_{j=1}^{k_n} r_j X^{d - n} f_{n,j}.$$
Then $g \in J$. Each term has degree at most $(d - n) + n = d$, so $\deg g \le d$, and
$$[X^d] g = \sum_j r_j [X^n] f_{n,j} = \sum_j r_j a_{n,j} = a.$$
So $f - g \in I$, $\deg(f - g) \le d$, and its coefficient of $X^d$ is $a - a = 0$. Thus $f - g = 0$ or $\deg(f - g) < d$. In both cases $f - g \in J$ by the induction hypothesis. Hence $f = (f - g) + g \in J$. $\square$

**Corollary (Several variables).** If $R$ is Noetherian, then $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian for every $n$.

*Proof.* By induction on $n$. The case $n = 0$ is the ring $R$ itself. For $n \ge 1$ there is a ring isomorphism $R[X_1, \dots, X_n] \cong R[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}][X_n]$, which sends a polynomial to the same polynomial, collected by powers of $X_n$. The ring $R[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}]$ is Noetherian by the induction hypothesis, and the theorem gives the claim. $\square$

**Corollary (Finitely generated algebras).** Let $R$ be Noetherian and let $A$ be an $R$-algebra generated by finitely many elements $y_1, \dots, y_n$. Then $A$ is Noetherian.

*Proof.* The $R$-algebra homomorphism $R[X_1, \dots, X_n] \to A$ with $X_i \mapsto y_i$ is surjective, since its image is a subalgebra that contains the generators. Its source is Noetherian by the previous corollary. A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian (Section 1). $\square$

The converse of the theorem holds too: if $R[X]$ is Noetherian, then $R \cong R[X]/(X)$ is Noetherian as a quotient. So $R[X]$ is Noetherian exactly when $R$ is. Chapter 7 proves that $R[[X]]$ is Noetherian when $R$ is, with the lowest coefficient in place of the leading one.

### Examples

**Example (Computing the leading coefficient ideals).** Let $I = (X^2 + 1, 3X) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$. First, $3 = 3(X^2 + 1) - X \cdot 3X \in I$, so $I = (3, X^2 + 1)$. The quotient is $\mathbb{Z}[X]/I \cong \mathbb{F}_3[X]/(X^2 + 1)$, and $X^2 + 1$ has no root in $\mathbb{F}_3$, since $0^2 + 1$, $1^2 + 1$ and $2^2 + 1$ are $1, 2, 2$ modulo 3. So the quotient is a field with $9$ elements, with basis $1, \bar{X}$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$. So $aX + b \in I$ exactly when $3 \mid a$ and $3 \mid b$, and $L_0 = 3\mathbb{Z}$ and $L_1 = 3\mathbb{Z}$. Since $X^2 + 1 \in I$ has leading coefficient $1$, $L_n = \mathbb{Z}$ for $n \ge 2$. The chain $3\mathbb{Z} = 3\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z} = \cdots$ stops at $N = 2$. The proof chooses $f_{0,1} = 3$, $f_{1,1} = 3X$ and $f_{2,1} = X^2 + 1$, and gives $I = (3, 3X, X^2 + 1) = (3, X^2 + 1)$.

**Example (A coordinate ring).** The ring $\mathbb{C}[x, y]/(y^2 - x^3)$ is generated as a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra by $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$. It is Noetherian by the corollary on finitely generated algebras, although it is not a unique factorization domain: $\bar{y}^2 = \bar{x}^3$ gives two factorizations into irreducibles.

**Counterexample (A subalgebra of $k[x, y]$ that is not Noetherian).** Let $k$ be a field and let $A = k[x, xy, xy^2, xy^3, \dots] \subseteq k[x, y]$. Every monomial $x^a y^b$ with $a \ge 1$ lies in $A$, since $x^a y^b = x^{a-1} \cdot xy^b$. So $A$ is the set of polynomials $\lambda + x h$ with $\lambda \in k$ and $h \in k[x, y]$. Let $J_n$ be the ideal of $A$ generated by $x, xy, \dots, xy^n$. Then $xy^{n+1} \notin J_n$. Indeed, assume $xy^{n+1} = \sum_{i=0}^{n} c_i x y^i$ with $c_i = \lambda_i + x h_i \in A$. The terms of $x$-degree exactly $1$ on the right are $\sum_i \lambda_i x y^i$, since the terms $x h_i \cdot x y^i$ have $x$-degree at least $2$. Comparing them with the left side gives $y^{n+1} = \sum_{i=0}^n \lambda_i y^i$ in $k[y]$, which is false. So $J_0 \subsetneq J_1 \subsetneq \cdots$ is a strict chain, and $A$ is not Noetherian. The ring $A$ is a $k$-algebra, but it is not finitely generated. So the corollary needs the hypothesis "finitely generated", and a subring of the Noetherian ring $k[x, y]$ need not be Noetherian.

### The library around it

`inferInstance` proves `IsNoetherianRing (MvPolynomial (Fin 3) ℚ)` and `IsNoetherianRing (ℤ[X] ⧸ I)`. For an algebra over a Noetherian ring, use `Algebra.FiniteType.isNoetherianRing`.

Common errors of a learner:

- `MvPolynomial σ R` with an infinite type `σ` is not Noetherian, and instance search fails, correctly, without `[Finite σ]`.
- `Ideal.leadingCoeffNth` uses `degree ≤ n`, not `degree = n`. The weaker condition makes $0$ an element and gives an ideal.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find the ideals $L_n(I)$ for $I = (2X, X^2) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$, and give a set of generators of $I$ from them.
2. (A) Show that $L_n(I) = R$ for all large $n$ when $I$ contains a monic polynomial.
3. (A) Show that $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}]$ and $\mathbb{Q}[x, y, z]/(xy - z^2)$ are Noetherian.
4. (B) Let $I \subseteq J$ be ideals of $R[X]$ with $L_n(I) = L_n(J)$ for all $n$. Show that $I = J$. Hint: induction on the degree, as in the proof of the theorem.
5. (B) Show that the ring $\mathbb{Z} + X\mathbb{Q}[X]$ of rational polynomials with an integer constant term is not Noetherian. Hint: the chain of ideals $(X/2^n)$.
6. (B) Let $R$ be a ring such that $R[X]$ is Noetherian. Show that $R$ is Noetherian, with an argument that does not use the quotient $R[X]/(X)$. Hint: for an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $R$, use the ideal $\mathfrak{a}R[X]$ and $L_0$.
7. (C) Let $R$ be Noetherian and $M$ a finitely generated $R$-module. Show that $M[X] = M \otimes_R R[X]$, the module of polynomials with coefficients in $M$, is a Noetherian $R[X]$-module. Hint: copy the proof with submodules $L_n(N) \subseteq M$.

### Notes and history

David Hilbert proved the theorem in 1890 in Mathematische Annalen. He used it to prove that rings of invariants of forms are finitely generated. His proof was not constructive, and Paul Gordan is reported to have said of it that it was theology and not mathematics. Gröbner bases make the theorem constructive for $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$: they replace the leading coefficient by a leading monomial for a fixed monomial order.
