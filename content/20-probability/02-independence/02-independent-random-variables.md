## Independent random variables

### Overview

Random variables are independent when the σ-algebras that they generate are independent. Section 1 of this chapter defined independence of σ-algebras, so the definition for variables costs nothing. This section translates it into the language of laws: two variables are independent if and only if the law of the pair is the product of the two laws. From this criterion follow the two rules used in every computation: functions of independent variables are independent, and the law of the sum of two independent variables is the convolution of their laws.

The second half of the section answers a question that the definition leaves open: does an infinite sequence of independent variables with given laws exist? The answer is yes, on the infinite product of probability spaces. This is the space on which the laws of large numbers of Chapter 5 live.

### Definitions

**Definition (Independent random variables).** Let $X_i : \Omega \to E_i$ be random variables for $i \in I$. The σ-algebra *generated* by $X_i$ is $\sigma(X_i) = \{X_i^{-1}(B) : B \in \mathcal{E}_i\}$. The family $(X_i)$ is *independent* if the σ-algebras $\sigma(X_i)$ are independent, that is, if
$$P(X_i \in B_i \text{ for all } i \in J) = \prod_{i \in J} P(X_i \in B_i)$$
for each finite $J \subseteq I$ and all measurable $B_i \subseteq E_i$. Two variables $X, Y$ are independent if the family $(X, Y)$ is.

A sequence is *i.i.d.* (independent and identically distributed) if it is independent and all its members have the same law.

**Definition (Product measure).** Let $(E_i, \mathcal{E}_i, \mu_i)_{i \in I}$ be probability spaces. A *cylinder set* of $\prod_i E_i$ is a set $\{x : (x_i)_{i \in J} \in C\}$ for a finite $J \subseteq I$ and a measurable $C \subseteq \prod_{i \in J} E_i$. The product σ-algebra is generated by the cylinders. The *product measure* $\bigotimes_i \mu_i$ is the probability measure on $\prod_i E_i$ with
$$\Big(\bigotimes_i \mu_i\Big)\Big(\prod_{i \in J} B_i \times \prod_{i \notin J} E_i\Big) = \prod_{i \in J} \mu_i(B_i)$$
for each finite $J$ and measurable $B_i$.

For a finite $I$ this is the product measure of Volume 18. For an infinite $I$ its existence is a theorem, stated below.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Independence and the joint law).** Let $X : \Omega \to E$ and $Y : \Omega \to F$ be random variables. They are independent if and only if the law of the pair $(X, Y)$ is the product of the laws: $P_{(X, Y)} = P_X \otimes P_Y$.

*Proof.* Assume independence. For measurable $B \subseteq E$ and $C \subseteq F$,
$$P_{(X,Y)}(B \times C) = P(X \in B, Y \in C) = P(X \in B) P(Y \in C) = (P_X \otimes P_Y)(B \times C).$$
The rectangles $B \times C$ form a π-system that generates the product σ-algebra, and both measures are probability measures. By the uniqueness theorem of Volume 18 they are equal. Conversely, if $P_{(X,Y)} = P_X \otimes P_Y$, the same computation read backwards gives $P(X \in B, Y \in C) = P(X \in B)P(Y \in C)$, which is the definition. $\square$

For a finite family the same proof shows: $(X_1, \ldots, X_n)$ is independent if and only if its law is $P_{X_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes P_{X_n}$.

**Theorem (Functions of independent variables).** Let $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ be independent.

1. For measurable maps $g_i$, the variables $g_i(X_i)$ are independent.
2. Let $S, T \subseteq I$ be disjoint finite sets. Then $(X_i)_{i \in S}$ and $(X_i)_{i \in T}$, as vector valued variables, are independent. So any function of the first block is independent of any function of the second block.

*Proof.* 1. $\sigma(g_i(X_i)) \subseteq \sigma(X_i)$, because $(g_i \circ X_i)^{-1}(B) = X_i^{-1}(g_i^{-1}(B))$. A subfamily of σ-algebras of an independent family is independent.

2. The σ-algebra generated by the vector $(X_i)_{i \in S}$ is generated by the sets $X_i^{-1}(B_i)$ for $i \in S$, so it is $\sigma\big(\bigcup_{i \in S} \sigma(X_i)\big)$. The grouping theorem of Section 1 gives the independence of the two blocks. Part 1, applied to the two blocks, gives the last claim. $\square$

For example, if $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent, then $X_1 + \cdots + X_{n-1}$ and $X_n$ are independent, and an induction on $n$ often starts from this remark.

**Theorem (The law of a sum).** Let $X, Y$ be independent real variables with laws $\mu$ and $\nu$. Then $X + Y$ has the law $\mu * \nu$, the image of $\mu \otimes \nu$ under $(x, y) \mapsto x + y$:
$$P(X + Y \in B) = \int \nu(B - x)\, d\mu(x).$$

*Proof.* The law of $(X, Y)$ is $\mu \otimes \nu$ by the joint law theorem. So the law of $X + Y$ is the image of $\mu \otimes \nu$ under the addition map. By Fubini's theorem (Volume 18) applied to $\mathbf{1}_B(x + y)$,
$$(\mu \otimes \nu)(\{(x,y) : x + y \in B\}) = \int \Big(\int \mathbf{1}_{B}(x + y)\, d\nu(y)\Big) d\mu(x) = \int \nu(B - x)\, d\mu(x). \quad \square$$

**Theorem (Existence of independent families).** Let $(\mu_i)_{i \in I}$ be probability measures on measurable spaces $E_i$. On $\Omega = \prod_i E_i$ with $P = \bigotimes_i \mu_i$, the coordinate maps $X_i(\omega) = \omega_i$ are independent and $X_i$ has law $\mu_i$.

*Proof.* The existence of the product measure on cylinders is the theorem of Kolmogorov (or of Ionescu-Tulcea for a countable $I$, Chapter 8); Volume 18 proves the finite case and Mathlib proves the general case as `Measure.infinitePi`. Given $P$, take a finite $J$ and measurable $B_i$ for $i \in J$. The event $\{X_i \in B_i \text{ for } i \in J\}$ is the box $\prod_{i \in J} B_i \times \prod_{i \notin J} E_i$, so its probability is $\prod_{i \in J} \mu_i(B_i)$. With $J = \{i\}$ this says $P(X_i \in B) = \mu_i(B)$, so $X_i$ has law $\mu_i$. Then the product formula is $\prod_{i \in J} P(X_i \in B_i)$, which is independence. $\square$

::: mathlib
`ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun_infinitePi` · `Mathlib.Probability.Independence.InfinitePi`

The general form: for probability spaces `(Ω i, P i)` and measurable maps `X i : Ω i → 𝓧 i`, the maps `ω ↦ X i (ω i)` on the product `infinitePi P` are independent. With `X i = id` these are the coordinates. The law of a coordinate is `infinitePi_map_eval`. The converse direction is `iIndepFun.hasLaw_infinitePi`: independent variables with laws `μ i` have the joint law `infinitePi μ`. The theorem `exists_iid` of Chapter 1 is the corollary for one law `μ` repeated.

### Examples

**Example (Coin tosses).** Let $\Omega = \{0, 1\}^n$ with $P(\{\omega\}) = p^{k(\omega)} (1 - p)^{n - k(\omega)}$, where $k(\omega)$ is the number of ones. This is the product of $n$ copies of the Bernoulli law $\beta_p$ with $\beta_p(\{1\}) = p$. The coordinates $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ are independent with law $\beta_p$. The sum $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ has
$$P(S_n = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k},$$
because the event $\{S_n = k\}$ is the set of the $\binom{n}{k}$ words with $k$ ones, each of probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. This is the binomial law of Chapter 4. The convolution theorem gives the same result by induction: the law of $S_{n-1} + X_n$ is the convolution of the binomial law with parameters $n - 1, p$ and $\beta_p$, and Pascal's rule $\binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1} = \binom{n}{k}$ finishes the computation.

**Example (The sum of two independent uniform variables).** Let $X, Y$ be independent and uniform on $[0, 1]$. By the convolution formula, $P(X + Y \le t) = \int_0^1 P(Y \le t - x)\, dx$. For $0 \le t \le 1$ this is $\int_0^t (t - x)\, dx = t^2/2$, and for $1 \le t \le 2$ it is $1 - (2 - t)^2/2$. The density is the derivative: $t$ on $[0, 1]$ and $2 - t$ on $[1, 2]$, a triangle.

**Counterexample (Uncorrelated but dependent).** Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$. Then $P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 1/3$, but $P(X = 0) P(Y = 0) = (1/3)(1/3) = 1/9$, so $X$ and $Y$ are not independent. Yet $E[XY] = E[X^3] = 0 = E[X] E[Y]$. Chapter 3 proves that independent integrable variables satisfy $E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$; this example shows that the converse fails.

### The library around it

The API of `IndepFun` and `iIndepFun` in `Mathlib.Probability.Independence.Basic` mirrors that of `Indep`. There are the characterisation lemmas `IndepFun_iff`, `iIndepFun_iff_measure_inter_preimage_eq_mul`, the symmetry `IndepFun.symm`, the congruence `IndepFun.congr` for a.e. equal maps, and the constant cases `indepFun_const_left` and `indepFun_const_right`. A large family gives the independence of arithmetic combinations of an independent family: `iIndepFun.indepFun_add_left`, `.indepFun_mul_left`, `.indepFun_sub_right`, `.indepFun_finsetSum_of_notMem`, `.indepFun_prodMk`. The file `Mathlib.Probability.IdentDistribIndep` has `IdentDistrib.prodMk`: if `X` and `Y` are independent on one space, `X'` and `Y'` independent on another, and the pairs agree in law componentwise, then the pairs `(X, Y)` and `(X', Y')` are identically distributed.

The module `Mathlib.Probability.Independence.BoundedContinuousFunction` tests independence with bounded continuous functions instead of measurable sets. The theorem `indepFun_of_bcf` says that two variables $X$ and $Y$ with values in Borel spaces with the property `HasOuterApproxClosed`, for example metric spaces, are independent if $E[f(X) g(Y)] = E[f(X)] \, E[g(Y)]$ for all real bounded continuous functions $f$ and $g$. The theorem `indep_comap_of_bcf` is the version for a $\sigma$-algebra and a variable: it asks $E[1_A f(X)] = P(A) \, E[f(X)]$ for each $A$ in the $\sigma$-algebra. The variants `pi_indepFun_pi_of_bcf` and `process_indepFun_process_of_bcf` treat finite families and processes, and they need only finite families of test functions. This criterion is the form that a proof with characteristic functions or with weak convergence uses.

The module `Mathlib.Probability.Independence.Process.Basic` reduces the independence of processes to finite families. The theorem `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun.indepFun_process` says that a variable $X$ is independent of a process $(Y_s)_{s \in S}$ if $X$ is independent of each finite family $(Y_s)_{s \in I}$. The theorem `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun.process_indepFun_process` is the statement for two processes, and `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun.iIndepFun_process` is the statement for a family of processes. The proofs use the $\pi$-system of cylinder sets. The lemma `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun.process_congr` says that independence passes to modifications, and `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun.process_congr` is the version for a family. Each statement has a version for kernels in the namespace `ProbabilityTheory.Kernel`, and a version with the subscript $0$ that asks only almost everywhere measurability.

Common errors of a learner:

- `IndepFun f g μ` for `f g : Ω → ℝ` uses the Borel σ-algebra of `ℝ` from the instance. If a different σ-algebra is meant, it must be given explicitly.
- `iIndepFun` of a family is stronger than the independence of each pair. For three variables, a proof of `iIndepFun` needs the product formula for the triple.
- The lemmas about sums need measurability of each `f i`, often as `∀ i, Measurable (f i)`, besides the independence.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $X$ and $Y$ with finitely many values are independent if and only if $P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x) P(Y = y)$ for all values $x, y$.
2. (A) Let $X$ be independent of itself. Show that $X$ is a.s. constant.
3. (A) Let $X_1, X_2, X_3$ be independent. Show that $X_1 + X_2$ and $X_3^2$ are independent.
4. (B) Let $X, Y$ be independent with values in $\mathbb{N}$. Show that $P(X + Y = n) = \sum_{k=0}^n P(X = k) P(Y = n - k)$, and use it to find the law of the sum of two independent Poisson variables with parameters $\lambda$ and $\mu$ (Chapter 4 defines them).
5. (B) Let $X, Y$ be independent and uniform on $\{1, \ldots, n\}$. Compute $P(X = Y)$ and $P(X < Y)$.
6. (B) Let $X, Y$ be independent with $P(X = \pm 1) = P(Y = \pm 1) = 1/2$. Show that $X$, $Y$ and $XY$ are pairwise independent but not independent.
7. (C) Let $\omega \in [0, 1)$ be uniform and $d_n(\omega)$ its binary digits. Show that $U = \sum_k d_{2k}(\omega) 2^{-k}$ and $V = \sum_k d_{2k+1}(\omega) 2^{-k}$ are independent and uniform on $[0, 1]$. Conclude that one uniform variable produces an i.i.d. sequence of uniform variables.

### Notes and history

The construction of infinite product measures is due to Kolmogorov, in his book of 1933, as a case of his extension theorem for consistent families of finite dimensional laws. For a countable product the theorem of Ionescu-Tulcea (1949) gives the measure without a topological assumption on the factors. Mathlib constructs `Measure.infinitePi` for an arbitrary index type and arbitrary measurable spaces, which is possible because each factor is a probability measure.
