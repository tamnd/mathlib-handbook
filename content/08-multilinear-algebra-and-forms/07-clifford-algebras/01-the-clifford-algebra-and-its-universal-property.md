## The Clifford algebra and its universal property

### Overview

In the exterior algebra of Chapter 4 every vector squares to zero. The Clifford algebra of a quadratic form $Q$ on $M$ changes this one relation: every vector $x$ squares to the scalar $Q(x)$. Orthogonal vectors then anticommute.

This section defines the Clifford algebra by the method of Chapter 3, proves its universal property, computes the small examples, and proves the rank $2^n$ for an orthogonal basis. It uses the quadratic forms of Chapter 6. Here $R$ is a commutative ring.

### Definitions

**Definition (Clifford algebra).** Let $I_Q \subseteq T(M)$ be the two-sided ideal generated by the elements $x \otimes x - Q(x) \cdot 1$ for $x \in M$. The Clifford algebra is $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) = T(M) / I_Q$. The map $\iota : M \to \mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ is $\iota$ of $T(M)$ followed by the quotient map. So $\iota(x)^2 = Q(x)$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$, where a scalar $r$ stands for $r \cdot 1$.

The polar form of $Q$ is $B(x, y) = Q(x + y) - Q(x) - Q(y)$ (Chapter 6). It is bilinear and symmetric, and $B(x, x) = 2 Q(x)$. The vectors $x, y$ are orthogonal when $B(x, y) = 0$. A basis $(e_i)$ is orthogonal when $B(e_i, e_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Proposition (Products of two vectors).** For all $x \in M$, $\iota(x)^2 = Q(x)$. For all $x, y \in M$,
$$\iota(x) \iota(y) + \iota(y) \iota(x) = B(x, y).$$
So orthogonal vectors anticommute in $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$.

*Proof.* The first equation holds because $x \otimes x - Q(x) \in I_Q$. For the second, expand $\iota(x + y)^2 = Q(x + y)$: the left side is $\iota(x)^2 + \iota(x)\iota(y) + \iota(y)\iota(x) + \iota(y)^2 = Q(x) + \iota(x)\iota(y) + \iota(y)\iota(x) + Q(y)$. Subtract $Q(x) + Q(y)$. $\square$

**Theorem (Universal property).** Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra and $f : M \to A$ a linear map with $f(x)^2 = Q(x) \cdot 1$ for all $x$. There is a unique algebra homomorphism $\bar f : \mathrm{Cl}(Q) \to A$ with $\bar f \circ \iota = f$.

*Proof.* Let $g : T(M) \to A$ be the lift of $f$ (Chapter 3, Section 2). Then $g(x \otimes x - Q(x)) = f(x)^2 - Q(x) = 0$, so the generators of $I_Q$ lie in the two-sided ideal $\ker g$. So $g$ factors through the quotient as $\bar f$. The $\iota(x)$ generate $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$, which gives uniqueness. $\square$

Let $f : M_1 \to M_2$ be an isometry, that is, a linear map with $Q_2(f x) = Q_1(x)$. Then $\iota \circ f$ satisfies $\iota(f x)^2 = Q_1(x)$, and its lift is an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(f) : \mathrm{Cl}(Q_1) \to \mathrm{Cl}(Q_2)$. Isometric forms have isomorphic Clifford algebras.

For a finite set $S = \{s_1 < \dots < s_k\}$ of indices put $e_S = \iota(e_{s_1}) \cdots \iota(e_{s_k})$, with $e_\emptyset = 1$.

**Theorem (Rank of a Clifford algebra).** Let $M$ be free with basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$. Then the $2^n$ products $e_S$ span $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$. If the basis is orthogonal, they form a basis, and $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ is free of rank $2^n$.

*Proof.* Spanning. The algebra is spanned by the products $\iota(e_{i_1}) \cdots \iota(e_{i_k})$ of basis vectors. Use induction on the length $k$, and for a fixed length on the number of pairs out of order. If two neighbours are equal, replace $\iota(e_i)^2$ by $Q(e_i)$, which gives a shorter product. If two neighbours are out of order, $i > j$, replace $\iota(e_i)\iota(e_j)$ by $-\iota(e_j)\iota(e_i) + B(e_i, e_j)$. This gives a product with fewer pairs out of order and a shorter product. In the end only products with strictly increasing indices remain, and these are the $e_S$.

Independence for an orthogonal basis. Let $q_i = Q(e_i)$, and for a finite set $U$ let $q_U = \prod_{i \in U} q_i$. Let $\varepsilon(S, T) = (-1)^N$, with $N$ the number of pairs $(s, t) \in S \times T$ with $s > t$. Let $E$ be the free module with basis $(b_S)$ over the subsets of $\{1, \dots, n\}$, with the product
$$b_S b_T = \varepsilon(S, T)\, q_{S \cap T}\, b_{S \triangle T},$$
where $S \triangle T$ is the symmetric difference. The sign $\varepsilon$ is multiplicative in each argument for $\triangle$, since a pair counted twice does not change the parity. So the signs of $(b_S b_T) b_U$ and $b_S (b_T b_U)$ are both $\varepsilon(S, T)\varepsilon(S, U)\varepsilon(T, U)$. For an index in exactly the sets marked by $s, t, u \in \{0, 1\}$, the power of its $q$ is $st + (s \oplus t) u$ on the left and $tu + s (t \oplus u)$ on the right, and the eight cases show that these are equal. So $E$ is associative, with unit $b_\emptyset$. Define $f : M \to E$ by $f(e_i) = b_{\{i\}}$. For $x = \sum_i x_i e_i$,
$$f(x)^2 = \sum_i x_i^2 q_i + \sum_{i < j} x_i x_j (b_{\{i\}} b_{\{j\}} + b_{\{j\}} b_{\{i\}}) = \sum_i x_i^2 q_i = Q(x),$$
since $b_{\{j\}} b_{\{i\}} = -b_{\{i, j\}}$ for $i < j$, and $Q(x) = \sum_i x_i^2 q_i$ for an orthogonal basis. The lift $\bar f$ sends $e_S$ to $b_S$, and the $b_S$ are independent. So the $e_S$ are independent. $\square$

When $2$ is a unit in a field $R$, each quadratic form has an orthogonal basis (Chapter 5), so the theorem gives the rank $2^n$ for all forms over such a field. When $2$ is a unit in $R$, the linear isomorphism `CliffordAlgebra.equivExterior` with $\Lambda(M)$ (Section 2) gives the rank for any free $M$.

### Examples

**Example (The complex numbers).** Let $M = \mathbb{R}$ and $Q(x) = -x^2$, the form of $\mathbb{R}^{0,1}$. Let $e = \iota(1)$. The theorem gives the basis $1, e$ with $e^2 = Q(1) = -1$. The linear map $f(x) = x i$ into $\mathbb{C}$ satisfies $f(x)^2 = -x^2 = Q(x)$, so it lifts to $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \to \mathbb{C}$. The lift sends $a + b e$ to $a + b i$, so it is an isomorphism, and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{0,1}) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Example (The quaternions).** Let $M = R^2$ and $Q(x, y) = c_1 x^2 + c_2 y^2$. Let $i = \iota(1, 0)$ and $j = \iota(0, 1)$. Then $i^2 = c_1$ and $j^2 = c_2$. The polar form is $B((1,0), (0,1)) = Q(1,1) - Q(1,0) - Q(0,1) = 0$, so $ij = -ji$. Put $k = ij$. Then $k^2 = ijij = -i^2 j^2 = -c_1 c_2$, $ik = i^2 j = c_1 j$, and $kj = i j^2 = c_2 i$. For $R = \mathbb{R}$ and $c_1 = c_2 = -1$ these are the rules $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$ of Hamilton.

**Example (The dual numbers).** Let $M = R$ and $Q = 0$. Then $\mathrm{Cl}(0) = \Lambda(R)$ has the basis $1, \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon = \iota(1)$ and $\varepsilon^2 = 0$. This is the ring $R[\varepsilon] = R[X]/(X^2)$ of dual numbers.

**Counterexample (The sign of the form matters).** Let $M = \mathbb{R}$ and $Q(x) = x^2$. Then $e = \iota(1)$ has $e^2 = 1$, and $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \cong \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ by $a + b e \mapsto (a + b, a - b)$. It has the zero divisors $(1 + e)(1 - e) = 0$, so the algebra depends on the form, not only on the module.

### Exercises

1. (A) In $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ for $Q(x, y) = -x^2 - y^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, compute $(1 + i)(j + k)$ in the basis $1, i, j, k$. Hint: use $ij = k$, $ik = -j$.
2. (A) Show that $\iota(x)$ is a unit of $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ when $Q(x)$ is a unit of $R$, with inverse $Q(x)^{-1} \iota(x)$. Hint: $\iota(x)^2 = Q(x)$.
3. (B) Show that $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$ for $Q(x, y) = x^2 - y^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Hint: send $e_1$ and $e_2$ to $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.
4. (B) For $Q(x, y) = xy$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, show that $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$. Hint: send $e_1, e_2$ to $E_{12}$ and $E_{21}$.
5. (B) Show that $\mathrm{Cl}(Q_1 \perp Q_2)$ has rank $2^{n_1 + n_2}$ when both forms have orthogonal bases. Hint: join the two bases.
6. (B) Let $Q(x) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$. Show that $\omega = e_1 e_2 e_3$ is central and $\omega^2 = -1$. Hint: move each $e_i$ past $\omega$.
7. (C) Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ let $Q(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2$. Show that $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ has rank $4$, although $Q$ has no orthogonal basis. Hint: find $u, v \in M_2(\mathbb{F}_2)$ with $u^2 = v^2 = 1$ and $uv + vu = 1$.

### Notes and history

Clifford introduced these algebras in 1878, as a common extension of the quaternions of Hamilton, from 1843, and of the exterior algebra of Grassmann, from 1844. Since Mathlib defines the polar form with no factor $\frac{1}{2}$, the definitions work over any commutative ring.
