## Coxeter groups

### Overview

The Weyl group of a root system (Chapter 7) is generated by the reflections in the simple roots. These reflections satisfy relations of a very special form: each one has order $2$, and the product of two of them has an order fixed by the angle between the two roots. Coxeter classified the finite groups generated by reflections in 1934, and these groups have presentations of this form. A Coxeter group is a group given by such a presentation, with no geometry attached. The combinatorics of words in the generators carries a large part of the theory of Weyl groups and of buildings.

This section explains the definitions, the universal property, the length function and its parity, reduced words, reflections and the inversion sequence of a word, with the deletion step that Mathlib proves. The examples are the Weyl groups of $A_2$, $B_2$ and $G_2$, which are dihedral groups of orders $6$, $8$ and $12$, and the infinite dihedral group.

Throughout, $B$ is an index set, $W$ is a group, $s_i$ are the simple reflections and $\ell$ is the length. A word is a finite list $\omega = (i_1, \ldots, i_r)$ of indices, and $\pi(\omega) = s_{i_1} \cdots s_{i_r}$ is its product.

### Definitions

**Definition (Coxeter matrix).** A Coxeter matrix on $B$ is a symmetric matrix $M = (m_{ij})$ with entries in $\mathbb{N}$ such that $m_{ii} = 1$ and $m_{ij} \ne 1$ for $i \ne j$. The value $m_{ij} = 0$ stands for $\infty$.

With $m_{ij} = 0$ the relation $(s_is_j)^{m_{ij}} = 1$ below is $1 = 1$, which imposes nothing, as $m_{ij} = \infty$ does in the textbooks.

**Definition (Coxeter group).** The Coxeter group of $M$ is the group
$$W(M) = \langle s_i, i \in B \mid (s_is_j)^{m_{ij}} = 1 \text{ for all } i, j \rangle,$$
the quotient of the free group on $B$ by the normal subgroup generated by the relators $(s_is_j)^{m_{ij}}$. A Coxeter system on a group $W$ is an isomorphism $W \cong W(M)$, and $W$ is a Coxeter group if it has a Coxeter system for some $M$.

The diagonal relations $(s_is_i)^1 = 1$ say that $s_i^2 = 1$. For $m_{ij} = 2$ the relation $(s_is_j)^2 = 1$ says that $s_i$ and $s_j$ commute. For $m_{ij} = 3$ it says that $s_is_js_i = s_js_is_j$.

**Definition (Length and reduced words).** The length $\ell(w)$ of $w \in W$ is the least $r$ such that $w = \pi(\omega)$ for a word $\omega$ of length $r$. A word $\omega$ is reduced if $\ell(\pi(\omega))$ equals the length of $\omega$.

The length is well defined: the $s_i$ generate $W$ as a group and $s_i^{-1} = s_i$, so they generate it as a monoid.

**Definition (Descents).** An index $i$ is a left descent of $w$ if $\ell(s_iw) < \ell(w)$, and a right descent if $\ell(ws_i) < \ell(w)$.

**Definition (Reflections and inversions).** A reflection is a conjugate $t = us_iu^{-1}$ of a simple reflection. A reflection $t$ is a right inversion of $w$ if $\ell(wt) < \ell(w)$, and a left inversion if $\ell(tw) < \ell(w)$.

**Definition (Inversion sequence).** For a word $\omega = (i_1, \ldots, i_r)$, let $\omega_{>j} = (i_{j+1}, \ldots, i_r)$. The right inversion sequence of $\omega$ is $(t_1, \ldots, t_r)$ with
$$t_j = \pi(\omega_{>j})^{-1}\, s_{i_j}\, \pi(\omega_{>j}).$$
Each $t_j$ is a reflection. The left inversion sequence is defined in the mirror way, with prefixes.

### Theorems and proofs

**Proposition (Braid relations).** Let $m = m_{ij} \ne 0$. Then the alternating products of length $m$ agree:
$$\underbrace{s_is_js_i \cdots}_{m} = \underbrace{s_js_is_j\cdots}_{m}.$$

*Proof.* Put $x = s_i$ and $y = s_j$, so $x^2 = y^2 = 1$ and $(xy)^m = 1$. Since $(xy)^{-1} = y^{-1}x^{-1} = yx$, the relation gives $(xy)^a = (yx)^{m-a}$ for each $a \le m$. If $m = 2k$, take $a = k$: $(xy)^k = (yx)^k$, and these are the two alternating products of length $2k$. If $m = 2k+1$, take $a = k+1$: $(xy)^{k}x\,y = (yx)^k$, and multiplying on the right by $y$ gives $(xy)^kx = (yx)^ky$. These are the two alternating products of length $2k+1$. $\square$

**Theorem (Universal property).** Let $G$ be a monoid and $f \colon B \to G$ a map with $(f(i)f(j))^{m_{ij}} = 1$ for all $i, j$. Then there is a unique monoid homomorphism $\bar f \colon W \to G$ with $\bar f(s_i) = f(i)$.

*Proof.* The case $i = j$ gives $f(i)^2 = 1$, so $f(i)$ is a unit of $G$, its own inverse. By the universal property of the free group, $f$ extends to a group homomorphism from the free group on $B$ to the group of units $G^\times$. It sends each relator $(s_is_j)^{m_{ij}}$ to $1$, so it factors through the quotient $W(M)$. Compose with $W \cong W(M)$ and the inclusion $G^\times \to G$. Uniqueness holds because the $s_i$ generate $W$ as a monoid. $\square$

**Theorem (Parity of the length).** There is a homomorphism $\varepsilon \colon W \to \{\pm 1\}$ with $\varepsilon(s_i) = -1$, and $\varepsilon(w) = (-1)^{\ell(w)}$. Consequently $\ell(s_i) = 1$, $\ell(uv) \equiv \ell(u) + \ell(v) \pmod 2$, and $\ell(ws_i) = \ell(w) \pm 1$ for each $w$ and $i$.

*Proof.* The constant map $f(i) = -1$ satisfies $((-1)(-1))^{m_{ij}} = 1$, so the universal property gives $\varepsilon$. If $w = \pi(\omega)$ with $\omega$ reduced, then $\varepsilon(w) = (-1)^{\ell(w)}$, since each letter contributes $-1$. The congruence follows because $\varepsilon$ is a homomorphism. Since $\varepsilon(s_i) = -1 \ne 1$, $s_i \ne 1$ and $\ell(s_i) \ge 1$, and the word $(i)$ gives $\ell(s_i) \le 1$. Finally $\ell(ws_i) \le \ell(w) + 1$ and $\ell(w) = \ell(ws_is_i) \le \ell(ws_i) + 1$, so $|\ell(ws_i) - \ell(w)| \le 1$. The parity of $\ell(ws_i)$ differs from that of $\ell(w)$, so the difference is $\pm 1$. $\square$

**Proposition (Basic properties of the length).** $\ell(w) = 0$ if and only if $w = 1$, $\ell(w^{-1}) = \ell(w)$, and $\ell(uv) \le \ell(u) + \ell(v)$. Every subword $(i_a, \ldots, i_b)$ of consecutive letters of a reduced word is reduced. If $w \ne 1$, then $w$ has a left descent.

*Proof.* The empty word is the only word of length $0$, and its product is $1$. If $w = s_{i_1}\cdots s_{i_r}$, then $w^{-1} = s_{i_r} \cdots s_{i_1}$, since $s_i^{-1} = s_i$; so $\ell(w^{-1}) \le \ell(w)$, and the same for $w^{-1}$ gives equality. The concatenation of words for $u$ and $v$ is a word for $uv$. For subwords, write $\omega = \alpha\beta\gamma$. If $\beta$ is not reduced, there is a shorter word $\beta'$ with $\pi(\beta') = \pi(\beta)$, and $\alpha\beta'\gamma$ is a shorter word for $\pi(\omega)$, a contradiction. If $w \ne 1$, take a reduced word $(i_1, \ldots, i_r)$ with $r \ge 1$. Then $s_{i_1}w = \pi(i_2, \ldots, i_r)$ has length at most $r - 1$, so $i_1$ is a left descent. $\square$

**Proposition (Long alternating words are not reduced).** If $m = m_{ij} \ne 0$ and $n > m$, the alternating word of length $n$ in $i$ and $j$ is not reduced.

*Proof.* By the previous proposition it is enough to treat $n = m + 1$, because the alternating word of length $n$ contains an alternating word of length $m + 1$ as a consecutive subword. Write the word as $a_1a_2\cdots a_{m+1}$. Its last $m$ letters form an alternating word that starts with $a_2$. By the braid relation, the product of these $m$ letters equals the product of the other alternating word of length $m$, which starts with the other letter, $a_1$. So $\pi(a_1 \cdots a_{m+1}) = s_{a_1}s_{a_1}\pi(\beta) = \pi(\beta)$, where $\beta$ has length $m - 1$. The original word had length $m + 1$, so it is not reduced. $\square$

**Lemma (Reflections have odd length).** For a reflection $t$, $t^2 = 1$, $\ell(t)$ is odd and $\ell(wt) \ne \ell(w)$ for every $w$. The simple reflection $s_i$ is a right inversion of $w$ if and only if $i$ is a right descent of $w$.

*Proof.* $(us_iu^{-1})^2 = us_i^2u^{-1} = 1$. Also $\varepsilon(t) = \varepsilon(u)\varepsilon(s_i)\varepsilon(u)^{-1} = -1$, so $\ell(t)$ is odd, and $\varepsilon(wt) = -\varepsilon(w)$ gives $\ell(wt) \ne \ell(w)$. The last claim is the definition, since $s_i$ is a reflection. $\square$

**Theorem (Deletion step and inversion sequences).** Let $\omega = (i_1, \ldots, i_r)$ with right inversion sequence $(t_1, \ldots, t_r)$.

1. $\pi(\omega)\,t_j = \pi(\omega \setminus j)$, where $\omega \setminus j$ is $\omega$ with the letter at position $j$ removed.
2. $t_1t_2\cdots t_r = \pi(\omega)^{-1}$.
3. If $\omega$ is reduced, each $t_j$ is a right inversion of $\pi(\omega)$, and the $t_j$ are pairwise distinct.

*Proof.* Write $\alpha = (i_1, \ldots, i_{j-1})$ and $\gamma = \omega_{>j}$. Then $\pi(\omega)t_j = \pi(\alpha)s_{i_j}\pi(\gamma)\pi(\gamma)^{-1}s_{i_j}\pi(\gamma) = \pi(\alpha)\pi(\gamma)$, which is (1). For (2), use induction on $r$. For $\omega = (i) \cdot \omega'$, the first term is $t_1 = \pi(\omega')^{-1}s_i\pi(\omega')$, the others form the sequence of $\omega'$, and by induction
$$t_1 \cdot \pi(\omega')^{-1} = \pi(\omega')^{-1}s_i = (s_i\pi(\omega'))^{-1} = \pi(\omega)^{-1}.$$
For (3), (1) gives $\ell(\pi(\omega)t_j) \le r - 1 < r = \ell(\pi(\omega))$. For distinctness, let $j < j'$ and write $\omega = \alpha\,x\,\beta\,y\,\gamma$, with the letter $x$ at position $j$ and $y$ at position $j'$. Then $t_{j'} = \pi(\gamma)^{-1}s_y\pi(\gamma)$ and $t_j = \pi(\beta y\gamma)^{-1}s_x\pi(\beta y \gamma)$. The equation $t_j = t_{j'}$ becomes, after multiplying on the left by $\pi(\beta y\gamma)$,
$$s_x\,\pi(\beta)\,s_y\,\pi(\gamma) = \pi(\beta)s_y\pi(\gamma)\pi(\gamma)^{-1}s_y\pi(\gamma) = \pi(\beta)\pi(\gamma).$$
So $s_x\pi(\beta)s_y = \pi(\beta)$, and $\pi(\omega) = \pi(\alpha)\pi(\beta)\pi(\gamma)$ is the product of a word of length $r - 2$. This contradicts the reducedness of $\omega$. $\square$

**Remark (What the index has).** The theorem gives $r = \ell(w)$ distinct right inversions for each reduced word of $w$. The classical theory proves that these are all the right inversions of $w$, and deduces the exchange condition: if $\ell(ws_i) < \ell(w)$, then $ws_i$ has a reduced word obtained from a reduced word of $w$ by deleting one letter. The index of this edition has no declaration for the exchange condition, for the statement that the inversion sequence lists all inversions, for Matsumoto's theorem or for the classification of finite Coxeter groups. The source file also lists the distinctness of the simple reflections as a goal that is not yet proved.

### Examples

**Example (Type $A_2$ and $S_3$).** Let $M$ be the matrix with $m_{12} = 3$, which is `CoxeterMatrix.A 2`. Then $W = \langle s, t \mid s^2 = t^2 = (st)^3 = 1\rangle$. The assignment $s \mapsto (1\,2)$, $t \mapsto (2\,3)$ satisfies the relations, because $(1\,2)(2\,3)$ is a $3$-cycle. By the universal property it gives a homomorphism $W \to S_3$, which is surjective since the two transpositions generate $S_3$. Each element of $W$ is an alternating product of $s$ and $t$, since $s^2 = t^2 = 1$ removes repeated letters. By the braid relation $sts = tst$, an alternating word of length $4$ reduces, for example $stst = s(tst) = s(sts) = ts$. So $W = \{1, s, t, st, ts, sts\}$, $|W| \le 6$, and $W \cong S_3$. The six images are distinct, so the lengths are $0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3$, and $w_0 = sts = tst$ is the longest element. For the reduced word $(s, t, s)$ the inversion sequence is
$$t_1 = (ts)^{-1}s(ts) = ststs = t, \qquad t_2 = sts, \qquad t_3 = s,$$
where $ststs = s(tst)s = s(sts)s = t$. These are the three reflections of $S_3$, the three transpositions. Their product is $t \cdot sts \cdot s = tst = w_0 = w_0^{-1}$, as (2) predicts. They match the three positive roots of $A_2$.

**Example (Type $B_2$ and the square).** For $m_{12} = 4$, which is `CoxeterMatrix.B 2` and also `CoxeterMatrix.I 2`, map $s$ to $(x, y) \mapsto (-x, y)$ and $t$ to $(x, y) \mapsto (y, x)$. Then $st$ maps $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$, a rotation by a right angle, of order $4$. The relations hold, and the image is the symmetry group of the square, of order $8$. As for $A_2$, the elements are the alternating words of length at most $4$, with $stst = tsts$, so $|W| \le 2 \cdot 4 = 8$ and $W$ is the dihedral group of order $8$. The longest element $stst = -\mathrm{id}$ has length $4$, and the four reflections $s$, $t$, $sts$, $tst$ match the four positive roots $e_1, e_2, e_1 + e_2, e_2 - e_1$ of $B_2$, with $s$ the reflection in the short root $e_1$ and $t$ the reflection in the long root $e_2 - e_1$.

**Example (Type $G_2$).** For `CoxeterMatrix.G₂`, with $m_{12} = 6$, the same argument with the reflections of the plane in two lines at angle $\pi/6$ gives the dihedral group of order $12$. The longest element $(st)^3 = -\mathrm{id}$ has length $6$, and the six reflections match the six positive roots of $G_2$.

**Example (The infinite dihedral group).** Let $m_{12} = 0$. Then $W = \langle s, t \mid s^2 = t^2 = 1\rangle$. Map $s$ to $x \mapsto -x$ and $t$ to $x \mapsto 1 - x$ on $\mathbb{R}$. Then $st$ is $x \mapsto x - 1$, of infinite order. The alternating words are $(st)^k$, $(ts)^k$, $(st)^ks$ and $(ts)^kt$ for $k \ge 0$. Their images are $x \mapsto x - k$, $x \mapsto x + k$, $x \mapsto -x - k$ and $x \mapsto -x + 1 + k$, and these maps are pairwise distinct, except that $k = 0$ gives the empty word twice. So all alternating words are reduced, and $W$ has exactly two elements of each length $n \ge 1$.

Common errors of a learner: The first error is to write $\infty$ in a Coxeter matrix; Mathlib uses $0$, and `M i i' = 0` gives no relation. The second error is to read `CoxeterMatrix.I m` as the dihedral type $I_2(m)$; it is $I_2(m+2)$, so `I 1` is $A_2$. The third error is to expect the exchange condition or the distinctness of the simple reflections from the library; neither is in this edition, and for a concrete matrix the distinctness must come from an explicit representation, as in the examples. The fourth error is to expect a link with `RootPairing.weylGroup` of Chapter 7; the index of this edition has no declaration that makes the Weyl group of a root pairing a Coxeter group.

### Exercises

1. (A) Write the Coxeter matrix of type $A_3$ and check that $s_1 \mapsto (1\,2)$, $s_2 \mapsto (2\,3)$, $s_3 \mapsto (3\,4)$ satisfies the relations. Hint: disjoint transpositions commute, and $(1\,2)(2\,3)$ is a $3$-cycle.
2. (A) In the group of type $A_2$, find all reduced words of the longest element and all left descents of $st$. Hint: there are two reduced words; compute $\ell(s \cdot st)$ and $\ell(t \cdot st)$.
3. (A) Show that $\ell(uv) \ge |\ell(u) - \ell(v)|$. Hint: apply subadditivity to $u = (uv)v^{-1}$.
4. (B) Show that for $m_{12} = m \ge 2$ the Coxeter group of rank $2$ has order $2m$ and is the dihedral group of the regular $m$-gon. Hint: copy the proof for $A_2$, with a rotation by $2\pi/m$.
5. (B) Show that the right inversions of $w$ in the group of type $B_2$ are exactly the entries of the inversion sequence of any reduced word of $w$, for each of the eight elements. Hint: list the four reflections and compute lengths with the example.
6. (B) Show that the map $B \to W$, $i \mapsto s_i$, is injective for $A_n$. Hint: the images in $S_{n+1}$ are distinct transpositions.
7. (C) For type $A_n$, let $w \in W$ map to $\sigma \in S_{n+1}$ under $s_i \mapsto (i\ \ i+1)$. Show that $\ell(w)$ is at least the number of inversions of $\sigma$, and deduce that a preimage of $i \mapsto n + 2 - i$ has length at least $n(n+1)/2$. Hint: right multiplication by an adjacent transposition changes the number of inversions by exactly one.

### Notes and history

Coxeter classified the finite groups generated by reflections in 1934 and studied their presentations, which now carry his name. Tits developed the abstract theory of Coxeter systems, and Bourbaki's treatise on Lie groups and Lie algebras made the theory standard. Matsumoto proved that two reduced words of the same element are related by braid relations. The Weyl groups of the semisimple Lie algebras of Killing and Cartan are the finite crystallographic Coxeter groups.

The Mathlib files are by these authors, read from their headers: `Coxeter.Matrix` by Newell Jensen and Mitchell Lee; `Coxeter.Basic` by Newell Jensen, Mitchell Lee and Óscar Álvarez; `Coxeter.Length` by Mitchell Lee; `Coxeter.Inversion` by Mitchell Lee and Óscar Álvarez.
