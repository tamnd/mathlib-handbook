# Combinatorics

This volume teaches the combinatorics that Mathlib contains, and its form in the library. Its objects are finite: graphs on a finite set of vertices, families of subsets of a finite set, finite sets of integers or of elements of a finite group, and the partitions and lattice paths that enumeration counts. Its methods are counting, induction on the size of an object, extremal choices, double counting and averaging. Each section gives the definitions, the theorems with complete proofs, examples that compute, and the names of Mathlib that state the same results.

Chapter 1 defines simple graphs as symmetric and irreflexive relations, with the neighbour sets, the degrees and the edge set. It proves the degree-sum formula and the handshake lemma, studies subgraphs, homomorphisms and isomorphisms, and the adjacency matrix. Its running example is the Petersen graph. Chapter 2 defines walks, trails, paths and cycles, connectivity and the connected components, the distance and the diameter. It proves that a tree on $n$ vertices has $n - 1$ edges, gives the equivalent definitions of a tree, and proves the theorem of Euler on closed trails. Chapter 3 defines colourings and the chromatic number, proves that a graph is bipartite if and only if it has no odd cycle, and studies cliques and the theorem of Turán on graphs with no clique of a given size.

Chapter 4 proves Hall's marriage theorem for finite families, and its form for infinite families with finite members, which Mathlib proves by compactness. It then studies matchings in graphs, vertex covers, the theorem of König and the theorem of Tutte on perfect matchings. Chapter 5 starts from the pigeonhole principle of Volume 3 and goes on to Ramsey theory. It proves the theorem of Ramsey for graphs and the value $R(3,3) = 6$ on paper, and then the theorems that Mathlib has: the Hales-Jewett theorem, the theorem of van der Waerden on arithmetic progressions, and the theorem of Hindman on finite sums. Chapter 6 is additive combinatorics. It studies sumsets, proves the Cauchy-Davenport theorem and the Erdős-Ginzburg-Ziv theorem, then the Ruzsa triangle inequality, the covering lemma and the Plünnecke-Ruzsa inequality, and ends with the regularity lemma of Szemerédi, the triangle removal lemma and the theorem of Roth on sets with no three-term arithmetic progression.

Chapter 7 studies families of finite sets. It defines the shadow and the colexicographic order, states and proves the Kruskal-Katona theorem, proves the LYM inequality and the theorem of Sperner on antichains, and then the Erdős-Ko-Rado theorem, the Harris-Kleitman inequality and the lemma of Sauer and Shelah on shattered sets. Chapter 8 is enumeration. It proves the formulas for the Catalan numbers with Dyck words and binary trees, computes them to $n = 10$, studies integer partitions, their generating function, the theorem of Glaisher and Euler on odd and distinct parts, and Young diagrams, and the pentagonal number theorem. It ends with the Stirling numbers, the Bell numbers, the derangements and the principle of inclusion and exclusion.

The volume needs Volume 3 on finite sets, finite types and big operators, Volume 4 on orders and lattices, and Volume 5 on groups. It uses the counting of Volume 3 throughout: the card of a finset, the binomial coefficients, the pigeonhole principle, double counting and inclusion and exclusion. It does not prove these again. Chapter 1 uses matrices from Volume 7 in one section, and Chapter 6 uses the integers modulo $p$ of Volume 12. The level goes from a first undergraduate course in Chapters 1 to 5 and 8 to a first graduate course in parts of Chapters 6 and 7. The standard references are Diestel, *Graph Theory*, Bollobás, *Modern Graph Theory*, Tao and Vu, *Additive Combinatorics*, and Stanley, *Enumerative Combinatorics*. Chapters 1 and 2 come first. Chapters 3, 4 and 5 need them and can be read in any order. Chapters 6, 7 and 8 need only Chapter 1 and Volume 3, except that the last section of Chapter 6 uses Chapter 3.

Mathlib writes a simple graph on a type `V` as a structure `SimpleGraph V` with a relation `Adj` that is symmetric and irreflexive. The type `V` can be infinite, so the library separates the general facts from the counting facts: a statement that counts edges or degrees asks for `Fintype V` or for `G.LocallyFinite`, and for decidable adjacency. Finite families are values of `Finset (Finset α)`, and most theorems of Chapter 7 are about such a family. Sumsets are the pointwise sums `s + t` of Volume 5, and the additive combinatorics of Chapter 6 is stated for `Finset` in a group. The text explains each of these choices where it appears.

The folder `Mathlib/Combinatorics` has more than this volume teaches. The volume leaves out matroids, designs and configurations, hypergraphs, digraphs, the ends of infinite graphs, the incidence algebra, compositions, tilings and the theory of valued constraint satisfaction problems. The modules of these topics have their home in this volume, and a later edition can add chapters on them. The quivers of `Mathlib/Combinatorics/Quiver` have their home in Volume 24. The volume has no Lean companion yet, so the sections show the Mathlib statements and give no Lean exercises.
