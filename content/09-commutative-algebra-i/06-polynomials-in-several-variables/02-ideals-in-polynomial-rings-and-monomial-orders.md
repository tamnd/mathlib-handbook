## Ideals in polynomial rings and monomial orders

### Overview

Let $k$ be a field and $A = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. A set of polynomials has a set of common zeros in $k^n$, and a set of points has an ideal of polynomials that vanish on it. The second half computes with ideals. In one variable, division decides whether $g \in (f)$. In several variables the monomials must first be ordered: a monomial order gives leading terms and a division algorithm with several divisors, and with Dickson's lemma it gives a second proof that every ideal of $A$ is finitely generated.

### Definitions

**Definition (Zero set and vanishing ideal).** For a set $S \subseteq A$ put $V(S) = \{ a \in k^n : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S \}$. For a set $X \subseteq k^n$ put $I(X) = \{ f \in A : f(a) = 0 \text{ for all } a \in X \}$. A set of the form $V(S)$ is an algebraic set.

The set $I(X)$ is an ideal, and $V(S) = V(J)$ for the ideal $J$ generated by $S$.

**Definition (Monomial order).** Identify the monomial $x^a$ with $a \in \mathbb{N}^n$. A monomial order is a total order $\le$ on $\mathbb{N}^n$ such that

1. $a \le b$ implies $a + c \le b + c$ for all $c$, and
2. $\le$ is a well-order: every nonempty set of monomials has a least element.

In the lexicographic order $a > b$ when the first nonzero entry of $a - b$ is positive. The degree lexicographic order compares $|a|$ first and breaks ties lexicographically.

**Definition (Leading term).** Fix a monomial order. For $f = \sum c_a x^a \ne 0$ the multidegree $\operatorname{md}(f)$ is the largest $a$ with $c_a \ne 0$. The leading coefficient is $\operatorname{lc}(f) = c_{\operatorname{md}(f)}$, the leading monomial is $\operatorname{lm}(f) = x^{\operatorname{md}(f)}$, and the leading term is $\operatorname{lt}(f) = \operatorname{lc}(f) \operatorname{lm}(f)$.

Over a field, property 1 gives $\operatorname{md}(fg) = \operatorname{md}(f) + \operatorname{md}(g)$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (Rules for $V$ and $I$).** For ideals $J, J'$ of $A$ and sets $X, Y \subseteq k^n$:

1. $J \subseteq J'$ implies $V(J') \subseteq V(J)$, and $X \subseteq Y$ implies $I(Y) \subseteq I(X)$.
2. $J \subseteq I(V(J))$ and $X \subseteq V(I(X))$.
3. $V(I(V(J))) = V(J)$.
4. $I(X)$ is a radical ideal.

*Proof.* (1) and (2) follow from the definitions. (3) Apply (2) to the set $V(J)$ for one inclusion, and (1) to $J \subseteq I(V(J))$ for the other. (4) If $f^m \in I(X)$, then $f(a)^m = 0$ for all $a \in X$, so $f(a) = 0$ in the field $k$. $\square$

**Theorem (Division algorithm).** Fix a monomial order, and let $f_1, \dots, f_s \in A$ be nonzero. Each $f \in A$ can be written
$$f = q_1 f_1 + \dots + q_s f_s + r$$
with $q_i, r \in A$, such that no monomial of $r$ is divisible by any $\operatorname{lm}(f_i)$, and $\operatorname{md}(q_i f_i) \le \operatorname{md}(f)$ whenever $q_i f_i \ne 0$.

*Proof.* The algorithm keeps the invariant
$$f = q_1 f_1 + \dots + q_s f_s + p + r. \tag{$*$}$$
It starts with $q_i = 0$, $r = 0$ and $p = f$. While $p \ne 0$, it does one step:

- If $\operatorname{lm}(f_i)$ divides $\operatorname{lm}(p)$ for some $i$, take the least such $i$, put $t = \operatorname{lt}(p) / \operatorname{lt}(f_i)$, a term, and replace $q_i$ by $q_i + t$ and $p$ by $p - t f_i$.
- Otherwise replace $r$ by $r + \operatorname{lt}(p)$ and $p$ by $p - \operatorname{lt}(p)$.

Both steps keep $(*)$. In the first step $\operatorname{lt}(t f_i) = \operatorname{lt}(p)$ cancels, and the other terms of $t f_i$ have monomials $c + b$ with $x^c$ the monomial of $t$ and $b < \operatorname{md}(f_i)$, so $c + b < \operatorname{md}(p)$ by property 1. So each step makes $p$ zero or lowers $\operatorname{md}(p)$. A strictly decreasing sequence in $\mathbb{N}^n$ is finite by property 2, as its set of members would have no least element otherwise. So the algorithm stops, with $p = 0$.

No $\operatorname{lm}(f_i)$ divides a term added to $r$. A term $t$ added to $q_i$ has $\operatorname{md}(t f_i) = \operatorname{md}(p) \le \operatorname{md}(f)$, since $\operatorname{md}(p)$ never increases. So $\operatorname{md}(q_i f_i) \le \operatorname{md}(f)$. $\square$

**Lemma (Monomial ideals).** Let $J$ be the ideal generated by a set $M$ of monomials. A polynomial $f$ lies in $J$ if and only if each monomial of $f$ is divisible by some element of $M$.

*Proof.* One direction is clear. Conversely let $f = \sum_j h_j x^{a_j}$ with $x^{a_j} \in M$. Expanding the $h_j$ shows that every monomial of $f$ is some $x^c x^{a_j}$. $\square$

**Theorem (Dickson's lemma).** Every set $M$ of monomials in $x_1, \dots, x_n$ contains a finite subset $M_0$ such that every element of $M$ is divisible by an element of $M_0$. So every monomial ideal is generated by finitely many monomials.

*Proof.* Divisibility of monomials is the componentwise order $\preceq$ on $\mathbb{N}^n$. First, every sequence $(a_j)$ in $\mathbb{N}^n$ has a subsequence that is increasing for $\preceq$. For $n = 1$, let $j_1$ be an index of the least value, $j_2 > j_1$ an index of the least value after $j_1$, and so on. For $n > 1$, take a subsequence increasing in the first $n - 1$ coordinates by induction, and then a subsequence of it increasing in the last coordinate.

Let $M_0$ be the set of minimal elements of $M$ for $\preceq$. Each element of $M$ lies above an element of $M_0$, because $\preceq$ is well-founded: a strictly decreasing chain decreases $|a|$. The set $M_0$ is finite. Otherwise it contains a sequence of distinct elements, which has an increasing subsequence, and then $a_{j_1} \prec a_{j_2}$ for two distinct minimal elements, a contradiction. The last claim follows from the lemma on monomial ideals. $\square$

**Corollary (Hilbert's basis theorem, by leading terms).** Every ideal $J$ of $A$ is finitely generated. More precisely, for each monomial order there are $g_1, \dots, g_t \in J$ such that every $\operatorname{lm}(g)$ with $0 \ne g \in J$ is divisible by some $\operatorname{lm}(g_i)$, and every such family generates $J$.

*Proof.* Let $J \ne 0$. Apply Dickson's lemma to the set $\{ \operatorname{lm}(g) : 0 \ne g \in J \}$, and let $g_1, \dots, g_t \in J$ have the leading monomials in the finite subset. Let $f \in J$ and divide: $f = \sum q_i g_i + r$. Then $r = f - \sum q_i g_i \in J$. If $r \ne 0$, then $\operatorname{lm}(r)$ is divisible by some $\operatorname{lm}(g_i)$, which the division forbids. So $r = 0$ and $f \in (g_1, \dots, g_t)$. $\square$

A family as in the corollary is a Gröbner basis of $J$, and for it $f \in J$ if and only if the remainder is $0$. Chapter 3, Section 2 proves the basis theorem over every Noetherian ring (`MvPolynomial.isNoetherianRing`).

### Examples

**Example (The union of two lines).** Let $J = (xy) \subseteq \mathbb{C}[x, y]$, so $V(J)$ is the union of the axes. In $B = \mathbb{C}[x, y]/(xy)$, $\bar x \bar y = 0$ with $\bar x, \bar y \ne 0$, so $B$ has zero divisors. The elements $x, y$ are prime, since the quotients $\mathbb{C}[y]$ and $\mathbb{C}[x]$ are domains. As non-associate primes, they give $(xy) = (x) \cap (y)$, and a prime over $(xy)$ contains $x$ or $y$: the minimal primes are $(x)$ and $(y)$. If $f^m \in (xy)$, then the primes $x$ and $y$ divide $f$, so $xy \mid f$. So $(xy)$ is radical, and $B$ has nilradical $0$.

**Example (A division).** Use the lexicographic order with $x > y$. Divide $f = x^2 y + x y^2 + y^2$ by $f_1 = xy - 1$ and $f_2 = y^2 - 1$. The steps:

- $xy \mid x^2 y$: $q_1 = x$, $p = x y^2 + x + y^2$.
- $xy \mid x y^2$: $q_1 = x + y$, $p = x + y^2 + y$.
- $x$ goes to the remainder: $r = x$, $p = y^2 + y$.
- $y^2 \mid y^2$: $q_2 = 1$, $p = y + 1$.
- $y$ and $1$ go to the remainder: $r = x + y + 1$, $p = 0$.

Expanding $(x + y)(xy - 1) + (y^2 - 1) + x + y + 1$ gives $f$ back.

**Counterexample (The remainder is not a test without a Gröbner basis).** With the same order, divide $f = x y^2 - x$ by $f_1 = xy + 1$ and $f_2 = y^2 - 1$. The first step gives $q_1 = y$ and $p = -x - y$, and neither $x$ nor $y$ is divisible by $xy$ or $y^2$, so $r = -x - y \ne 0$. But $f = x(y^2 - 1) \in (f_1, f_2)$. In the order $f_2, f_1$ the division gives $q_2 = x$ and $r = 0$. So the remainder depends on the order of the divisors.

### The library around it

Dickson's lemma is the instance `Finsupp.wellQuasiOrderedLE` of Volume 3: for finite `σ` the order on `σ →₀ ℕ` is a well quasi-order. A common error of a learner: `m.degree f` is a monomial `σ →₀ ℕ`, not a number.

### Exercises

1. (A) Give the leading term of $3x^2 y z + x^3 - 2 y^4 + x y^2 z^2$ in the lexicographic and the degree lexicographic order with $x > y > z$.
2. (A) Show that for one divisor $g$ the remainder is $0$ exactly when $f \in (g)$, and decide whether $x^3 - y \in (x - y^2)$ in the lexicographic order with $x > y$. Hint: $\operatorname{md}(hg) = \operatorname{md}(h) + \operatorname{md}(g)$.
3. (B) Show that a total order on $\mathbb{N}^n$ with property 1 and $0 \le a$ for all $a$ is a well-order. Hint: use Dickson's lemma.
4. (B) Show that $I(\mathbb{Z}^2) = 0$ in $\mathbb{C}[x, y]$, and that $I(\{(t, t^2) : t \in \mathbb{C}\}) = (y - x^2)$. Hint: for the second, divide by $y - x^2$ as a monic polynomial in $y$.
5. (C) Show that $\{xy + 1, y^2 - 1\}$ is not a Gröbner basis of $J = (xy + 1, y^2 - 1)$ in the lexicographic order, and that $\{x + y, y^2 - 1\}$ is one. Hint: $x + y = y f_1 - x f_2$. A remainder for $x + y$ and $y^2 - 1$ has the form $a + by$; map it to $k[y]$ by $x \mapsto -y$.

### Notes and history

Buchberger introduced Gröbner bases and the algorithm that computes them in his thesis of 1965. The basis theorem is due to Hilbert (1890), and Dickson's lemma appears in a paper of Dickson of 1913.
