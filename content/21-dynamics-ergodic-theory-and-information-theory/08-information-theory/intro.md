# Information theory

Information theory measures information by counting. A message from a source of $n$ equally likely symbols carries $\log n$ units of information, a channel with noise destroys some of it, and a code adds redundancy to recover it. This chapter presents the three parts of the subject that Mathlib covers: codes of variable length, codes for the correction of errors, and the divergence between two probability laws.

Section 1 defines prefix-free and uniquely decodable codes and proves the inequalities of Kraft and McMillan, with the source coding bounds for the mean length of a code on paper. Section 2 defines the Hamming distance and the Hamming weight, explains the type synonym `Hamming` that carries the metric in Mathlib, and proves the bounds for error detection and correction, with the Hamming code of length $7$ as the main example. Section 3 defines the Kullback-Leibler divergence of two finite measures, with values in $[0, \infty]$ and a mass term, proves Gibbs' inequality and the case of equality, and computes the divergence of two Gaussian laws. Section 4 proves the data processing inequality, for a sub-σ-algebra, a measurable map and a Markov kernel, and the chain rule for compositions with kernels, which gives the additivity on products.

Sections 1 and 2 need only finite sets and the metric spaces of Volume 13. Sections 3 and 4 need the Radon-Nikodym theorem of Volume 18 and the conditional expectation and kernels of Volume 20. The index of this edition has no declaration for the source coding theorem, the channel coding theorem or the mutual information.
