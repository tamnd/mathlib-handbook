# Projective spaces

A projective space is the set of lines through the origin of a vector space. Each line is one point, so a nonzero vector $v$ and its multiples $a v$ give the same point $[v]$, and the space $\mathbb{P}^n(K)$ of dimension $n$ is built from $K^{n+1}$. The construction adds points at infinity to the affine space $K^n$, one for each direction, and in the projective plane any two distinct lines meet. Projective spaces are the setting of classical projective geometry and of algebraic geometry, and their symmetry groups $\mathrm{PGL}_n(K)$ and $\mathrm{PSL}_n(K)$ are among the basic examples of finite groups.

Section 1 defines the projectivization $\mathbb{P}(V)$ as a quotient of $V \setminus \{0\}$ by the scalars, describes its points by representatives and by one-dimensional subspaces, and shows how injective linear maps induce maps of projective spaces. It defines independent families of points and counts the points over a finite field: $\mathbb{P}^n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ has $(q^{n+1} - 1)/(q - 1)$ points, so the Fano plane $\mathbb{P}^2(\mathbb{F}_2)$ has $7$. Section 2 defines projective subspaces and matches them with the submodules of $V$, proves that two points determine a line and that two lines of a plane meet, and computes these with the cross product in $K^3$. It then studies the action of the linear group: $\mathrm{GL}(V)$ acts $2$-transitively, the scalars form the kernel, and $\mathrm{PSL}_2(F)$ is simple when $F$ has at least $4$ elements, while $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{F}_2)$ and $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{F}_3)$ are not.

The chapter uses the bases and dimensions of Volume 7, Chapter 2, the matrices of Volume 7, Chapter 3, and the group actions of Volume 5, Chapter 6. It does not depend on the earlier chapters of this volume.
