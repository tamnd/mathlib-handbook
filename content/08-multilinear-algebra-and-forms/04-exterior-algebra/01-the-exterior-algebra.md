## The exterior algebra

### Overview

The exterior algebra $\Lambda(M)$ is the most general associative algebra that contains $M$ and in which every vector squares to zero. In it the product $x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge x_n$ is an alternating function of the vectors, like the determinant of Volume 7, Chapter 4.

This section builds $\Lambda(M)$ as a quotient of the tensor algebra of Chapter 3, Section 2. It proves the universal property, the rule of anticommutation, the grading by degree, and the theorem on bases. Here $R$ is a commutative ring.

### Definitions

**Definition (Exterior algebra).** Let $J \subseteq T(M)$ be the two-sided ideal generated by the elements $x \otimes x$ for $x \in M$. The exterior algebra is $\Lambda(M) = T(M)/J$. The map $\iota : M \to \Lambda(M)$ is $\iota$ of $T(M)$ followed by the quotient map. The product in $\Lambda(M)$ is written $u \wedge v$, and for vectors $\iota(x) \wedge \iota(y)$ is written $x \wedge y$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Proposition (Squares and anticommutation).** For all $x, y \in M$, $x \wedge x = 0$ and $x \wedge y = -\, y \wedge x$. So $\iota_k$ is alternating: it is zero when two arguments are equal, and it changes sign when two arguments are swapped.

*Proof.* The first equation holds because $x \otimes x \in J$. For the second, $0 = (x + y) \wedge (x + y) = x \wedge x + x \wedge y + y \wedge x + y \wedge y = x \wedge y + y \wedge x$. Now let $x_i = x_j$ with $i < j$. Move $x_j$ to the left by swaps of neighbours until it stands next to $x_i$. Each swap changes only the sign, and then the product contains the factor $x_i \wedge x_i = 0$. A swap of two arguments is a product of an odd number of swaps of neighbours, so it changes the sign. $\square$

**Theorem (Universal property).** Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra and $f : M \to A$ a linear map with $f(x)^2 = 0$ for all $x$. There is a unique algebra homomorphism $\bar f : \Lambda(M) \to A$ with $\bar f \circ \iota = f$.

*Proof.* Let $g : T(M) \to A$ be the lift of $f$ (Chapter 3, Section 2). Then $g(x \otimes x) = f(x)^2 = 0$, so the generators of $J$ lie in the two-sided ideal $\ker g$, and $J \subseteq \ker g$. So $g$ factors through the quotient as $\bar f$. The vectors $\iota(x)$ generate $\Lambda(M)$, and this gives uniqueness. $\square$

**Theorem (Grading).** $\Lambda(M) = \bigoplus_{k \ge 0} \Lambda^k(M)$, and $\Lambda^j(M) \wedge \Lambda^k(M) \subseteq \Lambda^{j+k}(M)$. For $u \in \Lambda^j(M)$ and $v \in \Lambda^k(M)$, $u \wedge v = (-1)^{jk} \, v \wedge u$.

*Proof.* The generators $x \otimes x$ of $J$ lie in $M^{\otimes 2}$. So $J$ is spanned by the products $a \otimes (x \otimes x) \otimes b$ with $a, b$ pure tensors, and each such product is homogeneous. Hence $J = \bigoplus_n (J \cap M^{\otimes n})$, and the quotient is the direct sum of the images of the $M^{\otimes n}$, which are the $\Lambda^n(M)$. The inclusion $\Lambda^j \wedge \Lambda^k \subseteq \Lambda^{j+k}$ holds on products of vectors. For the sign it is enough to take $u = x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge x_j$ and $v = y_1 \wedge \dots \wedge y_k$. Move each $y_l$ in turn to the left past the $j$ vectors $x_i$. This takes $jk$ swaps of neighbours, and each one gives a factor $-1$. $\square$

Fix a total order on an index set $I$. For a finite set $S = \{s_1 < \dots < s_k\} \subseteq I$ and a family $(e_i)_{i \in I}$, put $e_S = e_{s_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{s_k}$, and $e_\emptyset = 1$. For two finite sets $S, T$ let $\varepsilon(S, T) = (-1)^{N}$, where $N$ is the number of pairs $(s, t) \in S \times T$ with $s > t$.

**Theorem (Basis of the exterior algebra).** Let $(e_i)_{i \in I}$ be a basis of $M$. The elements $e_S$, for the finite subsets $S \subseteq I$, form a basis of $\Lambda(M)$. The $e_S$ with $|S| = k$ form a basis of $\Lambda^k(M)$. So for $M$ free of rank $n$, $\Lambda^k(M)$ has rank $\binom{n}{k}$, $\Lambda^k(M) = 0$ for $k > n$, and $\Lambda(M)$ has rank $2^n$. The products are $e_S \wedge e_T = \varepsilon(S, T)\, e_{S \cup T}$ for disjoint $S, T$, and $e_S \wedge e_T = 0$ otherwise.

*Proof.* First the product rule. If $S \cap T \neq \emptyset$, the product has a repeated factor and is zero. If $S, T$ are disjoint, sort the factors of $e_S \wedge e_T$ by swaps of neighbours. Each pair $(s, t)$ with $s > t$ must change places once, so the sign is $\varepsilon(S, T)$.

The $e_S$ span. By multilinearity, a product $x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge x_k$ is a combination of products $e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_k}$. By the proposition, such a product is $0$ if two indices are equal, and is $\pm e_S$ for $S = \{i_1, \dots, i_k\}$ otherwise.

For independence, build a model. Let $E$ be the free module with basis $(b_S)$, indexed by the finite subsets of $I$. Define a bilinear product by $b_S b_T = \varepsilon(S, T) b_{S \cup T}$ if $S \cap T = \emptyset$ and $b_S b_T = 0$ otherwise. It is associative. Both $(b_S b_T) b_U$ and $b_S (b_T b_U)$ are zero unless the three sets are disjoint. For disjoint sets, $\varepsilon$ is multiplicative in each argument, for example $\varepsilon(S \cup T, U) = \varepsilon(S, U) \varepsilon(T, U)$. So both products equal $\varepsilon(S, T) \varepsilon(S, U) \varepsilon(T, U) \, b_{S \cup T \cup U}$. So $E$ is an $R$-algebra with unit $b_\emptyset$. Let $f : M \to E$ be linear with $f(e_i) = b_{\{i\}}$. For $x = \sum_i x_i e_i$,
$$f(x)^2 = \sum_i x_i^2 \, b_{\{i\}} b_{\{i\}} + \sum_{i < j} x_i x_j \bigl(b_{\{i\}} b_{\{j\}} + b_{\{j\}} b_{\{i\}}\bigr) = 0 + \sum_{i < j} x_i x_j (b_{\{i,j\}} - b_{\{i,j\}}) = 0.$$
By the universal property, $f$ extends to $\bar f : \Lambda(M) \to E$. By the product rule in $E$, $\bar f(e_S) = b_S$. The $b_S$ are independent, so the $e_S$ are independent. The statements on $\Lambda^k(M)$ follow, as $e_S \in \Lambda^{|S|}(M)$ and the grading is direct, and there are $\binom{n}{k}$ subsets of size $k$ and $2^n$ subsets in all. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Two vectors in the plane).** In $\Lambda(\mathbb{Q}^2)$ with basis $1, e_1, e_2, e_1 \wedge e_2$,
$$(a e_1 + b e_2) \wedge (c e_1 + d e_2) = ad\, e_1 \wedge e_2 + bc\, e_2 \wedge e_1 = (ad - bc)\, e_1 \wedge e_2.$$
The coefficient is the determinant of the matrix with the columns $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$.

**Example (A computation in three variables).** In $\Lambda(\mathbb{Q}^3)$, write $e_{ij} = e_i \wedge e_j$. Then $(e_1 + 2 e_2) \wedge (e_2 - e_3) = e_{12} - e_{13} - 2 e_{23}$, since $e_2 \wedge e_2 = 0$. Multiply by $e_1$ on the right: $e_{12} \wedge e_1 = 0$, $e_{13} \wedge e_1 = 0$ and $e_{23} \wedge e_1 = \varepsilon(\{2,3\}, \{1\}) e_{123} = e_{123}$. So the product is $-2 e_{123}$.

**Counterexample (Anticommutation does not give squares zero).** Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, let $A = \mathbb{F}_2[X]$ and $f : \mathbb{F}_2 \to A$ with $f(1) = X$. Then $f(x) f(y) = - f(y) f(x)$ for all $x, y$, since $A$ is commutative and $-1 = 1$. But $f(1)^2 = X^2 \neq 0$, so $f$ does not extend to $\Lambda(\mathbb{F}_2)$. When $2$ is a unit, $2 f(x)^2 = 0$ gives $f(x)^2 = 0$, and the two conditions agree.

The file `OfAlternating` gives `ExteriorAlgebra.liftAlternating`, a linear map $\Lambda(M) \to N$ built from alternating maps $M^k \to N$. The map `TensorAlgebra.toExterior` is the quotient map of this section.

### Exercises

1. (A) In $\Lambda(\mathbb{Q}^3)$ compute $(e_1 - e_3) \wedge (2 e_1 + e_2) \wedge (e_2 + e_3)$. Hint: the answer is a multiple of $e_{123}$.
2. (A) Show that $x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge x_k = 0$ when $x_1, \dots, x_k$ are linearly dependent over a field. Hint: write one vector as a combination of the others.
3. (A) Show that the elements of even degree form a commutative subalgebra of $\Lambda(M)$. Hint: use the sign rule.
4. (B) Show that $\omega \in \Lambda^2(\mathbb{Q}^4)$ is a product of two vectors exactly when $\omega \wedge \omega = 0$, and that $e_{12} + e_{34}$ is not. Hint: write $\omega \wedge \omega$ in the coefficients $a_{ij}$.
5. (B) Over a field, let $x_1, \dots, x_k$ be independent. Show that $x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge x_k \neq 0$. Hint: extend to a basis and use the theorem on bases.
6. (B) Let $x \in M$. Show that left multiplication by $x$ on $\Lambda(M)$ squares to zero, and that for a field and $x \neq 0$ its kernel equals its image. Hint: choose a basis with $e_1 = x$.
7. (C) Show that the center of $\Lambda(\mathbb{Q}^n)$ is the span of the even part and of $\Lambda^n$ when $n$ is odd, and the even part when $n$ is even. Hint: compute $e_i \wedge e_S - e_S \wedge e_i$.

### Notes and history

Grassmann introduced the exterior product in his Ausdehnungslehre of 1844. The exterior algebra became a standard tool of differential geometry through the differential forms of Cartan.
