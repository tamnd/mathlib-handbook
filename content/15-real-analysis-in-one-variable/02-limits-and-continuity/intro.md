# Limits and continuity

Chapter 1 studied limits of sequences. This chapter studies limits of functions of a real variable and the continuous functions, and it proves the three theorems about continuous functions on intervals on which the rest of the volume depends: the intermediate value theorem, the extreme value theorem and the Heine-Cantor theorem.

Section 1 defines the limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ with $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ and translates it into the language of Mathlib: a statement `Tendsto f (𝓝[≠] a) (𝓝 L)` with the punctured neighbourhood filter. It treats one-sided limits and limits at infinity in the same way, proves the sequential characterization, and proves the algebra of limits, the squeeze theorem and the rule for compositions. Section 2 defines continuity and the four forms `ContinuousAt`, `ContinuousWithinAt`, `ContinuousOn` and `Continuous` of Mathlib. It proves the intermediate value theorem from the least upper bound, and it deduces the existence of $n$-th roots, the existence of a root of a polynomial of odd degree, the strict monotonicity of a continuous injective function on an interval and the continuity of its inverse. The square root `Real.sqrt` of Mathlib is the main example, with its junk value $0$ on negative numbers. Section 3 proves the extreme value theorem and the Heine-Cantor theorem with the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, shows by counterexamples that both need a closed bounded interval, and treats Lipschitz functions and the oscillation of a function at a point.

The chapter needs the real numbers of Volume 2 and the limits of sequences of Chapter 1, in particular the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem and the squeeze theorem. It uses filters, neighbourhoods, compactness and uniform structures of Volume 13 through the translations that it states, and a reader can follow the mathematics without them.

Chapter 3 uses the limits of this chapter to define the derivative, and it uses the extreme value theorem in the proof of Rolle's theorem. Chapter 5 uses the theorem on inverse functions to define the logarithm as the inverse of the exponential function, and Chapter 6 uses the intermediate value theorem to find a zero of the cosine in $[1, 2]$, and $\pi$ is twice that zero.
