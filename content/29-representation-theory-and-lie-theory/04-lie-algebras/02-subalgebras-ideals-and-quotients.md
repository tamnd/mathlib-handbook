## Subalgebras, ideals and quotients

### Overview

A Lie algebra has substructures of two kinds. A subalgebra is a submodule that is closed under the bracket. An ideal is a submodule $I$ with $[x, i] \in I$ for all $x$ in the full algebra, and because the bracket is antisymmetric, there is no difference between left, right and two-sided ideals. Ideals are exactly the kernels of morphisms, and the quotient $L / I$ is again a Lie algebra.

The bracket $[I, J]$ of two ideals is an ideal, and the derived series and the lower central series of the next chapter are built from it. The normalizer of a subalgebra $H$ is the largest subalgebra in which $H$ is an ideal; it controls Engel's theorem and the definition of a Cartan subalgebra. This section gives these constructions, the first isomorphism theorem, products and direct sums, and the examples in $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and $\mathfrak{gl}_n$.

In the whole section $R$ is a commutative ring, $L$ and $L'$ are Lie algebras over $R$, and $M$ is a Lie module over $L$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Lie subalgebra).** A Lie subalgebra of $L$ is an $R$-submodule $H \subseteq L$ with $[x, y] \in H$ for all $x, y \in H$. With the restricted bracket, $H$ is a Lie algebra, and the inclusion $H \to L$ is a morphism. For a subset $S$, the Lie span is the smallest subalgebra that contains $S$.

**Definition (Lie submodule and Lie ideal).** A Lie submodule of $M$ is an $R$-submodule $N \subseteq M$ with $[x, n] \in N$ for all $x \in L$ and $n \in N$. A Lie ideal of $L$ is a Lie submodule of $L$ as a module over itself, so $[x, i] \in I$ for all $x \in L$ and $i \in I$.

An ideal is a subalgebra.

**Definition (Bracket of ideals).** For an ideal $I$ of $L$ and a Lie submodule $N$ of $M$, the submodule $[I, N]$ is the Lie span of the set $\{[x, n] : x \in I, n \in N\}$. For two ideals $I, J$ of $L$, $[I, J]$ is an ideal of $L$. The derived algebra is $[L, L]$.

**Definition (Kernel, range and ideal range).** For a morphism $f : L \to L'$, the kernel $\ker f = \{x : f(x) = 0\}$ is an ideal of $L$, and the range $f(L)$ is a subalgebra of $L'$. The ideal range is the ideal of $L'$ generated by $f(L)$. The morphism $f$ is an ideal morphism if the ideal range equals the range, that is, if $f(L)$ is an ideal.

**Definition (Quotient).** For an ideal $I$ of $L$, the quotient $L / I$ is the quotient module with the bracket $[x + I, y + I] = [x, y] + I$. The map $\pi : L \to L / I$, $x \mapsto x + I$, is the quotient map. For a Lie submodule $N$ of $M$, the quotient $M / N$ is a Lie module over $L$ in the same way.

**Definition (Normalizer).** For a subalgebra $H$ of $L$, the normalizer is
$$N_L(H) = \{x \in L : [x, h] \in H \text{ for all } h \in H\}.$$
For a Lie submodule $N$ of $M$, the normalizer is the submodule $\{m \in M : [x, m] \in N \text{ for all } x \in L\}$, and the idealizer is the ideal $\{x \in L : [x, m] \in N \text{ for all } m \in M\}$. A subalgebra is self-normalizing if $N_L(H) = H$.

**Definition (Product and direct sum).** The product $L_1 \times L_2$ is the product module with the bracket $[(x_1, x_2), (y_1, y_2)] = ([x_1, y_1], [x_2, y_2])$. For a family $(L_i)_{i \in \iota}$, the direct sum $\bigoplus_i L_i$ is the direct sum of modules with the bracket on each component.

### Theorems and proofs

**Proposition (Left and right ideals agree).** If $I$ is an ideal of $L$, $x \in L$ and $i \in I$, then $[i, x] \in I$.

*Proof.* By antisymmetry $[i, x] = -[x, i]$. The element $[x, i]$ lies in $I$, and $I$ is a submodule, so $-[x, i] \in I$. $\square$

**Proposition (The bracket of ideals).** Let $I, J$ be ideals of $L$. The $R$-span of the brackets $[x, y]$ with $x \in I$ and $y \in J$ is an ideal, so it equals $[I, J]$. Also $[I, J] = [J, I]$ and $[I, J] \subseteq I \cap J$.

*Proof.* Let $S$ be the $R$-span of the brackets. For $z \in L$, $x \in I$ and $y \in J$, the Leibniz rule gives
$$[z, [x, y]] = [[z, x], y] + [x, [z, y]].$$
Here $[z, x] \in I$ and $[z, y] \in J$, so both terms lie in $S$. The map $[z, -]$ is linear, so $[z, S] \subseteq S$, and $S$ is an ideal. Each ideal that contains the brackets contains $S$, so $S = [I, J]$. The generators of $[J, I]$ are $[y, x] = -[x, y]$, so the two spans agree. Finally $[x, y] \in I$ because $I$ is an ideal, and $[x, y] \in J$ because $J$ is an ideal, so each generator lies in $I \cap J$. $\square$

**Proposition (The quotient is a Lie algebra).** For an ideal $I$ of $L$, the bracket on $L / I$ is well defined and makes $L / I$ a Lie algebra. The quotient map $\pi$ is a surjective morphism with kernel $I$.

*Proof.* Let $x' = x + i$ and $y' = y + j$ with $i, j \in I$. Then
$$[x', y'] = [x, y] + [x, j] + [i, y] + [i, j],$$
and the last three terms lie in $I$ by the proposition on left and right ideals. So the class of $[x', y']$ is the class of $[x, y]$. The bracket on $L / I$ is bilinear, alternating and satisfies the Leibniz rule, because each identity holds for representatives in $L$ and $\pi$ is linear. By definition $\pi([x, y]) = [\pi x, \pi y]$, so $\pi$ is a morphism; it is surjective, and $\pi(x) = 0$ if and only if $x \in I$. $\square$

**Theorem (First isomorphism theorem).** Let $f : L \to L'$ be a morphism. Then $\ker f$ is an ideal, $f(L)$ is a subalgebra, and $f$ induces an isomorphism of Lie algebras $L / \ker f \cong f(L)$.

*Proof.* If $f(i) = 0$, then $f([x, i]) = [f(x), 0] = 0$, so $\ker f$ is an ideal. The set $f(L)$ is a submodule, and $[f(x), f(y)] = f([x, y])$, so it is a subalgebra. The first isomorphism theorem for modules gives a linear bijection $\bar f : L / \ker f \to f(L)$ with $\bar f(x + \ker f) = f(x)$. It preserves the bracket:
$$\bar f([x, y] + \ker f) = f([x, y]) = [f(x), f(y)] = [\bar f(x + \ker f), \bar f(y + \ker f)].$$
A bijective morphism has a linear inverse, and the inverse also preserves the bracket, so $\bar f$ is an isomorphism. $\square$

**Proposition (Images of ideals).** Let $f : L \to L'$ be a surjective morphism and $I$ an ideal of $L$. Then $f(I)$ is an ideal of $L'$. For two ideals $I, J$ of $L$, $f([I, J]) = [f(I), f(J)]$.

*Proof.* The set $f(I)$ is a submodule. Each $z \in L'$ is $f(y)$ for some $y \in L$, so $[z, f(i)] = f([y, i]) \in f(I)$. For the second claim, $f([I, J])$ is the image of the span of the brackets $[x, y]$, so it is the span of the elements $f([x, y]) = [f(x), f(y)]$. These are the generators of $[f(I), f(J)]$, so the two ideals agree by the proposition on the bracket of ideals. $\square$

Without surjectivity both claims fail: the counterexamples follow below.

**Proposition (The normalizer).** Let $H$ be a subalgebra of $L$. Then $N_L(H)$ is a subalgebra that contains $H$, and $H$ is an ideal of $N_L(H)$. If $K$ is a subalgebra with $H \subseteq K$ and $H$ is an ideal of $K$, then $K \subseteq N_L(H)$.

*Proof.* The set $N_L(H)$ is a submodule, and it contains $H$ because $H$ is closed under the bracket. Let $x, y \in N_L(H)$ and $h \in H$. The form of the Jacobi identity in the previous section gives
$$[[x, y], h] = [x, [y, h]] - [y, [x, h]].$$
Here $[y, h] \in H$, so $[x, [y, h]] \in H$; the same holds for the second term, so $[x, y] \in N_L(H)$. By definition $[x, h] \in H$ for all $x \in N_L(H)$, so $H$ is an ideal of $N_L(H)$. If $H$ is an ideal of $K$, then each $x \in K$ satisfies $[x, h] \in H$ for all $h \in H$, so $x \in N_L(H)$. $\square$

**Proposition (Self-normalizing subalgebras).** The quotient $L / H$ of modules is a module over $H$ by $[h, x + H] = [h, x] + H$. The subalgebra $H$ is self-normalizing if and only if this module has no nonzero invariant: $(L / H)^H = 0$.

*Proof.* The action is well defined because $[h, H] \subseteq H$. The class $x + H$ is invariant if and only if $[h, x] \in H$ for all $h \in H$, that is, if and only if $x \in N_L(H)$. So $(L / H)^H = N_L(H) / H$, and this is $0$ if and only if $N_L(H) = H$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (A Borel subalgebra of sl2).** Over a field $K$ of characteristic not $2$, let $e, h, f$ be the basis of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(K)$ with $[h, e] = 2e$, $[h, f] = -2f$ and $[e, f] = h$. The span $\mathfrak{b} = K h + K e$ is a subalgebra, because $[h, e] = 2e \in \mathfrak{b}$. The line $K e$ is an ideal of $\mathfrak{b}$, since $[h, e] = 2e$ and $[e, e] = 0$, and $\mathfrak{b} / K e$ is one-dimensional, so it is abelian. The line $K e$ is not an ideal of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, because $[f, e] = -h$. The subalgebra $\mathfrak{b}$ is self-normalizing: for $x = a e + b h + c f$,
$$[x, h] = -2a e + 2c f,$$
and this lies in $\mathfrak{b}$ only if $2c = 0$, that is, $c = 0$. So $N(\mathfrak{b}) = \mathfrak{b}$.

**Example (sl2 is simple).** Over a field $K$ of characteristic not $2$, the only ideals of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(K)$ are $0$ and $\mathfrak{sl}_2(K)$. Let $I \neq 0$ be an ideal and $x = a e + b h + c f \in I$ nonzero. If $c \neq 0$, then
$$[e, [e, x]] = [e, -2b e + c h] = -2c e,$$
so $e \in I$. If $c = 0$ and $b \neq 0$, then $[e, x] = -2b e$, so $e \in I$. If $b = c = 0$, then $x = a e$ with $a \neq 0$, and again $e \in I$. In each case $e \in I$, so $h = [e, f] \in I$ and $f = -\tfrac12 [h, f] \in I$. So $I = \mathfrak{sl}_2(K)$.

**Example (The trace and the quotient of gl_n).** The trace $\operatorname{tr} : \mathfrak{gl}_n(R) \to R$, with $R$ abelian, is a morphism, because $\operatorname{tr}[x, y] = 0 = [\operatorname{tr} x, \operatorname{tr} y]$. For $n \geq 1$ it is surjective, since $\operatorname{tr}(r E_{11}) = r$, and its kernel is $\mathfrak{sl}_n(R)$. The first isomorphism theorem gives $\mathfrak{gl}_n(R) / \mathfrak{sl}_n(R) \cong R$. So $[\mathfrak{gl}_n, \mathfrak{gl}_n] \subseteq \mathfrak{sl}_n$.

**Example (gl2 as a product).** Suppose that $2$ is invertible in $R$. The map $\mathfrak{gl}_2(R) \to \mathfrak{sl}_2(R) \times R$,
$$x \mapsto \left(x - \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}(x) I, \ \operatorname{tr} x\right),$$
is a linear bijection with inverse $(y, t) \mapsto y + \tfrac12 t I$. It is a morphism: scalar matrices are central, so the first component of the image of $[x, y]$ is $[x, y] = [x - \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}(x) I, y - \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}(y) I]$, and the second is $\operatorname{tr}[x, y] = 0$. So $\mathfrak{gl}_2(R) \cong \mathfrak{sl}_2(R) \times R$.

**Counterexample (Images under a morphism that is not surjective).** Let $\iota : \mathfrak{b} \to \mathfrak{sl}_2(K)$ be the inclusion of the Borel subalgebra. The ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ of $\mathfrak{b}$ has image $\mathfrak{b}$, which is not an ideal of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, because $[f, h] = 2f \notin \mathfrak{b}$. So $\iota$ is not an ideal morphism, and its ideal range is the ideal generated by $\mathfrak{b}$, which is $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ by the simplicity example. For the line $K h$ and its inclusion $j$, the ideal generated by $j([K h, K h]) = 0$ is $0$, but the ideal generated by $K h$ is $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and $[\mathfrak{sl}_2, \mathfrak{sl}_2] \neq 0$.

Common errors of a learner: The first error is to treat a `LieIdeal R L` as a `LieSubalgebra R L`; they are different types, and the coercion `I.toLieSubalgebra` (written `(I : LieSubalgebra R L)`) is needed, for example in `LieHom.IsIdealMorphism`. The second error is to read `LieIdeal.map f I` as the image set; it is the ideal generated by the image, and the two agree only when `f` is surjective. The third error is to forget that the bracket `⁅I, J⁆` of ideals is a Lie span, so a proof of `⁅I, J⁆ ≤ K` goes through `LieSubmodule.lie_le_iff` and not through membership of an arbitrary element.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that the intersection of two ideals and the sum $I + J$ of two ideals are ideals, and that $I + J$ is the smallest ideal that contains $I$ and $J$. Hint: for the sum, $[x, i + j] = [x, i] + [x, j]$.
2. (A) Let $\mathfrak{n} \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}_n(K)$ be the strictly upper triangular matrices and $\mathfrak{t}$ the upper triangular matrices. Show that $\mathfrak{n}$ is an ideal of $\mathfrak{t}$ and that $\mathfrak{t} / \mathfrak{n}$ is abelian. Hint: the diagonal of a product of upper triangular matrices is the product of the diagonals.
3. (A) Show that the image of the center under a surjective morphism lies in the center, and give an example where it is smaller than the center of the target. Hint: use the quotient map of the two-dimensional algebra with $[x, y] = y$ onto $L / K y$.
4. (B) Prove the second isomorphism theorem: for ideals $I, J$ of $L$, $(I + J) / J \cong I / (I \cap J)$ as Lie algebras. Hint: apply the first isomorphism theorem to $I \to (I + J) / J$.
5. (B) Over a field of characteristic not $2$, compute the normalizer of the line $K h$ in $\mathfrak{sl}_2$, and show that $K h$ is self-normalizing. Hint: $[a e + b h + c f, h] = -2a e + 2c f$.
6. (C) Let $L = L_1 \times L_2$ where $L_1$ and $L_2$ are simple Lie algebras over a field, and let $I$ be an ideal of $L$. Show that $I$ is $0$, $L_1 \times 0$, $0 \times L_2$ or $L$. Hint: the projection of $I$ to $L_1$ is an ideal; if it is nonzero, then $[L_1 \times 0, I] = L_1 \times 0$ because $[L_1, L_1] = L_1$.

### Notes and history

Killing and Cartan used ideals in the classification of simple Lie algebras, and the simple algebras are those without proper nonzero ideals. The language of subalgebras, ideals and quotients modelled on rings became standard with the algebraic treatments of the twentieth century. The normalizer and the condition $N(H) = H$ enter the definition of a Cartan subalgebra, which is the subject of a later chapter.

The Mathlib files are by these authors, read from their headers: `Subalgebra`, `Submodule`, `Ideal`, `IdealOperations`, `Quotient`, `Normalizer` and `DirectSum` (all under `Mathlib.Algebra.Lie`) by Oliver Nash, and `Prod` by Leonid Ryvkin.
