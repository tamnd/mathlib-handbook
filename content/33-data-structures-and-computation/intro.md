# Data structures and computation

A proof assistant is also a programming language. Each list, map or stream in Mathlib is a value that Lean can compute, and each theorem about it is a statement about a program. This volume studies the data structures of Mathlib and the facts that make them useful in proofs. A student who has written a sorting function in some language knows that the function works on the examples that the student tried. This volume shows how to prove that the function works on all inputs, and how Mathlib states such facts so that other proofs can use them.

Chapter 1 treats lists: the inductive type, the basic operations and their lemmas, the induction principles, and the relations of sublist, prefix, suffix and infix. Chapter 2 studies lists up to order. It treats lists without duplicates, the permutation relation, sorted lists with insertion sort and merge sort, rotations and the type of cycles. Chapter 3 gives finite maps: association lists, the quotient type Finmap, binary trees and the size-balanced trees of Ordnode with their invariants. Chapter 4 treats vectors, which are lists of a fixed length, and infinite streams with proofs by coinduction.

Chapter 5 gives lazy data. It treats sequences that can be finite or infinite, computations that can fail to terminate, weak sequences and the parallel evaluation of computations. Chapter 6 gives the classes of functional programming: functors, applicative functors, monads and traversable functors, with their laws, and the fixed point operator for general recursion. Chapter 7 explains how a type of trees comes from a polynomial functor. It treats W types, M types, quotients of polynomial functors and the multivariate case. Chapter 8 treats the data of real machines, that is fixed-width integers, bit vectors, floating point numbers, characters and strings, and it ends with random generation and property-based testing.

The volume needs Volume 1 for the language of Lean and Mathlib, Volume 3 for sets, finite types and multisets, and Volume 4 for orders. Chapter 6 uses complete partial orders from Volume 4, and Chapter 7 uses cardinals from Volume 31. Chapters 1 and 2 come first. Chapters 3 and 4 need only them. Chapter 5 uses the streams of Chapter 4, and Chapter 7 uses the functors of Chapter 6. Chapter 8 needs only Chapter 1. The level is that of a first course in the theory of programs. The references are Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming*, the paper of McBride and Paterson on applicative functors, and the paper of Avigad, Carneiro and Hudon on datatypes as quotients of polynomial functors.

The conventions are those of Mathlib. A list is written `[3, 1, 2]`, and `l₁ ~ l₂` means that `l₂` is a permutation of `l₁`. Many definitions of this volume come from the core library of Lean or from the standard library Batteries, and Mathlib adds the lemmas. The boxes give the full names, so a reader can find each declaration in the documentation. The volume has no Lean companion yet, so the sections show the Mathlib statements and give no Lean exercises.
