## The L2 theory and Parseval

### Overview

Section 1 showed that the characters $e_n$ are orthonormal in $L^2$ of the circle. Volume 16, Chapter 6 proved that an orthonormal family whose span is dense is a Hilbert basis, and that a Hilbert basis satisfies Parseval's identity. So the whole $L^2$ theory of Fourier series reduces to one fact: the trigonometric polynomials are dense. This section proves the density with the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, in the same way as Mathlib.

The circle $\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}$ carries the probability measure $\mu_T$ of Section 1, and $\hat f(n) = \int e_{-n} f\,d\mu_T$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Trigonometric polynomial).** A *trigonometric polynomial* is a finite sum $p = \sum_{|n| \le N} a_n e_n$ with $a_n \in \mathbb{C}$. The trigonometric polynomials form the subalgebra of $C(\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{C})$ generated by the $e_n$, because $e_m e_n = e_{m+n}$. This subalgebra is closed under complex conjugation, because $\overline{e_n} = e_{-n}$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Density of trigonometric polynomials).** (a) The trigonometric polynomials are dense in $C(\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{C})$ for the sup norm. (b) For $1 \le p < \infty$, they are dense in $L^p(\mu_T)$.

*Proof.* (a) The circle is a compact Hausdorff space. This handbook takes the complex Stone-Weierstrass theorem from Mathlib without proof. It states: a star subalgebra of $C(X, \mathbb{C})$, for $X$ compact Hausdorff, that contains the constants and separates points is dense. The subalgebra $A$ of trigonometric polynomials contains $e_0 = 1$. It is a star subalgebra by the definition above. It separates points: let $x \ne y$ in $\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}$, with real representatives $s, t$. If $e_1(x) = e_1(y)$, then $e^{2\pi i (s - t)/T} = 1$, so $(s - t)/T \in \mathbb{Z}$ by the periodicity of the exponential (Chapter 1, Section 2). Then $x = y$, a contradiction. So $e_1$ separates $x$ and $y$, and $A$ is dense.

(b) Continuous functions are dense in $L^p(\mu)$ for $p < \infty$ and a finite regular measure $\mu$ on a compact Hausdorff space, and $\mu_T$ is one. In Mathlib this is `ContinuousMap.toLp_denseRange`. The sup norm controls the $L^p$ norm: $\|g\|_{L^p(\mu_T)} \le \sup|g|$, since $\mu_T$ has mass $1$. Let $f \in L^p$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Take $g$ continuous with $\|f - g\|_p < \varepsilon/2$. By (a) take $q$ in $A$ with $\sup|g - q| < \varepsilon/2$. Then $\|f - q\|_p < \varepsilon$. $\square$

**Corollary (Hilbert basis).** The family $(e_n)_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}$ is a Hilbert basis of $L^2(\mu_T)$. The coordinate of $f$ on $e_n$ is $\langle e_n, f\rangle = \hat f(n)$.

*Proof.* The family is orthonormal by Section 1, and its span is dense by part (b) with $p = 2$. Volume 16, Chapter 6, Section 3 proves that such a family is a Hilbert basis. The coordinate is $\langle e_n, f\rangle = \int \overline{e_n} f\,d\mu_T = \int e_{-n} f\,d\mu_T = \hat f(n)$. $\square$

**Theorem (Parseval).** Let $f \in L^2(\mu_T)$. Then the Fourier series $\sum_n \hat f(n) e_n$ converges to $f$ in $L^2(\mu_T)$, and
$$\sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} |\hat f(n)|^2 = \int |f|^2\,d\mu_T = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T |f(x)|^2\,dx.$$

*Proof.* Let $(u_n)$ be a Hilbert basis of a Hilbert space $H$ and $v \in H$. The partial sums $s_F = \sum_{n \in F}\langle u_n, v\rangle u_n$ over finite sets $F$ are the orthogonal projections of $v$ on the spans of the $u_n$ with $n \in F$. So $\|v - s_F\|$ is the distance from $v$ to that span. Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Density gives a finite combination $w$ of the $u_n$ with $\|v - w\| < \varepsilon$. Let $F_0$ hold the indices that occur in $w$. For every finite $F \supseteq F_0$, the span of the $u_n$ with $n \in F$ contains $w$, so $\|v - s_F\| \le \|v - w\| < \varepsilon$. Hence $s_F \to v$ along finite sets. By the Pythagorean theorem $\|s_F\|^2 = \sum_{n \in F}|\langle u_n, v\rangle|^2$, and $\|s_F\| \to \|v\|$ by continuity of the norm. So $\sum_n |\langle u_n, v\rangle|^2 = \|v\|^2$. Apply this to the Fourier basis, with $\langle e_n, f\rangle = \hat f(n)$ by the corollary and $\|f\|^2 = \int |f|^2\,d\mu_T$. $\square$

**Theorem (Absolutely summable series).** Let $f$ be continuous on $\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}$ with $\sum_n |\hat f(n)| < \infty$. Then the partial sums of $\sum_n \hat f(n) e_n$ converge to $f$ uniformly. In particular $f(x) = \sum_n \hat f(n) e_n(x)$ for every $x$.

*Proof.* Step 1. The series converges uniformly. Since $\sup|e_n| = 1$, the term $\hat f(n) e_n$ has sup norm $|\hat f(n)|$. The space $C(\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{C})$ with the sup norm is complete (Volume 16), and an absolutely summable series in a Banach space converges. Let $g$ be the sum. It is a continuous function.

Step 2. $g$ has the coefficients of $f$. The map $h \mapsto \hat h(m)$ is linear and satisfies $|\hat h(m)| \le \sup|h|$, so it is continuous for the sup norm. Apply it to the convergent series: $\hat g(m) = \sum_n \hat f(n)\,\hat{e_n}(m) = \hat f(m)$, by orthonormality.

Step 3. $f = g$. The continuous function $f - g$ has all its coefficients equal to $0$. By Parseval, $\int |f - g|^2\,d\mu_T = 0$. So $f = g$ almost everywhere. Suppose $f(x_0) \ne g(x_0)$. By continuity $f \ne g$ on an open arc around $x_0$, and an open arc has positive measure. This is a contradiction, so $f = g$ everywhere. $\square$

**Theorem (Several variables).** On the torus $\mathbb{T}^d = (\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^d$ with its product probability measure, put $e_n(x) = e^{2\pi i (n_1 x_1 + \cdots + n_d x_d)}$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}^d$. Then $(e_n)_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^d}$ is a Hilbert basis of $L^2(\mathbb{T}^d)$, Parseval holds, and an absolutely summable Fourier series of a continuous function converges uniformly to it.

*Proof.* The proofs above apply word for word. The products $e_n$ form a star subalgebra with the constants. It separates points: if $x \ne y$, then $x_i \ne y_i$ for some $i$, and the character $e_n$ with $n$ the $i$-th unit vector takes different values at $x$ and $y$. Orthonormality follows from Fubini (Volume 18, Chapter 6), since $\int e_n\,dx = \prod_i \int_0^1 e^{2\pi i n_i x_i}\,dx_i$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The Basel sum).** Let $f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1]$. Section 1 found $\hat f(0) = \frac12$ and $\hat f(n) = \frac{i}{2\pi n}$ for $n \ne 0$. Parseval gives
$$\frac14 + \sum_{n \ne 0}\frac{1}{4\pi^2 n^2} = \int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \frac13.$$
The sum over $n \ne 0$ is twice the sum over $n \ge 1$. So $\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac13 - \frac14 = \frac{1}{12}$, and
$$\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}.$$

**Example (A uniformly convergent series).** Let $f(x) = |x|$ on $[-\frac12, \frac12]$, with period $1$. Exercise 3 of Section 1 gives $\hat f(0) = \frac14$, $\hat f(n) = 0$ for even $n \ne 0$, and $\hat f(n) = -\frac{1}{\pi^2 n^2}$ for odd $n$. The coefficients are absolutely summable and $f$ is continuous, so the series converges uniformly to $f$. At $x = 0$ it gives $0 = \frac14 - \frac{2}{\pi^2}\sum_{n \text{ odd}, n > 0}\frac{1}{n^2}$, so $\sum_{n \text{ odd}} n^{-2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}$ over the positive odd $n$.

### The library around it

Everything on one variable sits in `Mathlib.Analysis.Fourier.AddCircle`, and the torus in `Mathlib.Analysis.Fourier.AddCircleMulti`. The index of this edition has no declaration for the pointwise convergence theorem of Dirichlet for piecewise smooth functions, for Fejér's theorem on Cesàro means, or for Carleson's theorem.

Common errors of a learner:

- Applying `AddCircle.hasSum_fourier_series_of_summable` to an element of `Lp`. It needs a `C(AddCircle T, ℂ)`.
- Using `AddCircle.tsum_sq_fourierCoeff` with the measure `volume`. The integral is for `haarAddCircle`, of mass $1$.

### Exercises

1. (A) Use Parseval for the square wave of Section 1 to prove $\sum_{n \text{ odd}, n > 0} n^{-2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}$.
2. (B) Let $f$ be $C^2$ and periodic. Show that its Fourier series converges uniformly to $f$. Hint: Exercise 4 of Section 1.
3. (B) Show that $f \mapsto (\hat f(n))_n$ is an isometry from $L^2(\mu_T)$ onto $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$. Hint: a Hilbert basis.
4. (B) Apply Parseval to $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0, 1]$ and deduce $\sum_{n \ge 1} n^{-4} = \frac{\pi^4}{90}$. Hint: $\hat f(n) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2 n^2} + \frac{i}{2\pi n}$ for $n \ne 0$.
5. (C) Let $f$ be $C^1$ and periodic, with $\int_0^1 f = 0$. Prove Wirtinger's inequality $\int_0^1 |f|^2 \le \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\int_0^1 |f'|^2$. Hint: Parseval for $f$ and $f'$.

### Notes and history

Parseval stated his identity for series in 1799, before Fourier's work. Fejér proved in 1900 that the Cesàro means of the Fourier series of a continuous function converge uniformly. Weierstrass proved his approximation theorem in 1885, and Stone extended it to compact spaces in 1937 and 1948. Carleson proved in 1966 that the Fourier series of an $L^2$ function converges almost everywhere.
