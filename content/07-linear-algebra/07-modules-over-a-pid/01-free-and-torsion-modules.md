## Free modules, torsion and submodules of free modules

### Overview

A module is free if it has a basis. Over a field each module is free, by Chapter 2. Over a ring two new phenomena appear. A module can have torsion: in $\mathbb{Z}/6$, the element $1$ satisfies $6 \cdot 1 = 0$ with $6 \neq 0$. And a submodule of a free module need not be free: the ideal $(2, X)$ of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not free. Over a principal ideal domain (PID) the second phenomenon does not occur, and torsion is the only obstruction to freeness for finitely generated modules.

In this section $R$ is a PID: an integral domain in which each ideal is generated by one element. The main examples are $\mathbb{Z}$ and $K[X]$ for a field $K$. The section proves that each submodule of $R^n$ is free of rank at most $n$, and that a finitely generated torsion free module over $R$ is free.

### Definitions

**Definition (Free module).** A module $M$ over a ring $R$ is free if it has a basis, that is, a linearly independent family that spans $M$.

**Definition (Torsion).** Let $R$ be an integral domain and $M$ an $R$-module. An element $x \in M$ is torsion if $a x = 0$ for some $a \neq 0$ in $R$. The torsion elements form a submodule $M_{\mathrm{tors}}$. The module $M$ is torsion if $M_{\mathrm{tors}} = M$, and torsion free if $M_{\mathrm{tors}} = 0$. For $a \in R$, the $a$-torsion is $M[a] = \{x : a x = 0\}$.

The torsion elements form a submodule since $R$ is a domain: if $a x = 0$ and $b y = 0$ with $a, b \neq 0$, then $a b \neq 0$ and $a b (x + y) = 0$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Submodules of free modules).** Let $R$ be a PID and $N$ a submodule of $R^n$. Then $N$ is free of rank at most $n$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$. For $n = 0$, $N = 0$ is free of rank $0$. For the step, let $\pi : R^n \to R$ be the last coordinate, and $N' = N \cap \ker \pi$. The module $\ker \pi$ is $R^{n-1}$, so by the induction hypothesis $N'$ is free of rank at most $n - 1$. The image $\pi(N)$ is an ideal of $R$, so $\pi(N) = (a)$ for some $a$.

If $a = 0$, then $N = N'$ and the claim holds. Otherwise choose $y \in N$ with $\pi(y) = a$. Each $x \in N$ has $\pi(x) = c a$ for a unique $c \in R$, since $R$ is a domain, and then $x - c y \in N'$. So $N = N' + R y$. The sum is direct: if $c y \in N'$, then $c a = 0$, so $c = 0$. Also $R y \cong R$, since $c y = 0$ gives $c a = 0$. So a basis of $N'$ with $y$ added is a basis of $N$, of size at most $n$. $\square$

The proof uses the PID property once, for the ideal $\pi(N)$. Over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the ideal $(2, X)$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}[X]^1$ that is not free, and the proof breaks exactly at this step.

**Theorem (Torsion free modules).** Let $R$ be a PID and $M$ a finitely generated torsion free $R$-module. Then $M$ is free.

*Proof.* Let $x_1, \dots, x_m$ generate $M$. Choose a maximal linearly independent subfamily, say $x_1, \dots, x_k$, and let $F$ be its span, a free module. For each $j > k$, the family $x_1, \dots, x_k, x_j$ is dependent, so there is a relation $a_j x_j + \sum_{i \le k} c_i x_i = 0$ with not all coefficients zero. Since $x_1, \dots, x_k$ are independent, $a_j \neq 0$, and $a_j x_j \in F$. Let $a = \prod_{j > k} a_j \neq 0$. Then $a x_j \in F$ for each $j$, so $a M \subseteq F$. The map $x \mapsto a x$ is injective on $M$, since $M$ is torsion free. So $M \cong a M$, a submodule of the free module $F \cong R^k$. By the last theorem, $M$ is free. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (A submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^2$).** Let $N = \{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2 : a \equiv b \pmod 2\}$. The proof of the theorem finds a basis. The last coordinate maps $N$ onto $\mathbb{Z}$, since $(1, 1) \in N$; take $y = (1, 1)$. The intersection $N' = N \cap (\mathbb{Z} \times 0)$ is $2\mathbb{Z} \times 0$, with the basis $(2, 0)$. So $(2, 0), (1, 1)$ is a basis of $N$. Check: $(a, b) = \tfrac{a - b}{2}(2, 0) + b (1, 1)$, with $\tfrac{a - b}{2} \in \mathbb{Z}$. The index of $N$ in $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is $|\det \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}| = 2$.

**Example (Torsion in a product).** In $M = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/6$, the torsion submodule is $0 \times \mathbb{Z}/6$, and $M / M_{\mathrm{tors}} \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is free. The $2$-torsion is $M[2] = 0 \times \{0, 3\}$, and the $3$-torsion is $M[3] = 0 \times \{0, 2, 4\}$.

**Example (Modules over $K[X]$ from matrices).** Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix over a field $K$. Then $K^n$ is a $K[X]$-module, with $p \cdot v = p(A) v$. By the theorem of Cayley and Hamilton, $\chi_A$ kills each vector, so this module is torsion. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the vector $e_2$ generates the module, since $(A - 1) e_2 = e_1$, and the module is $K[X]/((X - 1)^2)$.

**Counterexample (Torsion free, not free).** The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Q}$ is torsion free and not free. Any two elements $p/q$ and $r/s$ are dependent: $(r q) \cdot (p/q) - (p s) \cdot (r/s) = 0$. So a basis would have one element $x$, and $\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Z} x$, which fails for $x/2$. The theorem does not apply, since $\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated.

**Counterexample (A ring that is not a PID).** In $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]$, the ideal $I = (2, X)$ is a submodule of the free module $R$. It is not free: two elements $f, g$ of $I$ are always dependent, by $g \cdot f - f \cdot g = 0$, so a basis would have one element, and $I$ would be principal. But $I$ is not principal: a generator would divide $2$ and $X$, so it would be $\pm 1$, and $1 \notin I$.

### The library around it

The free modules are in `Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.FreeModule.Basic`, with the rank of a free module in `Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.FreeModule.Finite.Basic`. The torsion submodules are in `Mathlib.Algebra.Module.Torsion.Basic`. The results over a PID are in `Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.FreeModule.PID`. The projective modules, which are the direct summands of free modules, are in `Mathlib.Algebra.Module.Projective`.

Three small files give instances of `Module.IsTorsionFree`. A module over a division ring is torsion free (`DivisionSemiring.to_moduleIsTorsionFree`), since each nonzero scalar is invertible. A product of torsion free modules is torsion free, for two factors (`Prod.moduleIsTorsionFree`) and for a family (`Pi.instModuleIsTorsionFree`), since $r (x_i)_i = 0$ means $r x_i = 0$ for each $i$.

The rank of a free module is well defined over each nontrivial commutative ring. The instance `commRing_strongRankCondition` says that such a ring has the strong rank condition of Chapter 2 Section 5: an injective linear map $R^n \to R^m$ forces $n \le m$. The file `Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.FreeModule.StrongRankCondition` holds only this instance, so that the instance search finds it fast.

Common errors of a learner:

- `Module.Free R M` is a proposition. To use a basis, write `Module.Free.chooseBasis R M`, or obtain one with `Module.Free.exists_basis`.
- `Submodule.torsion R M` uses the nonzero divisors of `R`. Over a ring with zero divisors, it differs from the elements killed by a nonzero scalar.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find a basis of the submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ generated by $(4, 6)$ and $(2, 2)$, and its index.
2. (A) Find the torsion submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^2 / \mathbb{Z}(2, 4)$.
3. (A) Show that a free module over a domain is torsion free.
4. (B) Show that the submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^3$ of the vectors with the sum of the coordinates even is free of rank $3$, and give a basis.
5. (B) Show that $M / M_{\mathrm{tors}}$ is torsion free for each module over a domain.
6. (B) Show that each finitely generated module over a PID is $M_{\mathrm{tors}} \oplus F$ with $F$ free. Hint: $M / M_{\mathrm{tors}}$ is free, so the quotient map splits.
7. (C) Let $R = K[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ be the ring of polynomials in infinitely many variables. Show that the ideal of the polynomials without constant term is a submodule of $R$ that is not finitely generated. Hint: a finite set of generators uses only finitely many variables.

### Notes and history

The theorem that a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is free goes back to the work of Dedekind on lattices and ideals in the 1870s. The general form over a PID is in the algebra texts of the 1930s, after the work of Noether and her school on modules over rings. The counterexample $\mathbb{Q}$ shows the role of the finite generation; the theorem of Baer from 1937 says that $\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}$ is torsion free and not free.
