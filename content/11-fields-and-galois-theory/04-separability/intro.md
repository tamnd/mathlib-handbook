# Separability

A polynomial of degree $n$ over a field has at most $n$ roots, and in a splitting field it has exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity. Galois theory needs exactly $n$ distinct roots. A polynomial with this property is separable, and an element whose minimal polynomial is separable is a separable element. In characteristic $0$ each irreducible polynomial is separable, so a course over $\mathbb{Q}$ can almost forget the notion. In characteristic $p$ it cannot: the polynomial $X^p - t$ over the field $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ is irreducible and has one root of multiplicity $p$. This chapter makes the notion precise and shows where it is needed.

Section 1 defines the formal derivative, separable polynomials, separable elements and separable extensions. It proves that an irreducible polynomial is separable if and only if its derivative is not zero, and it counts the embeddings of a simple extension into a large field. Section 2 defines perfect fields, the fields over which each algebraic extension is separable, and proves that fields of characteristic $0$ and finite fields are perfect. It then splits each algebraic extension into a separable part and a purely inseparable part, and defines the separable degree. Section 3 proves the primitive element theorem: a finite separable extension is generated by one element. Its main example is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$.

The chapter needs the minimal polynomial of Chapter 2, the splitting fields and the algebraic closure of Chapter 3, and the characteristic and the Frobenius map of Volume 6, Chapter 4. Chapter 5 uses separability in the definition of a Galois extension, and the count of embeddings of Section 1 is the main input of the inequality $|\operatorname{Aut}(L/K)| \le [L:K]$.
