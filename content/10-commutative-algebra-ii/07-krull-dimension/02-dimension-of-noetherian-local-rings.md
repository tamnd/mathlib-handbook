## Dimension of Noetherian local rings

### Overview

For a Noetherian local ring $(R, \mathfrak m, k)$ there are two numbers attached to the maximal ideal: the Krull dimension $\dim R$, and the embedding dimension $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, which by Nakayama's lemma is the least number of generators of $\mathfrak m$. Krull's height theorem gives $\dim R \le \dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$; in particular, the dimension is finite. The rings with equality are the regular local rings, the local rings of smooth points.

The section also proves that dividing by a nonzerodivisor in $\mathfrak m$ lowers the dimension by exactly one, and that a regular local ring of dimension $1$ is a DVR.

### Definitions

Throughout, $(R, \mathfrak m, k)$ is a Noetherian local ring.

**Definition (Embedding dimension).** The embedding dimension of $R$ is $\operatorname{edim} R = \dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, the dimension of the cotangent space. By Nakayama's lemma (Chapter 2, Section 2), it is the least number of generators of $\mathfrak m$.

**Definition (Regular local ring).** $R$ is regular if $\dim R = \operatorname{edim} R$, that is, if $\mathfrak m$ can be generated by $\dim R$ elements. A Noetherian ring is regular if all its localizations at primes are regular local rings.

### Theorems and proofs

Two results of Volume 9 are used. Krull's principal ideal theorem says that a minimal prime over a principal ideal $(x)$ in a Noetherian ring has height at most $1$. Krull's height theorem says that a minimal prime over an ideal generated by $n$ elements has height at most $n$.

**Theorem (Dimension bound).** $\dim R \le \operatorname{edim} R < \infty$.

*Proof.* The ideal $\mathfrak m$ is generated by $e = \operatorname{edim} R$ elements, and it is a minimal prime over itself. By the height theorem $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak m \le e$. Every chain of primes in $R$ ends at a prime inside $\mathfrak m$ and can be extended to end at $\mathfrak m$, so $\dim R = \operatorname{ht}\mathfrak m \le e$. $\square$

**Theorem (Cutting by a nonzerodivisor).** Let $x \in \mathfrak m$ be a nonzerodivisor. Then $\dim R/(x) = \dim R - 1$.

*Proof.* Let $d = \dim R/(x)$. First, $d + 1 \le \dim R$. Take a chain $\bar{\mathfrak p}_0 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq \bar{\mathfrak p}_d$ in $R/(x)$, with preimages $\mathfrak p_0 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq \mathfrak p_d$ in $R$, all containing $x$. A minimal prime of $R$ consists of zero divisors (Volume 9), so $\mathfrak p_0$ is not minimal, and there is a prime $\mathfrak q \subsetneq \mathfrak p_0$. This gives a chain of length $d + 1$.

Second, $\dim R \le d + 1$. The ring $R/(x)$ is a Noetherian local ring of dimension $d$, so it has a system of parameters: elements $y_1, \dots, y_d \in \mathfrak m$ whose images generate an ideal with radical $\bar{\mathfrak m}$. The existence of a system of parameters is the other half of the dimension theorem for Noetherian local rings, used here without proof. Then $\mathfrak m$ is minimal over $(x, y_1, \dots, y_d)$, and the height theorem gives $\dim R = \operatorname{ht}\mathfrak m \le d + 1$. $\square$

The second half only needs $x \in \mathfrak m$; the hypothesis that $x$ is a nonzerodivisor is used for the first half.

**Theorem (Regular of dimension one).** A regular local ring of dimension $1$ is a DVR. Conversely, a DVR is a regular local ring of dimension $1$.

*Proof.* Let $R$ be regular of dimension $1$. Then $\mathfrak m = (\pi)$ is principal. Since $\dim R = 1$, $\mathfrak m$ is not a minimal prime, so $\pi$ is not nilpotent. By the Krull intersection theorem (Chapter 6, Section 1), every nonzero $x$ lies in $\mathfrak m^n \setminus \mathfrak m^{n+1}$ for some $n$, so $x = u\pi^n$ with $u$ a unit. A product of two nonzero elements is then $uv\pi^{a+b}$, which is nonzero because $\pi$ is not nilpotent. So $R$ is a Noetherian local domain with principal maximal ideal that is not a field, which is a DVR by the characterization of Chapter 3, Section 3. Conversely, a DVR has dimension $1$ and principal maximal ideal. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Fields and DVRs).** A field is regular of dimension $0$, with $\mathfrak m = 0$. Every DVR, such as $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $k[[X]]$, is regular of dimension $1$. The localization of a Dedekind domain at a maximal ideal is a DVR, so Dedekind domains are regular rings.

**Example (Affine space).** Let $R = k[X_1, \dots, X_n]_{\mathfrak m}$ with $\mathfrak m = (X_1, \dots, X_n)$. Then $\dim R = n$ (Section 1) and $\mathfrak m$ has $n$ generators, so $R$ is regular. Every localization of $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ at a prime is regular, by the theorem that a polynomial ring over a regular ring is regular. The power series ring $k[[X_1, \dots, X_n]]$ is regular of dimension $n$.

**Example (The cusp).** Let $A = k[X, Y]/(Y^2 - X^3)$ and $R = A_{\mathfrak m}$ with $\mathfrak m = (x, y)$. The ring $R$ has dimension $1$, as the quotient of a regular local ring of dimension $2$ by a nonzerodivisor. In $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ the images of $x$ and $y$ are independent, since $y^2 - x^3 \in \mathfrak m^2$ gives no linear relation. So $\operatorname{edim} R = 2 > 1$, and $R$ is not regular. It is also not normal: $y/x$ satisfies $T^2 = x$, a monic equation, but does not lie in $R$.

**Example (Embedding dimension of a point).** Let $R = k[X, Y]_{(X, Y)}/(XY)$, the local ring of the node. It has dimension $1$ and embedding dimension $2$: the relation $XY$ is in $\mathfrak m^2$. So $R$ is not regular. It is not a domain, in accordance with the theorem that regular local rings are domains.

**Example (Strict inequality $\mathfrak m^2 < \mathfrak m$).** If $\dim R \ne 0$, then $\mathfrak m^2 \subsetneq \mathfrak m$; otherwise $\mathfrak m = \mathfrak m^2$ gives $\mathfrak m = 0$ by Nakayama, and $R$ is a field. This is `IsLocalRing.maximalIdeal_sq_lt_of_ringKrullDim_ne_zero`.

### The library around it

The definitions of regular local rings and regular rings are in `Mathlib/RingTheory/RegularLocalRing/Defs.lean`, and `RegularLocalRing/Polynomial.lean` proves `Polynomial.isRegularRing_of_isRegularRing` and `MvPolynomial.isRegularRing_of_isRegularRing`: polynomial rings in finitely many variables over a regular ring are regular. The dimension theory of quotients by regular elements and regular sequences is in `KrullDimension/Regular.lean`. The heights, the principal ideal theorem and the height theorem are in Volume 9, and the cotangent space is in `Mathlib/RingTheory/Ideal/Cotangent.lean`, also in Volume 9.

The index of this edition has no declaration for the Hilbert-Samuel function of a local ring or for the theorem that its degree is the dimension. Mathlib has the Hilbert polynomials of graded modules, which are in Volume 9.

Common errors of a learner:

- `IsRegularLocalRing` compares `spanFinrank` with `ringKrullDim`, a value in `WithBot ℕ∞`; the coercion from `ℕ` is part of the statement.
- The bound `ringKrullDim R ≤ spanFinrank` needs a Noetherian local ring. Without the Noetherian hypothesis, $\mathfrak m$ may be finitely generated while the dimension is larger, as for a valuation ring of dimension $2$ with principal maximal ideal.
- Cutting by `x` lowers the dimension by one only for `x` in the maximal ideal; for a unit, the quotient is the zero ring.

2. (A) Show that $k[X, Y]_{(X, Y)}/(Y - X^2)$ is regular, and compute its dimension.
3. (A) Compute the dimension and embedding dimension of $k[X]/(X^2)$, and decide whether it is regular.
4. (B) Let $R$ be regular of dimension $d$ and $x \in \mathfrak m \setminus \mathfrak m^2$. Show that $R/(x)$ is regular of dimension $d - 1$, assuming that $x$ is a nonzerodivisor.
5. (B) Show that $\mathbb{Z}[X]_{(p, X)}/(X^2 - p)$ is a DVR for every prime $p$.
6. (B) Show that $k[X, Y, Z]_{(X, Y, Z)}/(XY - Z^2)$ has dimension $2$ and embedding dimension $3$.
7. (C) Show that a regular local ring of dimension $2$ is a domain.

### Notes and history

Krull defined local rings, their dimension and regular local rings in the 1930s. Serre characterized regular local rings in the 1950s as the Noetherian local rings of finite global dimension, which gave the theorem that localizations of regular local rings are regular. Auslander and Buchsbaum proved in 1959 that regular local rings are unique factorization domains.
