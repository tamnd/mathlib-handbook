## Sums of nonnegative terms

### Overview

A series of nonnegative real numbers converges exactly when its partial sums are bounded. The tests of Volume 15, Chapter 1, reduce to it. This section proves the same test for unconditional sums. It works in an ordered commutative group with a topology that is compatible with the order, and it uses the finite sums $S_F = \sum_{i \in F} f_i$ of Chapter 4 in place of the partial sums over $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$.

The main results: sums preserve the order, the sum of a nonnegative family is the supremum of its finite sums, a nonnegative real family is summable exactly when its finite sums are bounded, and in a complete lattice such as $[0, \infty]$ every family is summable.

### Definitions

Let $\alpha$ be a commutative monoid with a preorder and a topology. The topology is *order closed* when the set $\{(x, y) : x \le y\}$ is closed in $\alpha \times \alpha$. Then limits preserve the order: if $x_j \to x$ and $y_j \to y$ along a filter that is not trivial, and $x_j \le y_j$ eventually, then $x \le y$. The order topology of a linear order, generated by the open rays, is order closed. The monoid is *ordered* when $x \le y$ implies $x + z \le y + z$. In Mathlib these are the classes `OrderClosedTopology α`, `OrderTopology α` and `IsOrderedAddMonoid α`.

A family $f : I \to \alpha$ is *nonnegative* when $0 \le f_i$ for every $i$. For a nonnegative family the finite sums increase with the set: if $F \subseteq F'$, then $S_{F'} = S_F + S_{F' \setminus F} \ge S_F$. So the function $F \mapsto S_F$ is monotone on the directed set of finite subsets of $I$.

A monoid is *canonically ordered* when $x \le y$ holds exactly when $y = x + z$ for some $z$. In such a monoid every element is nonnegative. The examples are $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ and $[0, \infty]$. Mathlib writes the class `CanonicallyOrderedAdd α`.

::: mathlib
`SupConvergenceClass` · `Mathlib.Topology.Order.MonotoneConvergence`

The class says that a monotone net converges to its supremum: if a set has the least upper bound $a$, then the filter `atTop` on the set tends to $a$. The order topology of a linear order and the topology of `ℝ≥0∞` satisfy it.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Comparison of sums).** Let $\alpha$ be an ordered commutative monoid with an order closed topology. Let $f, g : I \to \alpha$ with $f_i \le g_i$ for every $i$, and suppose that $f$ has the sum $a$ and $g$ has the sum $b$. Then $a \le b$.

*Proof.* For each finite set $F$ the inequalities $f_i \le g_i$ add up to $S_F(f) \le S_F(g)$, since the monoid is ordered. The pair $(S_F(f), S_F(g))$ tends to $(a, b)$ along $\mathrm{atTop}$, and this filter is not trivial. The pair lies in the closed set $\{(x, y) : x \le y\}$ for every $F$, so its limit $(a, b)$ lies in it. $\square$

The strict form `hasSum_lt` needs a group: if also $f_i < g_i$ for one $i$, then $a < b$. Apply the theorem to $f$ and to the family $g'$ equal to $g$ except at $i$, where $g'_i = f_i$; then $a \le b - g_i + f_i < b$.

::: mathlib
`hasSum_le` · `Mathlib.Topology.Algebra.InfiniteSum.Order`

The statement takes the hypothesis `∀ i, f i ≤ g i`, then `HasSum f a₁ L` and `HasSum g a₂ L`, and concludes `a₁ ≤ a₂` for any summation filter `L` with `L.NeBot`. The version with `tsum` is `Summable.tsum_le_tsum`, and `Summable.tsum_mono` states it with `f ≤ g` as a relation of functions. Both need the two families to be summable.

<?decl hasSum_le?>
:::

**Theorem (Finite sums lie below the sum).** Under the hypotheses of the comparison theorem, let $f$ have the sum $a$, and let $s$ be a finite set with $f_i \ge 0$ for every $i \notin s$. Then $S_s \le a$.

*Proof.* For each finite $F \supseteq s$, the set $F \setminus s$ consists of indices outside $s$, so $S_F = S_s + S_{F \setminus s} \ge S_s$. The sets $F \supseteq s$ form a set of the filter $\mathrm{atTop}$. So $S_F \ge S_s$ eventually, and the limit $a$ satisfies $a \ge S_s$, since the set $\{x : x \ge S_s\}$ is closed. $\square$

Two special cases are used often. If $f$ is summable and nonnegative, then $f_i \le \sum' f$ for each $i$ (`Summable.le_tsum`), and $\sum' f \ge 0$. The second inequality, `tsum_nonneg`, holds even when $f$ is not summable, since then $\sum' f = 0$.

**Theorem (The sum is the supremum).** Let $\alpha$ be as above and $f$ nonnegative with the sum $a$. Then $a$ is the least upper bound of the set $\{S_F : F \text{ finite}\}$.

*Proof.* The previous theorem with $s = F$ shows that $a$ is an upper bound. Let $c$ be another upper bound. Then $S_F \le c$ for every $F$, and $S_F \to a$, so $a \le c$ because the set $\{x : x \le c\}$ is closed. $\square$

The argument of the second part needs no nonnegativity: if $f$ has the sum $a$ and all finite sums are at most $c$, then $a \le c$. Mathlib names this `hasSum_le_of_sum_le`, and it assumes only that the intervals $(-\infty, c]$ are closed.

::: mathlib
`isLUB_hasSum` · `Mathlib.Topology.Algebra.InfiniteSum.Order`

The conclusion is `IsLUB (Set.range fun s ↦ ∑ i ∈ s, f i) a`, where `s` runs over `Finset ι`. The finite version `sum_le_hasSum` takes the set `s` and the hypothesis `∀ i ∉ s, 0 ≤ f i`. The forms with `tsum` are `Summable.sum_le_tsum` and `Summable.tsum_le_of_sum_le`.

<?decl isLUB_hasSum?>
:::

The converse needs completeness of the order. A monotone net converges to its supremum when the topology is the order topology of a linear order.

**Theorem (A supremum is a sum).** Let $\alpha$ be a linearly ordered commutative monoid with the order topology, and let $f$ be nonnegative. If the set of finite sums has a least upper bound $a$, then $f$ has the sum $a$.

*Proof.* If $a$ is the least element of $\alpha$, every $S_F$ equals $a$ and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise let $U$ be a neighbourhood of $a$. Since the topology is the order topology, $U$ contains a set $\{x : l < x < u\}$ with $l < a < u$, where the condition $x < u$ is dropped when $a$ is the largest element. Since $l < a$ and $a$ is the least upper bound, $l$ is not an upper bound, so $S_{F_0} > l$ for some finite $F_0$. For each $F \supseteq F_0$, monotonicity gives $l < S_{F_0} \le S_F$, and $S_F \le a < u$. So $S_F \in U$ for all $F \supseteq F_0$, and $S_F \to a$. $\square$

::: mathlib
`hasSum_of_isLUB_of_nonneg` · `Mathlib.Topology.Algebra.InfiniteSum.Order`

The hypotheses are `∀ i, 0 ≤ f i` and `IsLUB (Set.range fun s ↦ ∑ i ∈ s, f i) b`, in a linear order with `OrderTopology α`. In a canonically ordered monoid the nonnegativity is automatic, and the lemma `hasSum_of_isLUB` drops it.

**Corollary (Bounded finite sums in ℝ).** A nonnegative family $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ is summable exactly when there is a constant $c$ with $S_F \le c$ for every finite $F$. Then $\sum' f \le c$.

*Proof.* If $f$ is summable, take $c = \sum' f$. Conversely the set of finite sums is nonempty and bounded above, so it has a least upper bound in $\mathbb{R}$ by completeness of the order. The previous theorem shows that $f$ has this supremum as its sum, and the supremum is at most $c$. $\square$

For $I = \mathbb{N}$ it suffices to bound the partial sums $s_n = S_{\{0, \dots, n-1\}}$, since every finite $F$ lies in some $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$, and then $S_F \le s_n$. So a nonnegative real sequence is summable exactly when its partial sums do not tend to $+\infty$ (`summable_iff_not_tendsto_nat_atTop_of_nonneg`).

**Corollary (Comparison test).** Let $0 \le g_i \le f_i$ for every $i$, with $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ summable. Then $g$ is summable and $\sum' g \le \sum' f$.

*Proof.* For each finite $F$, $S_F(g) \le S_F(f) \le \sum' f$, by the theorem on finite sums. The corollary on bounded finite sums gives that $g$ is summable, and the comparison of sums gives the inequality. $\square$

**Theorem (Sums in a complete lattice).** Let $\alpha$ be a canonically ordered commutative monoid that is a complete lattice, with a topology in the class `SupConvergenceClass`. Then every family $f : I \to \alpha$ has the sum $\sup_F S_F$.

*Proof.* Every element of $\alpha$ is nonnegative, so $F \mapsto S_F$ is monotone. The supremum $a = \sup_F S_F$ exists because $\alpha$ is a complete lattice. The class says that the monotone net $F \mapsto S_F$ converges to its supremum $a$. $\square$

Chapter 6 applies this theorem to $[0, \infty]$. There `tsum` is always the supremum of the finite sums (`tsum_eq_iSup_sum`), and two summations can always be exchanged (`tsum_comm`, `tsum_sigma`), with no hypothesis of summability.

::: mathlib
`CompleteLattice.hasSum` · `Mathlib.Topology.Algebra.InfiniteSum.Order`

The conclusion is `HasSum f (⨆ s, ∑ a ∈ s, f a)`, with `CompleteLattice α`, `CanonicallyOrderedAdd α` and `SupConvergenceClass α`. The corollary `CompleteLattice.summable` replaces the older `ENNReal.summable`, which is deprecated. The unprimed names `tsum_comm` and `tsum_sigma` without a namespace are the lattice versions; the versions for summable families in a group are `Summable.tsum_comm` and `Summable.tsum_sigma` of Chapter 4.

### Examples

**Example (The sum of $1/n^2$).** Let $f(n) = 1/n^2$ for $n \ge 1$ and $f(0) = 0$. For $n \ge 2$, $\frac{1}{n^2} \le \frac{1}{n(n-1)} = \frac{1}{n-1} - \frac{1}{n}$. The partial sums telescope:
$$s_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N-1} \frac{1}{n^2} \le 1 + \sum_{n=2}^{N-1} \Bigl(\frac{1}{n-1} - \frac{1}{n}\Bigr) = 2 - \frac{1}{N-1} < 2$$
for $N \ge 3$. So $f$ is summable, by the corollary on bounded partial sums, and $\sum' f \le 2$. The exact value $\pi^2/6$ needs Fourier series or the Euler product of the sine, and Mathlib proves it in number theory.

::: mathlib
`hasSum_zeta_two` · `Mathlib.NumberTheory.ZetaValues`

The statement is `HasSum (fun n : ℕ ↦ 1 / (n : ℝ) ^ 2) (π ^ 2 / 6)`. The term $n = 0$ is $1/0 = 0$ by the convention of Mathlib on division, so the sum runs over all of `ℕ`. The general $p$-series test is `Real.summable_one_div_nat_pow` in `Mathlib.Analysis.PSeries`, which states `Summable (fun n ↦ 1 / n ^ p) ↔ 1 < p`.

<?decl Real.summable_one_div_nat_pow?>
:::

**Example (The harmonic sum).** Let $f(n) = 1/n$ for $n \ge 1$. The block $2^k < n \le 2^{k+1}$ has $2^k$ terms, each at least $2^{-k-1}$, so its sum is at least $1/2$. So $s_{2^m + 1} \ge 1 + m/2$, the partial sums tend to $+\infty$, and $f$ is not summable. The same bound shows that a constant family $b > 0$ on an infinite type is not summable: a set of $n$ indices has the finite sum $nb$, which is not bounded. This is `Finite.of_summable_const`, which holds in any archimedean ordered group.

**Example (A counterexample: bounded sums in ℚ).** The corollary on bounded finite sums uses the completeness of the order of $\mathbb{R}$. It fails in $\mathbb{Q}$. Let $f(n) = 1/n!$ in $\mathbb{Q}$. The finite sums are positive and bounded by $3$. If $f$ had a sum $q \in \mathbb{Q}$, then the continuous additive inclusion $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R}$ would carry it to a sum of $f$ in $\mathbb{R}$, by the rule for images of Chapter 4, and the uniqueness of sums in $\mathbb{R}$ would give $q = e$. Since $e$ is irrational, $f$ has no sum in $\mathbb{Q}$. The finite sums have no least upper bound in $\mathbb{Q}$ either, so the theorem on suprema does not apply.

**Remark (Common errors: comparison with a family that is not summable).** The comparison of `tsum` values needs both families to be summable. Let $f(n) = 1/n^2$ and $g(n) = 1$ on $\mathbb{N}$. Then $f \le g$ termwise. The family $g$ is not summable, so $\sum' g = 0$, while $\sum' f = \pi^2/6 > 0$. The inequality $\sum' f \le \sum' g$ is false. A proof that cites `Summable.tsum_le_tsum` must supply `Summable g`. In contrast, `Real.tsum_le_of_sum_range_le` needs no hypothesis of summability, since its hypothesis gives $0 \le c$.

### The library around it

The module `Mathlib.Topology.Algebra.InfiniteSum.Order` has three parts. The first compares sums in an ordered monoid with an order closed topology, with `Summable.tsum_le_tsum_of_inj` for a family that is the image of another under an injection. The second treats ordered groups: the strict inequalities `hasSum_lt` and `Summable.tsum_pos`, the lemmas on `|f|` of Section 2, and lemmas for an archimedean field such as `Summable.tendsto_atTop_of_pos`. The third treats complete lattices.

The module `Mathlib.Topology.Algebra.InfiniteSum.Real` collects the facts that need $\mathbb{R}$: `summable_of_sum_le`, `summable_of_sum_range_le`, `summable_iff_not_tendsto_nat_atTop_of_nonneg`, and the sums over a partition: a nonnegative family on a sigma type is summable exactly when each fibre is summable and the sums over the fibres form a summable family (`summable_sigma_of_nonneg`, and `summable_partition` for a partition into sets).

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\sum_{n \ge 0} \frac{1}{2^n + n}$ is summable in $\mathbb{R}$. *Hint:* compare with $2^{-n}$.
2. (A) Let $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ be nonnegative and summable, and $J \subseteq I$. Show that $\sum'_{i \in J} f_i \le \sum'_{i \in I} f_i$. *Hint:* extend the family on $J$ by $0$ and compare.
3. (A) Show that $\sum'_n f(n) \ge 0$ for every $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $f \ge 0$, without a hypothesis of summability.
4. (B) Show that $\sum_{n \ge 2} \frac{1}{n \log n}$ is not summable, and that $\sum_{n \ge 2} \frac{1}{n (\log n)^2}$ is summable. *Hint:* group the terms in the blocks $2^k \le n < 2^{k+1}$, as in the example on the harmonic sum.
5. (B) Let $f : \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(m, n) = \frac{1}{(m + n + 1)^3}$. Show that $f$ is summable. *Hint:* group by $k = m + n$; the fibre has $k + 1$ elements, and use `summable_sigma_of_nonneg` through an equivalence with a sigma type.
6. (B) Let $\alpha$ be an ordered commutative group with an order closed topology, and $f, g$ summable with $f \le g$ and $\sum' f = \sum' g$. Show that $f = g$. *Hint:* apply `hasSum_zero_iff_of_nonneg` to $g - f$.
7. (C) Let $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ be nonnegative and summable. Show that the set $\{i : f_i > 0\}$ is countable. *Hint:* for each $k$ the set $\{i : f_i > 1/k\}$ is finite, since a finite subset of $n$ elements has a sum above $n/k$.

### Notes and history

The criterion of bounded partial sums for series of positive terms is classical, and the comparison test was in use in the analysis of the nineteenth century. In the unconditional setting the sum of a nonnegative family is simply the supremum of its finite sums, which is why measure theory and probability take sums in $[0, \infty]$ before any question of convergence arises.
