## Subrings, generated subrings and the center

### Overview

A subring is a subset of a ring that contains $1$ and is closed under the sum, the negative and the product. It is then a ring with the operations of the big ring. Many rings of interest are born as subrings: the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ inside $\mathbb{C}$, the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt 2]$ inside $\mathbb{R}$, the ring $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ of the fractions with a power of $2$ as denominator inside $\mathbb{Q}$, and the continuous functions inside all functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$.

This section gives the subring test, shows that the subrings of a ring form a complete lattice, and describes the subring generated by a set: it consists of the sums of products of the generators and their negatives. It proves that images and preimages of subrings under homomorphisms are subrings, so the range of a homomorphism is a subring, and that two homomorphisms that agree on a set agree on the subring it generates. The center of a ring is a subring, and the center of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ is computed. The smallest subring, the image of $\mathbb{Z}$, is the prime subring.

### Definitions

**Definition (Subring).** Let $R$ be a ring. A subring of $R$ is a subset $S \subseteq R$ with $1 \in S$ and, for all $a, b \in S$, $a + b \in S$, $-a \in S$ and $a b \in S$. A subsemiring of a semiring is a subset that contains $0$ and $1$ and is closed under the sum and the product.

A subring contains $0 = 1 + (-1)$. So a subring is an additive subgroup and a multiplicative submonoid at the same time. With the restricted operations it is a ring: each axiom is an identity between elements of $R$, so it holds for the elements of $S$. The inclusion $S \to R$ is an injective ring homomorphism.

The condition $1 \in S$ is part of the definition. The even integers $2\mathbb{Z}$ are closed under the sum, the negative and the product, but $1 \notin 2\mathbb{Z}$, so $2\mathbb{Z}$ is not a subring of $\mathbb{Z}$. It is an ideal, in the sense of Chapter 2.

**Definition (Generated subring).** Let $s \subseteq R$. The subring generated by $s$, written $\langle s \rangle$, is the intersection of all the subrings of $R$ that contain $s$. For $\alpha \in R$, the subring generated by $\{\alpha\}$ is written $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$.

**Definition (Center).** The center of a ring $R$ is $Z(R) = \{ z \in R : z a = a z \text{ for all } a \in R \}$.

**Definition (Range).** The range, or image, of a ring homomorphism $f : R \to S$ is $f(R) = \{ f(a) : a \in R \}$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Subring test).** A subset $S$ of a ring $R$ is a subring if and only if $1 \in S$ and $a - b \in S$ and $a b \in S$ for all $a, b \in S$.

*Proof.* A subring has these properties, since $a - b = a + (-b)$. Conversely, assume them. Then $0 = 1 - 1 \in S$. For $b \in S$, $-b = 0 - b \in S$. For $a, b \in S$, $a + b = a - (-b) \in S$. So $S$ is closed under the sum, the negative and the product, and it contains $1$. $\square$

**Theorem (Intersections).** Let $(S_i)_{i \in I}$ be a family of subrings of $R$, with $I$ possibly empty. Then $\bigcap_i S_i$ is a subring, with the convention that the empty intersection is $R$.

*Proof.* Each $S_i$ contains $1$, so the intersection does. If $a$ and $b$ are in each $S_i$, then $a - b$ and $a b$ are in each $S_i$, by the subring test, so they are in the intersection. $\square$

So $\langle s \rangle$ is a subring. It contains $s$, and it is contained in each subring that contains $s$: it is the smallest subring that contains $s$. The subrings of $R$, ordered by inclusion, form a complete lattice. The meet of a family is the intersection, and the join is the subring generated by the union.

The definition of $\langle s \rangle$ as an intersection does not say what its elements look like. The next theorem does.

**Theorem (Elements of the generated subring).** Let $s \subseteq R$. Let $M$ be the set of all finite products $x_1 x_2 \cdots x_k$ with $k \ge 0$ and $x_j \in s$, where the empty product is $1$. Then $\langle s \rangle$ is the set $T$ of all finite sums $\pm m_1 \pm m_2 \pm \dots \pm m_r$ with $r \ge 0$ and $m_j \in M$.

*Proof.* First, $T \subseteq \langle s \rangle$. Each subring that contains $s$ contains each product of elements of $s$, since it is closed under products and contains $1$. So it contains $M$. It is closed under sums and negatives, so it contains $T$. Hence $T$ is contained in the intersection $\langle s \rangle$.

Second, $T$ is a subring that contains $s$. It contains $s$, since $M$ does, and $1 \in M \subseteq T$. A difference of two elements of $T$ is again a signed sum of elements of $M$, so it is in $T$. The product of $\pm m_1 \pm \dots \pm m_r$ and $\pm n_1 \pm \dots \pm n_q$ is, by the distributive laws and the rule of signs, the signed sum of the $r q$ products $m_i n_j$. Each $m_i n_j$ is a product of elements of $s$, so it is in $M$. Hence the product is in $T$. By the subring test $T$ is a subring, and so $\langle s \rangle \subseteq T$. $\square$

If $R$ is commutative, a product of elements of $s$ can be reordered. For $s = \{\alpha\}$ the theorem gives
$$\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] = \{ a_0 + a_1 \alpha + \dots + a_n \alpha^n : n \ge 0,\ a_j \in \mathbb{Z} \},$$
the set of the values at $\alpha$ of the polynomials with integer coefficients.

**Theorem (Images and preimages).** Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism. If $A$ is a subring of $R$, then $f(A)$ is a subring of $S$. If $B$ is a subring of $S$, then $f^{-1}(B)$ is a subring of $R$. In particular the range $f(R)$ is a subring of $S$.

*Proof.* Image: $1 = f(1) \in f(A)$. For $f(a), f(b) \in f(A)$ with $a, b \in A$, $f(a) - f(b) = f(a - b)$ and $f(a) f(b) = f(a b)$ are in $f(A)$, since $a - b, a b \in A$. Preimage: $f(1) = 1 \in B$, so $1 \in f^{-1}(B)$. If $f(a), f(b) \in B$, then $f(a - b) = f(a) - f(b) \in B$ and $f(a b) = f(a) f(b) \in B$. The subring test applies in both cases. $\square$

::: mathlib

**Theorem (Agreement on generators).** Let $f, g : R \to S$ be ring homomorphisms and let $s \subseteq R$. If $f(x) = g(x)$ for each $x \in s$, then $f(x) = g(x)$ for each $x \in \langle s \rangle$.

*Proof.* The set $E = \{ x \in R : f(x) = g(x) \}$ is a subring. It contains $1$, and if $x, y \in E$, then $f(x - y) = f(x) - f(y) = g(x) - g(y) = g(x - y)$ and in the same way $f(x y) = g(x y)$. By hypothesis $s \subseteq E$. So $\langle s \rangle \subseteq E$. $\square$

::: mathlib

**Theorem (The prime subring).** The smallest subring of a ring $R$ is the image of the unique homomorphism $\chi : \mathbb{Z} \to R$, that is, $\{ n \cdot 1_R : n \in \mathbb{Z} \}$.

*Proof.* The image of $\chi$ is a subring by the theorem on images. Each subring contains $1_R$ and is closed under sums and negatives, so it contains each $n \cdot 1_R$. $\square$

::: mathlib

**Theorem (The center is a subring).** For each ring $R$, $Z(R)$ is a commutative subring of $R$.

*Proof.* $1 a = a = a 1$, so $1 \in Z(R)$. Let $z, w \in Z(R)$ and $a \in R$. Then $(z - w) a = z a - w a = a z - a w = a (z - w)$, by the distributive laws. And $(z w) a = z (w a) = z (a w) = (z a) w = (a z) w = a (z w)$, by associativity. So $z - w, z w \in Z(R)$. The ring $Z(R)$ is commutative, since its elements commute with all elements of $R$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The Gaussian integers).** Let $i \in \mathbb{C}$ with $i^2 = -1$. By the description of $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, the subring $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ consists of the values $a_0 + a_1 i + a_2 i^2 + \dots$ with integer $a_j$. Since $i^2 = -1$, $i^3 = -i$ and $i^4 = 1$, each such value is $a + b i$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$. Conversely each $a + b i$ is such a value. So
$$\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{ a + b i : a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \}.$$
It is closed under the product by the formula $(a + b i)(c + d i) = (a c - b d) + (a d + b c) i$. Chapter 6 studies this ring in detail.

**Example (A generated subring of $\mathbb{Q}$).** The subring $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ of $\mathbb{Q}$ generated by $1/2$ is the set of the fractions $a / 2^k$ with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $k \ge 0$. Each value $a_0 + a_1/2 + \dots + a_n/2^n$ has the form $a/2^n$, with $a = a_0 2^n + a_1 2^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$. Conversely $a/2^k = a \cdot (1/2)^k$. The element $1/3$ is not in $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$: from $1/3 = a/2^k$ follows $2^k = 3 a$, which is impossible, as $3 \nmid 2^k$.

**Example (Real quadratic integers).** The subring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt 2]$ of $\mathbb{R}$ is $\{ a + b \sqrt 2 : a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \}$, by the same argument as for $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, with $(\sqrt 2)^2 = 2$. The product is $(a + b \sqrt 2)(c + d \sqrt 2) = (a c + 2 b d) + (a d + b c) \sqrt 2$. For example $(1 + \sqrt 2)(-1 + \sqrt 2) = -1 + 2 = 1$, so $1 + \sqrt 2$ has an inverse in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt 2]$.

**Counterexample (A ring inside a ring that is not a subring).** In $R = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, the set $A = \{ (a, 0) : a \in \mathbb{Z} \}$ is closed under the sum, the negative and the product. With these operations it is a ring with unit $(1, 0)$, isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$. But it does not contain the unit $(1, 1)$ of $R$, so it is not a subring. The inclusion $A \to R$ keeps sums and products, but not the unit.

**Example (The center of the matrix ring).** Let $R = M_2(\mathbb{R})$ and let $E_{jk}$ be the matrix with $1$ at the place $(j, k)$ and $0$ elsewhere. Let $Z = \begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ r & s \end{pmatrix}$ be central. Then
$$E_{11} Z = \begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z E_{11} = \begin{pmatrix} p & 0 \\ r & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
so $q = r = 0$. Next,
$$E_{12} Z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & s \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z E_{12} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & p \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
so $s = p$. Hence $Z = p I$ is a scalar matrix. Conversely each scalar matrix commutes with every matrix. So $Z(M_2(\mathbb{R})) = \{ p I : p \in \mathbb{R} \}$, a subring isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$.

**Example (Prime subrings).** The prime subring of $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{Z}$. The prime subring of $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is $\mathbb{Z}/n$ itself, since $[k] = k \cdot [1]$. The prime subring of $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/3$ is the whole ring too, since this ring is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/6$.

### The library around it

The subobjects of Mathlib follow one pattern. For each structure there is a type of subobjects (`Submonoid`, `AddSubgroup`, `Subsemiring`, `Subring`, `Subfield`, `Subalgebra`), a `SetLike` instance for the membership, a complete lattice of the subobjects, a `closure`, a `map` and a `comap` for homomorphisms, and a `range` of a homomorphism. The lemmas carry the same names in each case: `mem_closure`, `closure_le`, `closure_induction`, `mem_map`, `mem_comap`, `map_map`, `comap_comap`, `mem_top`, `mem_bot`, `mem_inf`, `mem_sInf`. A subring is also a submonoid and an additive subgroup, by `S.toSubsemiring` and `S.toAddSubgroup`, so the lemmas of Volume 5 apply to it.

The lemmas for the closure properties of a subring are the generic `add_mem`, `neg_mem`, `sub_mem`, `mul_mem`, `pow_mem`, `one_mem`, `zero_mem`, which apply to each `SetLike` type with the matching class. So a proof writes `mul_mem ha hb` for `a * b ∈ S`, with no reference to `Subring`.

The module `Mathlib.Algebra.Ring.Subsemiring.Basic` builds the same API for subsemirings, for use in `ℕ` and other semirings without negation. It has the complete lattice of subsemirings, `Subsemiring.closure` with `Subsemiring.closure_induction` and `Subsemiring.mem_closure_iff`, which describes the closure as the additive closure of the generated submonoid, and the images and preimages `Subsemiring.map` and `Subsemiring.comap`. The range of a ring homomorphism into a semiring is `RingHom.rangeS`, with the suffix `S` to tell it apart from `RingHom.range`, and `RingHom.rangeSRestrict` is the surjective homomorphism onto it. The center and the centralizers are `Subsemiring.center` and `Subsemiring.centralizer`, with `Subsemiring.mem_center_iff` and `Subsemiring.center.commSemiring`. The module also has products, `Subsemiring.prod` with `Subsemiring.prodEquiv`, the inclusion `Subsemiring.inclusion` of a smaller subsemiring in a larger one, and the scalar actions of a subsemiring on any type on which the big semiring acts, such as `Subsemiring.module`.

The element lemmas behind the center come in separate modules. `Mathlib.Algebra.Ring.Center` shows that the center of a ring is closed under sums and negatives and contains the casts of integers, with `Set.add_mem_center`, `Set.neg_mem_center`, `Set.natCast_mem_center` and `Set.intCast_mem_center`. `Mathlib.Algebra.Ring.Centralizer` gives the same closure lemmas for a centralizer, `Set.add_mem_centralizer` and `Set.neg_mem_centralizer`. `Mathlib.Algebra.Ring.Submonoid.Basic` shows that the additive closure of a multiplicatively closed set is closed under products, with `MulMemClass.mul_mem_add_closure`, which is the key step in the description of the generated subsemiring. `Mathlib.Algebra.Ring.Subring.Units` defines the subgroup `Units.posSubgroup R` of positive units in an ordered ring, and proves that an element of the equalizer `RingHom.eqLocus f g` is a unit there if and only if it is a unit in the big ring, by `RingHom.isUnit_eqLocus_mk_iff`. `Mathlib.Algebra.Ring.Subring.IntPolynomial` turns a polynomial over a field $K$ whose coefficients lie in a subring $R$ into a polynomial over $R$, with `Polynomial.int`, and proves that the degree, the leading coefficient and monicity do not change, with `Polynomial.int_natDegree`, `Polynomial.int_leadingCoeff_eq` and `Polynomial.int_monic_iff`.

Common errors of a learner:

- `S : Subring R` is not a type. The type of its elements is `↥S`, and an element `x : ↥S` has the value `(x : R)`. A goal about `x * y` in `↥S` reduces to `R` with `Subring.coe_mul`, usually by `simp` or `norm_cast`.
- `Subring.closure s` is not the set of polynomial values in a noncommutative ring. Use `closure_induction` and not the explicit description.
- `Subring` needs a ring. For `ℕ` use `Subsemiring`.
- The intersection of two subrings is `S ⊓ T`, not `S ∩ T`. The coercion `↑(S ⊓ T) = ↑S ∩ ↑T` is `Subring.coe_inf`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\{ a + b \sqrt{-3} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \}$ and $\{ a + b \omega : a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \}$, with $\omega = (-1 + \sqrt{-3})/2$, are subrings of $\mathbb{C}$. Which one contains the other? Hint: $\omega^2 = -1 - \omega$.
2. (A) Show that the set of upper triangular matrices is a subring of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$, and find its center.
3. (A) Show that the union of two subrings of $\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$ need not be a subring. Hint: consider the subrings $\{(a, b) : a \equiv b \bmod 2\}$ and $\{(a, b) : a \equiv b \bmod 3\}$.
4. (A) Find the subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ generated by $\{1/2, 1/3\}$, and show that it equals the subring generated by $1/6$.
5. (B) Show that every subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ that contains $1/p$ for each prime $p$ is $\mathbb{Q}$.
6. (B) Show that the continuous functions form a subring of $\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}}$, and that the functions with compact support form a non-unital subring that is not a subring.
7. (B) Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism and $s \subseteq R$. Show that $f(\langle s \rangle) = \langle f(s) \rangle$. Hint: the inclusion one way is the theorem on images; the other uses the theorem on agreement or the description by sums of products.
8. (B) Show that $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt 2]$ is not generated by any element $a + b\sqrt 2$ with $|b| \ge 2$. Hint: each element of $\mathbb{Z}[a + b\sqrt 2]$ has the form $c + d b \sqrt 2$.
9. (C) Show that the center of the ring $M_n(R)$, for a commutative ring $R$, is the set of scalar matrices $r I$. Hint: use the matrices $E_{jk}$ as in the example.
10. (C) Show that the subrings of $\mathbb{Q}$ are exactly the rings $\mathbb{Z}[1/p : p \in P]$ for the sets $P$ of primes, and that different $P$ give different subrings. Hint: if $a/b \in A$ in lowest terms, Bezout gives $1/b \in A$.

### Notes and history

The generated subring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ for an algebraic number $\alpha$ appears in the work of Kummer and Dedekind in the nineteenth century, as the ring of the values of integer polynomials at $\alpha$. Dedekind saw that $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is often smaller than the ring of all algebraic integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, as in Exercise 1, where $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ is strictly smaller than $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$. The standard references are Dummit and Foote, *Abstract Algebra*, Section 7.1, and Jacobson, *Basic Algebra I*, Chapter 2.

In Mathlib the file `Mathlib/Algebra/Ring/Subring/Basic.lean` has its origin in the Lean 3 file of Ashvni Narayanan (2020), which followed the earlier files on subgroups and subsemirings. The `SetLike` class, which gives the membership and the generic lemmas, came in 2021.
