## The primitive element theorem

### Overview

An extension $L/K$ is simple if $L = K(\gamma)$ for one element $\gamma$, and $\gamma$ is then a primitive element. A simple finite extension is the easiest kind to compute with: by Chapter 2, $L \cong K[X]/(m)$ for the minimal polynomial $m$ of $\gamma$, and $1, \gamma, \dots, \gamma^{n-1}$ is a basis. The primitive element theorem says that each finite separable extension is simple. So $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$, which is given by two generators, is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$.

The section proves the theorem twice. The first proof is constructive: for two generators $\alpha$ and $\beta$, all but finitely many $c \in K$ make $\alpha + c\beta$ a primitive element. The second proof goes through the theorem of Steinitz, which characterizes the simple extensions as those with finitely many intermediate fields. The section ends with an extension of degree $p^2$ in characteristic $p$ that is not simple, so the hypothesis of separability is needed.

The section uses separable polynomials (Section 1), the minimal polynomial and the structure of $K(\alpha)$ (Chapter 2), and splitting fields (Chapter 3). The last proof uses that a finite subgroup of the units of a field is cyclic (Volume 6, Chapter 5).

### Definitions

**Definition (Simple extension, primitive element).** An extension $L/K$ is simple if there is $\gamma \in L$ with $L = K(\gamma)$. Such a $\gamma$ is a primitive element of $L/K$.

For a finite extension, $\gamma$ is a primitive element if and only if the degree of its minimal polynomial is $[L:K]$: the field $K(\gamma)$ has the degree $\deg m_\gamma$ over $K$, and a subspace of $L$ of dimension $[L:K]$ is all of $L$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (Two generators).** Let $K$ be an infinite field and $L = K(\alpha, \beta)$ a finite extension, with $\beta$ separable over $K$. Then for all but finitely many $c \in K$, $L = K(\alpha + c\beta)$.

*Proof.* Let $f$ and $g$ be the minimal polynomials of $\alpha$ and of $\beta$ over $K$, and let $\Omega$ be an extension of $L$ in which $f g$ splits. Let $\alpha = \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r$ be the roots of $f$ in $\Omega$ and $\beta = \beta_1, \dots, \beta_s$ the roots of $g$. The $\beta_j$ are distinct, since $g$ is separable.

For $j \ge 2$, $\beta_j \neq \beta$, so for each pair $(i, j)$ with $j \ge 2$ there is at most one $c \in K$ with $\alpha_i + c\beta_j = \alpha + c\beta$, namely $c = (\alpha_i - \alpha)/(\beta - \beta_j)$ if this lies in $K$. Exclude these at most $r(s - 1)$ values. For each other $c$ put $\gamma = \alpha + c\beta$ and $E = K(\gamma) \subseteq L$. It suffices to show $\beta \in E$, since then $\alpha = \gamma - c\beta \in E$ and $E = L$.

Consider $h(X) = f(\gamma - cX) \in E[X]$. Then $h(\beta) = f(\alpha) = 0$, so $\beta$ is a common root of $g$ and $h$. Let $\beta_j$ be any common root. Then $f(\gamma - c\beta_j) = 0$, so $\gamma - c\beta_j = \alpha_i$ for some $i$, that is, $\alpha + c\beta = \alpha_i + c\beta_j$. By the choice of $c$, $j = 1$. So $\beta$ is the only common root of $g$ and $h$ in $\Omega$, and it is a simple root of $g$. Since $g$ splits in $\Omega$, the monic $\gcd(g, h)$ in $\Omega[X]$ is $X - \beta$.

The gcd of two polynomials does not change under an extension of fields: the Euclidean algorithm (Volume 6, Chapter 6) runs inside $E[X]$ and gives the same result in $\Omega[X]$. So $X - \beta = \gcd(g, h)$ has its coefficients in $E$, and $\beta \in E$. $\square$

The proof uses the separability of $\beta$ only, not of $\alpha$. This matters in Exercise 6.

**Theorem (Primitive element theorem).** Let $L/K$ be a finite separable extension. Then $L/K$ is simple.

*Proof.* First let $K$ be finite. Then $L$ is finite, and its group of units $L^\times$ is a finite subgroup of the units of a field. By Volume 6, Chapter 5, it is cyclic. Let $\gamma$ be a generator. Each nonzero element of $L$ is a power of $\gamma$, so $L = K(\gamma)$. This case needs no separability.

Now let $K$ be infinite. Since $L/K$ is finite, $L = K(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$, for example with a basis. Use induction on $n$. For $n = 1$ there is nothing to prove. For $n \ge 2$, the induction hypothesis applied to $K(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{n-1})$, which is separable over $K$ as a subfield of $L$, gives $\delta$ with $K(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{n-1}) = K(\delta)$. Then $L = K(\delta, \alpha_n)$, and $\alpha_n$ is separable. The lemma gives $\gamma$ with $L = K(\gamma)$. $\square$

**Theorem (Steinitz).** Let $L/K$ be a finite extension. Then $L/K$ is simple if and only if there are only finitely many fields $M$ with $K \subseteq M \subseteq L$.

*Proof.* Let $L = K(\alpha)$ with minimal polynomial $f$. For an intermediate field $M$, let $f_M \in M[X]$ be the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $M$, and let $M_0$ be the field generated over $K$ by the coefficients of $f_M$. Then $M_0 \subseteq M$. The polynomial $f_M$ lies in $M_0[X]$ and is irreducible over $M$, so it is irreducible over the smaller field $M_0$, and it is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $M_0$. So
$$[L : M_0] = [M_0(\alpha) : M_0] = \deg f_M = [L : M].$$
With $M_0 \subseteq M$ and the tower law, $[M : M_0] = 1$, so $M = M_0$. So $M$ is determined by $f_M$. Each $f_M$ is a monic divisor of $f$ in $L[X]$. Since $L[X]$ is a unique factorization domain, $f$ has only finitely many monic divisors: the products of subfamilies of its monic irreducible factors. So there are only finitely many $M$.

Conversely, suppose that there are only finitely many intermediate fields. If $K$ is finite, so is $L$, and the first case of the primitive element theorem applies. Let $K$ be infinite, and let $\alpha, \beta \in L$. The fields $K(\alpha + c\beta)$ for $c \in K$ are intermediate fields, so two of them agree: $K(\alpha + c\beta) = K(\alpha + c'\beta) = E$ with $c \neq c'$. Then $E$ contains $(c - c')\beta$, so $\beta$, and then $\alpha$. So $K(\alpha, \beta) = E$ is simple. By induction on the number of generators, as in the theorem, $L$ is simple. $\square$

### Examples

**Example ($\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3)$).** Let $\theta = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3$. Compute the powers:
$$\theta^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt 6, \qquad \theta^3 = \theta \theta^2 = 5\sqrt 2 + 5\sqrt 3 + 2\sqrt{12} + 2\sqrt{18} = 11\sqrt 2 + 9\sqrt 3.$$
So $\theta^3 - 9\theta = 2\sqrt 2$ and $11\theta - \theta^3 = 2\sqrt 3$, that is,
$$\sqrt 2 = \tfrac12(\theta^3 - 9\theta), \qquad \sqrt 3 = \tfrac12(11\theta - \theta^3).$$
Both generators lie in $\mathbb{Q}(\theta)$, and $\theta \in \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt 3)$, so the two fields are equal. The degree is $4$ (Chapter 1), so the minimal polynomial of $\theta$ has degree $4$. From $\theta^4 = (5 + 2\sqrt 6)^2 = 49 + 20\sqrt 6$ and $10\theta^2 = 50 + 20\sqrt 6$, it is $X^4 - 10X^2 + 1$. In the lemma, with $\alpha = \sqrt 2$ and $\beta = \sqrt 3$, the roots are $\pm\sqrt 2$ and $\pm \sqrt 3$. The bad value of $c$ for $(i, j) = (1, 2)$ is $c = 0$, and for $(i, j) = (2, 2)$ it is $c = -2\sqrt 2/(2\sqrt 3) \notin \mathbb{Q}$. So each $c \neq 0$ in $\mathbb{Q}$ gives a primitive element $\sqrt 2 + c\sqrt 3$.

**Example (Separability is needed).** Let $k = \mathbb{F}_p$, $L = k(s, t)$ with two independent variables, and $K = k(s^p, t^p)$. The degree $[L:K]$ is $p^2$, with the basis $s^i t^j$ for $0 \le i, j < p$. Each $\gamma \in L$ satisfies $\gamma^p \in K$, since $\gamma^p$ is a rational function in $s^p$ and $t^p$. So $\gamma$ is a root of $X^p - \gamma^p \in K[X]$, and $[K(\gamma):K] \le p < p^2$. No element generates $L$, so $L/K$ is not simple. By Steinitz's theorem there are infinitely many intermediate fields. Explicitly, the fields $K(s + ct)$ for $c \in K$ are pairwise distinct: if $K(s + ct) = K(s + c't)$ with $c \neq c'$, the argument of the proof puts $s$ and $t$ in a field of degree at most $p$ over $K$, which is false.

### The library around it

The file `Mathlib.FieldTheory.PrimitiveElement` also has `Field.primitive_element_iff_algHom_eq_of_eval`: $\alpha$ is a primitive element if and only if each $K$-embedding into a large field is determined by the image of $\alpha$. The file `Mathlib.FieldTheory.LinearDisjoint` treats a related notion. Two intermediate fields $A$ and $B$ of $L/K$ are linearly disjoint if a family of $A$ that is linearly independent over $K$ stays linearly independent over $B$. For finite extensions this gives $[AB : K] = [A:K][B:K]$, which is `IntermediateField.LinearDisjoint.finrank_sup`, and coprime degrees give linear disjointness, `IntermediateField.LinearDisjoint.of_finrank_coprime`. The degree of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \sqrt[3]{2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, which is $6$, is an instance. The reference table lists the declarations of the section.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find a primitive element of $\mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt 2)/\mathbb{Q}$ and its minimal polynomial.
2. (A) Express $\sqrt 2$ and $\sqrt[3]{2}$ as polynomials in $\gamma = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt[3]{2}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, or show that $[\mathbb{Q}(\gamma):\mathbb{Q}] = 6$.
3. (B) Show that $L = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2})$ has exactly three intermediate fields over $\mathbb{Q}$: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$ and $L$. Hint: with $a = \sqrt[4]{2}$, $X^4 - 2 = (X - a)(X + a)(X^2 + a^2)$ over $L$; list the monic divisors and the fields generated by their coefficients, as in the proof of Steinitz's theorem.
4. (A) Let $L$ be a finite field. Show that a primitive element of $L/\mathbb{F}_p$ need not generate the group $L^\times$. Hint: in $\mathbb{F}_9 = \mathbb{F}_3(i)$ the element $i$ has order $4$.
5. (B) Show that each extension of degree $2$ is simple, without separability.
6. (B) Show that $L = K(\alpha, \beta)$ is simple if $\beta$ is separable, even if $\alpha$ is not. Conclude that a finite extension with at most one inseparable generator is simple.
7. (B) Prove that an extension with finitely many intermediate fields has only finitely many intermediate fields in each subextension, and use it to show that each subextension of a simple finite extension is simple.

### Notes and history

A form of the theorem for subfields of $\mathbb{C}$ is in the work of Galois, who used a primitive element of the splitting field of a polynomial to define its group. Steinitz proved the characterization by intermediate fields in his paper of 1910.
