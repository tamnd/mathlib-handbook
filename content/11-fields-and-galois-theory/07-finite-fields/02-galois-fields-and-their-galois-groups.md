## Galois fields and their Galois groups

### Overview

Section 1 showed that a field with $q$ elements is a splitting field of $X^q - X$ over its prime field. This section turns the statement around. For each prime power $q = p^n$, the splitting field of $X^q - X$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ has exactly $q$ elements, so a field with $q$ elements exists, and by the uniqueness of splitting fields it is unique up to isomorphism. Its Galois group over $\mathbb{F}_p$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by the Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^p$, and the fundamental theorem then lists its subfields: one field $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$.

The same facts count the irreducible polynomials of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$. The section works out $\mathbb{F}_8$ in full and ends with the theorem of Wedderburn: a finite division ring is a field.

The section uses Chapters 3 to 5, the Frobenius map (Volume 6, Chapter 4) and the class equation (Volume 5, Chapter 6).

### Definitions

**Definition (Galois field).** For a prime $p$ and $n \geq 1$, $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ is the splitting field of $X^{p^n} - X$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$.

For `n = 0` it is the junk case `ZMod p`. For an arbitrary finite field `k`, `FiniteField.Extension k p n` has degree `n` (`FiniteField.finrank_extension`).

**Definition (Frobenius automorphism).** Let $F$ be a finite field with $q$ elements and $L$ a finite extension of $F$. The Frobenius map of $L/F$ is $\varphi(x) = x^q$.

It is a power of the Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^p$ (Volume 6, Chapter 4), so an injective ring homomorphism of the finite field $L$, and it fixes $F$ by Section 1.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Existence).** Let $q = p^n$ with $n \geq 1$. The splitting field $L$ of $X^q - X$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ has exactly $q$ elements, and it is the set of the roots of $X^q - X$.

*Proof.* The derivative of $f = X^q - X$ is $qX^{q-1} - 1 = -1$, since $q = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$. So $f$ and $f'$ are coprime, $f$ is separable, and it has $q$ distinct roots in $L$ (Chapter 4, Section 1). Let $R \subseteq L$ be the set of the roots. The map $\psi(x) = x^q$ is the $n$th power of the Frobenius map, so it is a ring homomorphism, and $R$ is the set of the fixed points of $\psi$. The fixed points of a ring homomorphism of a field form a subfield, so $R$ is a subfield of $L$ that contains $\mathbb{F}_p$ and all the roots of $f$. Since $L$ is generated by these roots, $L = R$, and $|L| = q$. $\square$

**Theorem (Uniqueness).** Two finite fields with the same number of elements are isomorphic.

*Proof.* Let $|F| = |F'| = q = p^n$. Both have characteristic $p$, so both contain $\mathbb{F}_p$. By Section 1, each is a splitting field of $X^q - X$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$. Splitting fields of one polynomial are isomorphic (Chapter 3). $\square$

**Theorem (Galois group).** Let $F$ have $q$ elements and $[L:F] = n$. Then $L/F$ is Galois, and $\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by the Frobenius $\varphi(x) = x^q$.

*Proof.* $L$ has $q^n$ elements, so it is the splitting field over $F$ of the separable polynomial $X^{q^n} - X$, and $L/F$ is Galois (Chapter 5, Section 2), with $|\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)| = n$. The power $\varphi^k$ is $x \mapsto x^{q^k}$. If $1 \leq k < n$ and $\varphi^k = 1$, then all $q^n$ elements of $L$ are roots of $X^{q^k} - X$, which has at most $q^k < q^n$ roots. So $\varphi$ has order exactly $n$, and it generates the group of order $n$. $\square$

**Corollary (Subfields).** Let $|L| = p^n$. For each $d \mid n$, $L$ has exactly one subfield with $p^d$ elements, namely $\{x \in L : x^{p^d} = x\}$, and it has no other subfields. A finite field $K$ embeds into $L$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ if and only if $[K : \mathbb{F}_p]$ divides $n$.

*Proof.* The group $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{F}_p)$ is cyclic of order $n$, generated by $\varphi(x) = x^p$, so it has exactly one subgroup of each order $e \mid n$, namely $\langle \varphi^{n/e} \rangle$ (Volume 5). By the fundamental theorem its fixed field $M$ has degree $n/e = d$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, so $|M| = p^d$, and $M$ is the set of the fixed points of $\varphi^d$, that is, of the $x$ with $x^{p^d} = x$. Each subfield arises in this way. The embedding statement follows from the tower law and uniqueness. $\square$

**Theorem (Irreducible polynomials).** Let $F$ have $q$ elements and $n \geq 1$. Then $X^{q^n} - X$ is the product of all the monic irreducible polynomials in $F[X]$ whose degree divides $n$. If $N_d$ is the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree $d$, then
$$q^n = \sum_{d \mid n} d\, N_d, \qquad N_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d)\, q^{n/d},$$
where $\mu$ is the Möbius function. In particular $N_n \geq 1$.

*Proof.* Let $L$ be the field with $q^n$ elements containing $F$. Let $g \in F[X]$ be monic irreducible of degree $d$. If $d \mid n$, then a root $\alpha$ of $g$ generates a field $F(\alpha)$ with $q^d$ elements, which embeds into $L$ by the corollary, so $g$ has a root in $L$ and, being the minimal polynomial of it, divides $X^{q^n} - X$. Conversely, if $g$ divides $X^{q^n} - X$, it has a root $\alpha \in L$, and $d = [F(\alpha):F]$ divides $[L:F] = n$. Since $X^{q^n} - X$ is separable, it has no repeated irreducible factor, so it is the product of the $g$ with $\deg g \mid n$. Comparing degrees gives $q^n = \sum_{d \mid n} d N_d$, and Möbius inversion gives the formula for $N_n$. For the bound, let $n \geq 2$ and $m = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$. The terms with $d > 1$ have $n/d \leq m$, so their sum has absolute value at most $\sum_{k=0}^{m} q^k < q^{m+1} \leq q^n$. So $n N_n > 0$; for $n = 1$, $N_1 = q$. $\square$

**Theorem (Wedderburn).** Each finite division ring is commutative.

*Proof.* Let $D$ be a finite division ring with center $Z$. The center is a commutative division ring, so a field, with $q$ elements, and $D$ is a vector space over $Z$ of some dimension $n$, so $|D| = q^n$. Suppose $n > 1$.

For $x \in D$, the centralizer $C(x) = \{y : xy = yx\}$ is a division ring that contains $Z$, so $|C(x)| = q^{d}$ for some $d$. Also $D$ is a vector space over $C(x)$, acting by left multiplication, so $q^n = (q^{d})^m$ and $d \mid n$; and $d < n$ when $x \notin Z$. The class equation of the group $D^\times$ (Volume 5, Chapter 6) reads
$$q^n - 1 = (q - 1) + \sum_{x} \frac{q^n - 1}{q^{d(x)} - 1},$$
with one term for each conjugacy class of $D^\times$ outside $Z^\times$, and $d(x) \mid n$, $d(x) < n$.

Let $\Phi_n = \prod (X - \zeta) \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, the product over the primitive $n$th roots of unity $\zeta \in \mathbb{C}$. Then $X^n - 1 = \prod_{e \mid n} \Phi_e$, and these polynomials have integer coefficients (Chapter 8, Section 1). For $d \mid n$ with $d < n$, $X^d - 1 = \prod_{e \mid d} \Phi_e$ does not contain the factor $\Phi_n$, so $\Phi_n$ divides $(X^n - 1)/(X^d - 1)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. So the integer $\Phi_n(q)$ divides $q^n - 1$ and each term of the sum, and so it divides $q - 1$.

But for each primitive $n$th root $\zeta \neq 1$, $|q - \zeta| > q - 1$, since $\zeta$ lies on the unit circle and is not $1$, and $q \geq 2$. So $|\Phi_n(q)| = \prod |q - \zeta| > q - 1 \geq 1$, which contradicts $\Phi_n(q) \mid q - 1$. So $n = 1$ and $D = Z$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (The field with eight elements).** Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, the formula gives $N_3 = (2^3 - 2)/3 = 2$. The two irreducible cubics are $X^3 + X + 1$ and $X^3 + X^2 + 1$; the other six monic cubics have a root $0$ or $1$. Let $\mathbb{F}_8 = \mathbb{F}_2[X]/(X^3 + X + 1)$ and $\alpha$ the class of $X$, so $\alpha^3 = \alpha + 1$. The powers of $\alpha$ are
$$\alpha^3 = \alpha + 1,\ \alpha^4 = \alpha^2 + \alpha,\ \alpha^5 = \alpha^2 + \alpha + 1,\ \alpha^6 = \alpha^2 + 1,\ \alpha^7 = 1.$$
So $\alpha$ generates the group $\mathbb{F}_8^\times$ of order $7$. The Frobenius $\varphi(x) = x^2$ maps $\alpha \mapsto \alpha^2 \mapsto \alpha^4 \mapsto \alpha^8 = \alpha$, and it has order $3 = [\mathbb{F}_8 : \mathbb{F}_2]$. The roots of $X^3 + X + 1$ are $\alpha, \alpha^2, \alpha^4$, one orbit, and the roots of $X^3 + X^2 + 1$ are $\alpha^3, \alpha^6, \alpha^5$, the inverses. So $X^8 - X = X(X+1)(X^3 + X + 1)(X^3 + X^2 + 1)$. The trace $x + x^2 + x^4$ is $1$ on $1$ and $0$ on $\alpha$. Since $3$ is prime, $\mathbb{F}_8$ has only the subfields $\mathbb{F}_2$ and $\mathbb{F}_8$; it does not contain $\mathbb{F}_4$.

**Example (Division rings that are not finite).** The quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ form a division ring of dimension $4$ over its center $\mathbb{R}$, and $ij = -ji$, so Wedderburn's theorem needs finiteness.

### Exercises

1. (A) List the three monic irreducible polynomials of degree $2$ over $\mathbb{F}_3$, and check $N_2 = (9 - 3)/2$.
2. (A) Which of $\mathbb{F}_4$, $\mathbb{F}_8$, $\mathbb{F}_{16}$, $\mathbb{F}_{32}$, $\mathbb{F}_{64}$ are subfields of $\mathbb{F}_{64}$? Draw the lattice.
3. (A) Compute $N_4$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, and list the irreducible quartics.
4. (B) Show that $X^4 + X + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_2$, and that a root generates $\mathbb{F}_{16}^\times$. Show that $X^4 + X^3 + X^2 + X + 1$ is irreducible, but that its roots have order $5$.
5. (B) Let $L/F$ be an extension of finite fields of degree $n$. Show that the norm $N(x) = x^{(q^n - 1)/(q-1)}$ on $L^\times$ is surjective onto $F^\times$.
6. (C) Let $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$ be an algebraic closure. Show that $\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p/\mathbb{F}_p)$ is the profinite completion $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ of $\mathbb{Z}$, topologically generated by the Frobenius, and that the subgroup generated by the Frobenius is not closed.

### Notes and history

Galois constructed the finite fields beyond $\mathbb{F}_p$ in 1830. E. H. Moore proved in 1893 that each finite field is one of them. The count of irreducible polynomials goes back to Gauss. Wedderburn proved his theorem in 1905; the proof with the class equation and cyclotomic polynomials is due to Witt (1931).
