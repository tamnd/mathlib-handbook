# Complex and harmonic analysis

This volume teaches the analysis of functions of a complex variable and the Fourier analysis of functions on the circle, on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and on finite abelian groups, together with their form in Mathlib. The two subjects share one idea: a function is studied through its values on a circle or through its frequencies, and an integral against a well chosen kernel recovers it. The Cauchy kernel $1/(w - z)$, the Poisson kernel and the characters $e^{2\pi i n x}$ are the three kernels of the volume.

Each main theorem has a proof on paper: the theorem of Cauchy-Goursat, the Cauchy integral formula, the analyticity of holomorphic functions, Liouville's theorem and the fundamental theorem of algebra, the maximum modulus principle, the open mapping theorem, the Schwarz lemma, the removable singularity theorem of Riemann, the mean value property and the Poisson formula for harmonic functions, the theorem of Phragmén-Lindelöf, the formula of Parseval, the lemma of Riemann-Lebesgue, the Fourier inversion formula, the Poisson summation formula, and the theorem of Bohr-Mollerup on the Gamma function. The examples compute: $\int_{|z| = 1} dz/z = 2\pi i$, the Fourier series of a sawtooth and the sum $\sum_{n \ge 1} 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$, the transform of a Gaussian, the discrete Fourier transform of a short vector, and $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$.

Chapter 1 sets up the complex plane as a complete normed field, and the complex exponential, the trigonometric functions and the argument. Chapter 2 defines holomorphic functions by complex differentiability, derives the Cauchy-Riemann equations, proves the theorem of Cauchy-Goursat and the Cauchy integral formula, and deduces that a holomorphic function is analytic. Chapter 3 draws the consequences: the Cauchy estimates, Liouville, the fundamental theorem of algebra, the maximum modulus principle, the open mapping theorem, the Schwarz lemma, removable singularities and the locally uniform limits of holomorphic functions. Chapter 4 studies meromorphic functions, the order of a zero or a pole, divisors, the logarithmic derivative and the formula of Jensen, the first steps of Nevanlinna theory, and the Weierstrass function $\wp$. Chapter 5 studies harmonic functions in the plane: their mean value property, the Poisson integral, and the maximum principles of Phragmén-Lindelöf and Hadamard on unbounded domains. Chapter 6 develops Fourier series on the circle $\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}$ and Fourier analysis on finite abelian groups. Chapter 7 develops the Fourier transform on $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Schwartz space, the inversion and summation formulas, and the distributions as far as Mathlib has them. Chapter 8 studies the Gamma and Beta functions, the Mellin transform, and the hypergeometric and Bessel functions. Chapter 9 describes the unit disc and the upper half-plane as types, the Möbius action of $\mathrm{GL}(2, \mathbb{R})$, the hyperbolic metric, the invariant measure and the expansion at the cusp. Chapters 1 to 3 are the core of a first course in complex analysis. Chapter 6 and the first three sections of Chapter 7 are the core of a first course in Fourier analysis, and they can be read after Chapter 1 alone.

The volume needs Volume 17 for the derivative in normed spaces and, above all, Chapter 7 of that volume: power series, analytic functions, the isolated zeros and the identity theorem are proved there and used here without a new proof. It needs Volume 18 for the Lebesgue and Bochner integrals, dominated convergence, Fubini, the spaces $L^p$ and the integral over an interval, and Volume 16 for Banach and Hilbert spaces, Hilbert bases and the abstract theorem of Parseval. Volume 15 supplies the real exponential, logarithm and trigonometric functions and the number $\pi$, and Volume 13 supplies connectedness and compactness. The Riemann zeta function, its functional equation and its special values have their home in Volume 12 on number theory. This volume proves the analytic tools that they need and points to the statements.

Mathlib calls a holomorphic function `DifferentiableOn ℂ f U`: there is no separate word, and the analytic structure comes from the general theory of Volume 17. Path integrals are mostly integrals over circles, `∮ z in C(c, R), f z`, and over the boundaries of rectangles, and many theorems allow a countable set of exceptional points where $f$ is only continuous. The Fourier transform uses the kernel $e^{-2\pi i \langle x, \xi \rangle}$, so that it needs no constant in front of the inversion formula, and the circle $\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}$ carries the Haar measure of total mass $1$. Several results that a textbook has are not in this edition of Mathlib, the residue theorem for a general contour among them, and the sections say so. The volume has no Lean companion yet, so the sections show the Mathlib statements and give no Lean exercises.
