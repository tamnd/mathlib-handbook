## Splitting fields

### Overview

The polynomial $X^2 + 1$ has no root in $\mathbb{R}$, but $\mathbb{C}$ has both roots $\pm i$. For a polynomial over an arbitrary field there is no $\mathbb{C}$ at hand. Kronecker's idea is to build the root: if $g$ is irreducible over $K$, then $K[X]/(g)$ is a field, and the class of $X$ is a root of $g$. Repeated for each root, the construction gives a splitting field of $f$, the smallest field in which $f$ is a product of linear factors.

This section proves that splitting fields exist, that their degree is at most $n!$ for $\deg f = n$, and that any two are isomorphic. The proof of uniqueness gives a stronger result, the extension of embeddings, which Chapter 5 uses for normal extensions. The section needs Chapter 2, in particular the theorem on embeddings of $K(\alpha)$ from Section 3.

### Definitions

**Definition (Splitting).** Let $L/K$ be an extension and $f \in K[X]$. Then $f$ splits over $L$ if $f = c(X - \alpha_1)\cdots(X - \alpha_n)$ in $L[X]$ with $c \in K$ and $\alpha_i \in L$. The zero polynomial and the constants split.

**Definition (Splitting field).** Let $f \in K[X]$. An extension $L/K$ is a splitting field of $f$ if $f$ splits over $L$ and $L = K(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$, where $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ are the roots of $f$ in $L$.

The second condition says that $L$ is minimal: no intermediate field $E \neq L$ has all the roots. A splitting field is finite over $K$, since each $\alpha_i$ is algebraic and the tower law applies.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Kronecker).** Let $f \in K[X]$ have positive degree. Then there is an extension $L/K$ with $[L:K] \leq \deg f$ in which $f$ has a root.

*Proof.* The ring $K[X]$ has unique factorization (Volume 6, Chapter 6), so $f$ has an irreducible factor $g$. The ideal $(g)$ is maximal, so $L = K[X]/(g)$ is a field. The map $K \to L$ is a ring map of fields, so $L$ is an extension of $K$. Let $\alpha$ be the class of $X$. For $g = \sum b_iX^i$, $g(\alpha) = \sum b_i\alpha^i$ is the class of $g$, which is $0$. So $g(\alpha) = 0$ and $f(\alpha) = 0$. By Chapter 2, Section 2, $[L:K] = \deg g \leq \deg f$. $\square$

**Theorem (Existence of splitting fields).** Let $f \in K[X]$ have degree $n \geq 1$. Then $f$ has a splitting field $L$, and $[L:K] \leq n!$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$, for all fields $K$ at once. For $n = 1$, $f$ splits over $K$ and $L = K$ works. Let $n > 1$. By Kronecker, there is an extension $K_1 = K(\alpha)$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$ and $[K_1:K] \leq n$. Over $K_1$, $f = (X - \alpha)g$ with $\deg g = n - 1$. By induction, $g$ has a splitting field $L$ over $K_1$, with $[L:K_1] \leq (n-1)!$. Then $f$ splits over $L$, with the roots $\alpha$ and the roots $\beta_2, \dots, \beta_n$ of $g$. Also $L = K_1(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_n) = K(\alpha, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_n)$ by adjunction in steps. By the tower law $[L:K] \leq n \cdot (n-1)! = n!$. $\square$

The index of this edition has no declaration for the bound $[L:K] \leq n!$. Mathlib proves the finiteness as `Polynomial.IsSplittingField.finiteDimensional`.

`Polynomial.SplittingField f : Type v` is a splitting field of `f : K[X]`, in the same universe as `K`. Do not use the construction in proofs. **Theorem (Extension of embeddings).** Let $L$ be a splitting field of $f \in K[X]$, and let $\varphi \colon K \to M$ be a ring map into a field $M$ over which $\varphi(f)$ splits. Then $\varphi$ extends to a ring map $\sigma \colon L \to M$. There are at most $[L:K]$ such extensions.

*Proof.* Induction on $[L:K]$, for all $K$, $f$ and $\varphi$ at once. If $[L:K] = 1$, then $L = K$ and $\sigma = \varphi$ is the only extension.

Let $[L:K] > 1$. The roots of $f$ generate $L$, so some root $\alpha$ is not in $K$. Let $g = m_{\alpha,K}$, of degree $d > 1$. In $K[X]$, $g$ divides $f$, so $\varphi(g)$ divides $\varphi(f)$, and $\varphi(g)$ splits over $M$. Let $\beta$ be a root of $\varphi(g)$ in $M$. The map $K[X] \to M$, $p \mapsto \varphi(p)(\beta)$, is a ring map whose kernel contains $g$. With $K(\alpha) \cong K[X]/(g)$ from Chapter 2, it gives $\varphi_1 \colon K(\alpha) \to M$ with $\varphi_1(\alpha) = \beta$ that extends $\varphi$. As in the theorem on embeddings of Chapter 2, Section 3, the extensions of $\varphi$ to $K(\alpha)$ correspond to the roots of $\varphi(g)$ in $M$, so there are at most $d$ of them.

Now $L$ is a splitting field of $f$ over $K(\alpha)$: $f$ splits over $L$, and the roots generate $L$ over $K$, so also over $K(\alpha)$. The polynomial $\varphi_1(f) = \varphi(f)$ splits over $M$. Since $[L:K(\alpha)] = [L:K]/d < [L:K]$, induction gives an extension of $\varphi_1$ to $L$, and at most $[L:K(\alpha)]$ extensions of each $\varphi_1$. Each extension $\sigma$ of $\varphi$ restricts to some $\varphi_1$, so there are at most $d \cdot [L:K(\alpha)] = [L:K]$ of them. $\square$

**Corollary (Uniqueness of splitting fields).** Let $L$ and $L'$ be splitting fields of $f \in K[X]$. Then there is an isomorphism $L \to L'$ of $K$-algebras.

*Proof.* Apply the theorem with $M = L'$ and $\varphi$ the map $K \to L'$. It gives a $K$-algebra map $\sigma \colon L \to L'$, injective since $L$ is a field. Write $f = c\prod(X - \alpha_i)$ in $L[X]$. Applying $\sigma$ gives $f = c\prod(X - \sigma(\alpha_i))$ in $L'[X]$. So the roots of $f$ in $L'$ are the $\sigma(\alpha_i)$, and they lie in $\sigma(L)$. They generate $L'$, so $\sigma(L) = L'$. $\square$

**Theorem (Splitting fields in steps).** Let $f, g \in K[X]$ be nonzero, $E$ a splitting field of $f$ over $K$, and $L$ a splitting field of $g$ over $E$. Then $L$ is a splitting field of $fg$ over $K$.

*Proof.* Both $f$ and $g$ split over $L$, so $fg$ does. The roots of $f$ generate $E$ over $K$, and the roots of $g$ generate $L$ over $E$. By adjunction in steps the roots of $fg$ generate $L$ over $K$. $\square$

In Mathlib this is `Polynomial.IsSplittingField.mul`. Inside a given field, `IntermediateField.adjoin_rootSet_isSplittingField` says that the field generated by the roots is a splitting field, and `IntermediateField.splits_iff_mem` says that $f$ splits over an intermediate field $E$ exactly when $E$ contains all roots.

### Examples

**Example (The polynomial $X^3 - 2$).** Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the roots in $\mathbb{C}$ are $c, c\omega, c\omega^2$ with $c = \sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$. So the splitting field is $L = \mathbb{Q}(c, c\omega, c\omega^2) = \mathbb{Q}(c, \omega)$, since $\omega = (c\omega)/c$. By Chapter 2, $[\mathbb{Q}(c):\mathbb{Q}] = 3$. The field $\mathbb{Q}(c)$ lies in $\mathbb{R}$, and $\omega \notin \mathbb{R}$, so $\omega \notin \mathbb{Q}(c)$. The element $\omega$ is a root of $X^2 + X + 1$, so $[L:\mathbb{Q}(c)] = 2$. By the tower law $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = 6 = 3!$, and the bound is attained.

**Example (The polynomial $X^4 + 1$).** Let $\zeta = e^{\pi i/4} = (1 + i)/\sqrt 2$. The roots of $X^4 + 1$ are $\zeta, \zeta^3, \zeta^5, \zeta^7$, all powers of $\zeta$. So the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$, of degree $4$ since $X^4 + 1$ is irreducible (Chapter 2, Section 2, Exercise 7). Here one root generates the splitting field, and the degree $4$ is far below $4! = 24$. Also $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta) = \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt 2)$, because $\zeta + \zeta^7 = \sqrt 2$ and $\zeta^2 = i$.

**Example (A field with four elements).** Over $\mathbb{F}_2$, $f = X^2 + X + 1$ has no root, since $f(0) = f(1) = 1$. So it is irreducible, and $L = \mathbb{F}_2[X]/(f)$ is a field with $4$ elements $0, 1, \alpha, \alpha + 1$. The second root is $\alpha + 1$: the sum of the roots is $-1 = 1$. Thus $L$ is a splitting field, and one step of Kronecker suffices. Chapter 7 shows that each finite field arises in this way.

**Example (The minimality condition is needed).** The polynomial $X^2 + 1$ splits over $\mathbb{C}$, but $\mathbb{C}$ is not a splitting field over $\mathbb{Q}$: it has infinite degree, while $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ has degree $2$. The isomorphism of the corollary is not unique either: $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ has the two automorphisms $i \mapsto i$ and $i \mapsto -i$. Chapter 5 studies these automorphisms.

### The library around it

`Polynomial.IsSplittingField.splits_iff` says that `f` splits over `K` itself exactly when the splitting field `L` satisfies `⊤ = ⊥`, that is $L = K$. To work with a splitting field in Lean, state the theorem for an arbitrary `L` with `[IsSplittingField K L f]`.

**Common errors of a learner.** The first error is to write `f.Splits` for `f : K[X]` and expect it to mean splitting over `L`. It means splitting over `K`. The second error is to unfold `SplittingField` and work with `SplittingFieldAux`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find the splitting field of $X^4 - 5X^2 + 6$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and its degree. Hint: factor as $(X^2 - 2)(X^2 - 3)$.

2. (A) Show that a quadratic extension $L/K$ is the splitting field of the minimal polynomial of any $\alpha \in L \setminus K$. Hint: the sum of the two roots lies in $K$.

3. (A) Find the degree of the splitting field of $X^3 - 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. Hint: $X^3 - 1 = (X - 1)(X^2 + X + 1)$.

4. (B) Let $p$ be prime. Show that the splitting field of $X^p - 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ with $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/p}$, of degree $p - 1$. Hint: Chapter 2, Section 2, Exercise 4.

5. (B) Find the splitting field of $X^4 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ and show that its degree is $8$. Hint: the roots are $\pm\sqrt[4]{2}, \pm i\sqrt[4]{2}$, and $i \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

6. (B) Let $L$ be a splitting field of $f$ over $K$ and $E$ an intermediate field. Show that $L$ is a splitting field of $f$ over $E$. Hint: the roots generate $L$ over $E$ too.

7. (B) Show that the splitting field of $X^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has at most $6$ automorphisms, and that each permutes the three roots. Hint: the extension theorem with $M = L$.

8. (C) Let $f \in K[X]$ have degree $n$ and let $L$ be its splitting field. Show that $[L:K]$ divides $n!$. Hint: induction on $n$. If $f$ is irreducible, adjoin one root and apply induction to $f/(X - \alpha)$. If $f = gh$ with $\deg g = k$, use the steps theorem and that $k!\,(n-k)!$ divides $n!$.

### Notes and history

Kronecker described the adjunction of a root as a quotient of a polynomial ring in the 1880s. Before him, the roots were taken in $\mathbb{C}$. His construction works over every field.
