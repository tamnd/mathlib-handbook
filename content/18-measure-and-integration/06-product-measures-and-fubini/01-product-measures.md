## Product measures

### Overview

The area of a plane region is the integral of the lengths of its vertical sections. For measures $\mu$ on $X$ and $\nu$ on $Y$ it defines $(\mu \otimes \nu)(s) = \int \nu(s_x) \, d\mu(x)$, where $s_x$ is the section of $s$ at $x$. The section proves that this is a measure with $(\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)$, and that this property fixes it when both measures are σ-finite. Finite products give the measure `volume` on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Definitions

Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$ be measure spaces. The product σ-algebra $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ on $X \times Y$ is generated by the rectangles $A \times B$ with $A \in \mathcal{A}$ and $B \in \mathcal{B}$ (Chapter 1, Section 3). The rectangles form a π-system, because $(A \times B) \cap (A' \times B') = (A \cap A') \times (B \cap B')$.

**Definition (Section).** For $s \subseteq X \times Y$ and $x \in X$, the section of $s$ at $x$ is $s_x = \{y \in Y : (x, y) \in s\}$. For $y \in Y$, the section at $y$ is $s^y = \{x \in X : (x, y) \in s\}$.

Sections commute with complements and unions, and $(A \times B)_x$ is $B$ if $x \in A$ and $\emptyset$ otherwise. So the sets with measurable sections form a σ-algebra that contains the rectangles, and every measurable set has measurable sections.

**Definition (Product measure).** Let $\nu$ be s-finite. The product measure is
$$(\mu \otimes \nu)(s) = \int_X \nu(s_x) \, d\mu(x), \qquad s \in \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}.$$

Recall from Chapter 2 that $\nu$ is s-finite if it is a countable sum of finite measures. Every σ-finite measure is s-finite.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem 1 (Measurability of the section measure).** Let $\nu$ be s-finite and let $s \in \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$. Then $x \mapsto \nu(s_x)$ is a measurable function $X \to [0, \infty]$.

*Proof.* First let $\nu$ be finite. Let $\mathcal{D}$ be the family of the measurable sets $s$ for which $x \mapsto \nu(s_x)$ is measurable. For a rectangle, $\nu((A \times B)_x) = \mathbf{1}_A(x) \, \nu(B)$, which is measurable. So $\mathcal{D}$ contains the π-system of rectangles. If $s \in \mathcal{D}$, then $\nu((s^c)_x) = \nu(Y) - \nu(s_x)$. This is measurable, and here the finiteness of $\nu(Y)$ is used. If $s_1, s_2, \dots \in \mathcal{D}$ are disjoint, then their sections at $x$ are disjoint, so $\nu((\bigcup_n s_n)_x) = \sum_n \nu((s_n)_x)$. A series of measurable functions is measurable. So $\mathcal{D}$ is a Dynkin system. By the π-λ theorem of Chapter 1, Section 2, $\mathcal{D}$ contains the σ-algebra generated by the rectangles, which is $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$.

Now let $\nu = \sum_n \nu_n$ with each $\nu_n$ finite. Then $\nu(s_x) = \sum_n \nu_n(s_x)$. Each term is measurable by the first part, so the sum is measurable. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (Existence).** Let $\nu$ be s-finite. Then $\mu \otimes \nu$ is a measure on $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$, and $(\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A) \, \nu(B)$ for all $A \in \mathcal{A}$ and $B \in \mathcal{B}$.

*Proof.* The integrand is measurable by Theorem 1. The section of $\emptyset$ is empty, so $(\mu \otimes \nu)(\emptyset) = 0$. Let $s_1, s_2, \dots$ be disjoint and measurable. Their sections at each $x$ are disjoint, so
$$(\mu \otimes \nu)\Big(\bigcup_n s_n\Big) = \int \sum_n \nu((s_n)_x) \, d\mu(x) = \sum_n \int \nu((s_n)_x) \, d\mu(x) = \sum_n (\mu \otimes \nu)(s_n).$$
The middle equality is the integral of a series of nonnegative functions, a consequence of monotone convergence (Chapter 4, Section 2). For a rectangle, $\int \mathbf{1}_A(x) \, \nu(B) \, d\mu(x) = \nu(B) \, \mu(A)$, with the convention $0 \cdot \infty = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 3 (Uniqueness).** Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be σ-finite. If a measure $\rho$ on $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ satisfies $\rho(A \times B) = \mu(A) \, \nu(B)$ for all measurable $A$ and $B$, then $\rho = \mu \otimes \nu$.

*Proof.* Choose $A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \dots$ with $\bigcup_n A_n = X$ and $\mu(A_n) < \infty$, and $B_n$ in the same way for $\nu$. The rectangles $A_n \times B_n$ increase to $X \times Y$ and have finite measure $\mu(A_n)\nu(B_n)$ for both measures. The two measures agree on the π-system of rectangles, which generates $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$. By the uniqueness theorem of Chapter 2, Section 3, they are equal. $\square$

**Corollary 4 (Symmetry).** Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be σ-finite and let $s$ be measurable. Then
$$\int_X \nu(s_x) \, d\mu(x) = \int_Y \mu(s^y) \, d\nu(y).$$

*Proof.* Theorem 2 with the roles of the factors exchanged gives a measure $\nu \otimes \mu$ on $Y \times X$. Let $\sigma(x, y) = (y, x)$ and $\rho(s) = (\nu \otimes \mu)(\sigma(s))$. Then $\rho$ is a measure on $X \times Y$, and $\rho(s)$ is the right side of the formula, because the section of $\sigma(s)$ at $y$ is $s^y$. Since $\sigma(A \times B) = B \times A$, $\rho(A \times B) = \nu(B)\mu(A)$. By Theorem 3, $\rho = \mu \otimes \nu$. $\square$

**Proposition 5 (Null sets).** Let $\nu$ be s-finite and $s$ measurable. Then $(\mu \otimes \nu)(s) = 0$ if and only if $\nu(s_x) = 0$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$.

*Proof.* The integral of a measurable function $f \ge 0$ is zero if and only if $f = 0$ almost everywhere (Chapter 4, Section 1). Apply this to $f(x) = \nu(s_x)$. $\square$

**Finite products.** For a finite family of σ-finite measures $\mu_i$ on $X_i$, the same method gives a measure $\bigotimes_i \mu_i$ on $\prod_i X_i$ with
$$\Big(\bigotimes_i \mu_i\Big)\Big(\prod_i A_i\Big) = \prod_i \mu_i(A_i),$$
and this property fixes it. For $\mathbb{R}^n$ with Lebesgue measure on each factor, this is the $n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure of Chapter 3, Section 2.

### Examples

**Example (The graph of a function is null).** Let $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ be measurable and let $\lambda$ be Lebesgue measure. The graph $\Gamma = \{(x, f(x))\}$ is measurable, because it is the preimage of $\{0\}$ under the measurable map $(x, y) \mapsto y - f(x)$. Its section at $x$ is the singleton $\{f(x)\}$, so $\lambda(\Gamma_x) = 0$ for each $x$. Then $(\mu \otimes \lambda)(\Gamma) = \int 0 \, d\mu = 0$ for every measure $\mu$ on $X$. With $f(x) = x$, the diagonal of $\mathbb{R}^2$ has area zero.

**Example (The area of a triangle).** Let $\lambda_2 = \lambda \otimes \lambda$ on $[0, 1]^2$, $T = \{(x, y) : 0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$ and $T' = \{(x, y) : 0 \le x \le y \le 1\}$. The swap map $\sigma$ sends $T$ onto $T'$ and preserves $\lambda_2$ by Corollary 4, so $\lambda_2(T) = \lambda_2(T')$. The union $T \cup T'$ is the unit square, of area $1$, and $T \cap T'$ is part of the diagonal, of area $0$ by the first example. So $2\lambda_2(T) = 1$ and $\lambda_2(T) = 1/2$. Since $\lambda_2(T) = \int_0^1 \lambda([0, x]) \, dx$, this also gives $\int_0^1 x \, dx = 1/2$ without a primitive.

**Counterexample (Uniqueness needs σ-finiteness).** Let $\mu = \nu = \infty \cdot \lambda$ on $[0, 1]$, the measure with $\mu(A) = \infty$ if $\lambda(A) > 0$ and $\mu(A) = 0$ otherwise. It is s-finite, as the sum of countably many copies of $\lambda$, but not σ-finite. Let $\delta$ be the image of $\lambda$ under $x \mapsto (x, x)$ and $\rho = \mu \otimes \nu + \delta$. On a rectangle, if $\lambda(A) = 0$ or $\lambda(B) = 0$, then $\lambda(A \cap B) = 0$, so $\delta(A \times B) = 0$ and $\rho(A \times B) = 0 = \mu(A)\nu(B)$. Otherwise both sides are $\infty$. So $\rho$ and $\mu \otimes \nu$ agree on all rectangles. On the diagonal $\Delta$, the sections are singletons, so $(\mu \otimes \nu)(\Delta) = 0$, but $\rho(\Delta) = 1$. Theorem 3 fails.

Common errors of a learner:

- Using `prod_apply` without `[SFinite ν]`. Without it, `μ.prod ν` is the junk value `0`.
- Using `prod_eq` for s-finite measures that are not σ-finite. The counterexample above shows that it fails.
- Treating `ℝ × ℝ` and `Fin 2 → ℝ` as one type. Their `volume` instances differ.

### Exercises

1. (A) Compute $(\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B)$ for $\mu = \delta_a$, any $\nu$, and verify that $\delta_a \otimes \delta_b = \delta_{(a, b)}$.
2. (A) Show that $\lambda_2(\{(x, y) : x \in \mathbb{Q}\}) = 0$.
3. (B) Show that $\lambda_2(\{(x, y) \in [0, 1]^2 : x^2 + y^2 \le 1\}) = \int_0^1 \sqrt{1 - x^2} \, dx$. Hint: compute the sections.
4. (B) Let $\mu$ and $\nu$ be σ-finite and $s \subseteq X \times Y$ measurable. Show that $\mu(s^y) = 0$ for $\nu$-almost every $y$ if and only if $\nu(s_x) = 0$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$. Hint: Corollary 4 and Proposition 5.
5. (B) Let $f \ge 0$ be measurable on $X$. Show that the region under the graph $\{(x, t) : 0 \le t < f(x)\}$ has $(\mu \otimes \lambda)$-measure $\int f \, d\mu$. Hint: the section at $x$ is $[0, f(x))$.
6. (C) Let $\nu$ be the sum of the Dirac measures at the points of a set $t \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ that is not Borel. Show that $x \mapsto \nu(\Delta_x)$ is not measurable for the diagonal $\Delta$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$. Hint: $\nu(\Delta_x) = \mathbf{1}_t(x)$.

### Notes and history

Fubini published his theorem in 1907 and Tonelli his in 1909. The Mathlib files have Floris van Doorn as author. The library uses s-finite measures and keeps σ-finiteness only for uniqueness.
