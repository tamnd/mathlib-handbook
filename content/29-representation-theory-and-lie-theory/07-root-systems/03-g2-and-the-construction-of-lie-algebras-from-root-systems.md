## G2 and the construction of Lie algebras from root systems

### Overview

The section has two parts. The first is about $G_2$, the only reduced irreducible crystallographic root system with a pairing equal to $-3$. Two roots with pairing $-3$ produce twelve roots by reflection. A finite case check then shows that every other root is orthogonal to both of them, so irreducibility forces the system to be the plane they span. Mathlib needs this to set $G_2$ aside, as some chain identities fail for it.

The second part runs Chapter 6 backwards. Chapter 6 takes a simple Lie algebra to a root system; here a reduced root system with a base gives a simple Lie algebra. Mathlib has Serre's presentation by generators and relations, and Geck's construction by explicit matrices of size $|\Delta| + |\Phi|$. It proves that Geck's algebra is simple with the given root system, and that every simple Lie algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$ arises this way.

The notation is that of Sections 1 and 2, with $A_{ij} = p_{ij}$ for $i, j \in \Delta$, and $q$, $r$ the top and bottom coefficients of a root chain, so $r - q = \langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Embedded $G_2$).** An *embedded $G_2$* in a crystallographic reduced root pairing is a choice of two indices, a *long* root $\beta$ and a *short* root $\alpha$, such that $\langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle = -3$. A crystallographic, reduced, irreducible pairing *is $G_2$* if some $p_{ij}$ equals $-3$, and *is not $G_2$* if every $p_{ij}$ lies in $\{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}$.

**Definition (Serre construction).** Let $A$ be a square integer matrix indexed by $B$. Take the free Lie algebra over $R$ on generators $H_i, E_i, F_i$ for $i \in B$. The *Serre Lie algebra* of $A$ is its quotient by the ideal generated by $[H_i, H_j]$, $[E_i, F_i] - H_i$, $[E_i, F_j]$ for $i \neq j$, $[H_i, E_j] - A_{ij}E_j$, $[H_i, F_j] + A_{ij}F_j$, $\operatorname{ad}(E_i)^{1 - A_{ij}}(E_j)$ and $\operatorname{ad}(F_i)^{1 - A_{ij}}(F_j)$ for $i \neq j$.

**Definition (Geck construction).** Let $P$ be a crystallographic root pairing with finite index set $\iota$ and a base $\Delta$. Let $V$ be the free module with basis $u_k$ for $k \in \Delta$ and $v_j$ for $j \in \iota$. For $i \in \Delta$ write $\alpha = \alpha_i$ and let $-i$ be the index of $-\alpha_i$. Define endomorphisms of $V$ on the basis:

- $h_i(u_k) = 0$ and $h_i(v_j) = \langle \alpha_j, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle\, v_j$;
- $e_i(u_k) = |A_{ik}|\, v_i$, $e_i(v_{-i}) = u_i$, and for $j \neq -i$, $e_i(v_j) = (r_{ij} + 1)\, v_l$ if $\alpha_l = \alpha_j + \alpha_i$ is a root and $0$ otherwise, where $r_{ij}$ is the bottom coefficient of the $\alpha_i$-chain through $\alpha_j$;
- $f_i(u_k) = |A_{ik}|\, v_{-i}$, $f_i(v_i) = u_i$, and for $j \neq i$, $f_i(v_j) = (q_{ij} + 1)\, v_l$ if $\alpha_l = \alpha_j - \alpha_i$ is a root and $0$ otherwise, where $q_{ij}$ is the top coefficient.

The *Geck Lie algebra* $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$ is the Lie subalgebra of $\operatorname{End}(V)$ generated by all $h_i$, $e_i$, $f_i$. Its *Cartan subalgebra* $\mathfrak{h}$ is the span of the $h_i$.

**Definition (Basis of a Lie algebra).** Let $H$ be a subalgebra of $L$. A *basis* of $L$ relative to $H$, indexed by a finite set $I$, consists of a nondegenerate integer matrix $A$ and elements $h_i, e_i, f_i$ such that $H$ is generated by the $h_i$, the $e_i$ and $f_i$ generate $L$, the $h_i$ are linearly independent, each $(h_i, e_i, f_i)$ is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ triple, and $[h_i, h_j] = 0$, $[h_j, e_i] = A_{ij}e_i$, $[h_j, f_i] = -A_{ij}f_i$, $[e_i, f_j] = 0$ for $i \neq j$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (Twelve roots).** In an embedded $G_2$ with $\alpha$ short and $\beta$ long, $\langle \alpha, \beta^\vee \rangle = -1$, and the vectors $\pm\alpha$, $\pm\beta$, $\pm(\alpha + \beta)$, $\pm(2\alpha + \beta)$, $\pm(3\alpha + \beta)$, $\pm(3\alpha + 2\beta)$ are roots. For an invariant form $B$, $B(\beta, \beta) = 3B(\alpha, \alpha)$.

*Proof.* Step 1. By the reduced list of Section 2, a pair $(\langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle, \langle \alpha, \beta^\vee \rangle)$ with first entry $-3$ must be $(-3, -1)$. Step 2. By the lemma $2B(\alpha_i, \alpha_j) = p_{ij}B(\alpha_j, \alpha_j)$ of Section 2, $2B(\beta, \alpha) = -3B(\alpha, \alpha)$ and $2B(\alpha, \beta) = -B(\beta, \beta)$. Since $B$ is symmetric, $B(\beta, \beta) = 3B(\alpha, \alpha)$. Step 3. Reflections send roots to roots: $s_\alpha(\beta) = \beta + 3\alpha$ and $s_\beta(\alpha) = \alpha + \beta$. Then $\langle \alpha + \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle = 2 - 3 = -1$, so $s_\alpha(\alpha + \beta) = 2\alpha + \beta$; and $\langle 3\alpha + \beta, \beta^\vee \rangle = -3 + 2 = -1$, so $s_\beta(3\alpha + \beta) = 3\alpha + 2\beta$. Step 4. $s_i(\alpha_i) = -\alpha_i$ and $s_i$ permutes the roots, so the set of roots is closed under negation. $\square$

The coroots of these roots follow from $s_i^\vee(y) = y - \langle \alpha_i, y \rangle \alpha_i^\vee$ applied to the same reflections: $(\alpha + \beta)^\vee = \alpha^\vee + 3\beta^\vee$, $(2\alpha + \beta)^\vee = 2\alpha^\vee + 3\beta^\vee$, $(3\alpha + \beta)^\vee = \alpha^\vee + \beta^\vee$, $(3\alpha + 2\beta)^\vee = \alpha^\vee + 2\beta^\vee$.

**Theorem (Orthogonality).** In an embedded $G_2$, let $\gamma$ be a root that is not one of the twelve. Then $\langle \gamma, \alpha^\vee \rangle = \langle \gamma, \beta^\vee \rangle = 0$.

*Proof.* Step 1. Write $a = \langle \gamma, \alpha^\vee \rangle$ and $b = \langle \gamma, \beta^\vee \rangle$. By the coroot formulas, the pairings $\langle \gamma, \delta^\vee \rangle$ for the short positive roots $\delta = \alpha, \alpha + \beta, 2\alpha + \beta$ are $a$, $a + 3b$, $2a + 3b$, and for the long positive roots $\delta = \beta, 3\alpha + \beta, 3\alpha + 2\beta$ they are $b$, $a + b$, $a + 2b$. Call these the short values and the long values. Step 2. Since $\gamma \neq \pm\delta$ and the pairing is reduced, Section 2 gives $(x, x') = (\langle \gamma, \delta^\vee \rangle, \langle \delta, \gamma^\vee \rangle)$ in the set $S$ of pairs $(0, 0)$, $\pm(1, 1)$, $\pm(1, 2)$, $\pm(2, 1)$, $\pm(1, 3)$, $\pm(3, 1)$. Step 3. Fix an invariant form $B$ and put $\rho = B(\gamma, \gamma)/B(\alpha, \alpha)$. Short roots are Weyl images of $\alpha$ and long ones of $\beta$, so by the lemma $B(\delta, \delta) = B(\alpha, \alpha)$ for short $\delta$ and $3B(\alpha, \alpha)$ for long $\delta$. The lemma of Section 2 gives $xB(\delta, \delta) = x'B(\gamma, \gamma)$. So a nonzero short value $x$ has $x/x' = \rho$, and a nonzero long value $y$ has $y/y' = \rho/3$. Step 4. For a nonzero pair in $S$, $x/x' \in \{1, 2, 3, \tfrac12, \tfrac13\}$. If all short values vanish then $a = 0 = a + 3b$, so $b = 0$. If all long values vanish then $b = 0 = a + b$, so $a = 0$. Otherwise $\rho$ lies in $\{1, 2, 3, \tfrac12, \tfrac13\}$ and in $3 \cdot \{1, 2, 3, \tfrac12, \tfrac13\}$, so $\rho \in \{1, 3\}$. Step 5. If $\rho = 1$, short values satisfy $x = x'$, so they lie in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$; long values satisfy $y' = 3y$, so they also lie in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Then $3b = (a + 3b) - a$ has absolute value at most $2$, so $b = 0$, and then $2a = 2a + 3b \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ gives $a = 0$. Step 6. If $\rho = 3$, short values satisfy $x = 3x'$, so they lie in $\{-3, 0, 3\}$; long values satisfy $y = y'$, so they lie in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Then $a \in 3\mathbb{Z}$ and $|a| = |(a + b) - b| \leq 2$, so $a = 0$; then $2b = a + 2b \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ gives $b = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem ($G_2$ is two dimensional).** Let $P$ be crystallographic, reduced and irreducible with an embedded $G_2$. Then $\alpha$ and $\beta$ span $M$, and $P$ has exactly twelve roots.

*Proof.* Step 1. Let $W$ be the span of $\alpha$ and $\beta$. A root $\gamma$ in $W$ is one of the twelve: otherwise the theorem and the symmetry of vanishing pairings give $\langle \alpha, \gamma^\vee \rangle = \langle \beta, \gamma^\vee \rangle = 0$, giving $\langle \gamma, \gamma^\vee \rangle = 0 \neq 2$. Step 2. $W$ is stable under every reflection. For one of the twelve roots $\delta$, $s_\delta(w) = w - \langle w, \delta^\vee \rangle \delta \in W$; any other $s_\gamma$ fixes $W$ pointwise, as above. Step 3. $W \neq 0$, so irreducibility gives $W = M$. Step 4. By Step 1 every root is one of the twelve. They are distinct, as $\alpha, \beta$ are independent. $\square$

**Theorem (Weights of the Geck generators).** For $i, j \in \Delta$, $[h_i, h_j] = 0$, $[h_j, e_i] = A_{ij}e_i$ and $[h_j, f_i] = -A_{ij}f_i$.

*Proof.* Step 1. Every $h_j$ is diagonal in the basis $u_k, v_l$, so the $h_j$ commute. Call the diagonal entry the weight: $u_k$ has weight $0$ and $v_l$ has weight $\langle \alpha_l, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle$. Step 2. If $T$ sends each basis vector to a multiple of a basis vector whose weight is larger by $c$, then $[h_j, T] = cT$. Step 3. $e_i$ sends $v_l$ to a multiple of $v_{l'}$ with $\alpha_{l'} = \alpha_l + \alpha_i$, raising the weight by $\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_j^\vee \rangle = A_{ij}$. It sends $v_{-i}$ (weight $-A_{ij}$) to $u_i$ (weight $0$), and $u_k$ (weight $0$) into $Kv_i$ (weight $A_{ij}$). So $[h_j, e_i] = A_{ij}e_i$. Step 4. $f_i$ lowers every weight by $A_{ij}$ in the same way. $\square$

**Theorem ($\mathfrak{sl}_2$ triples).** If $P$ is reduced, $[e_i, f_i] = h_i$ for $i \in \Delta$, so $(h_i, e_i, f_i)$ is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ triple.

*Proof.* Write $\alpha = \alpha_i$. Step 1, on $u_k$. $e_if_i(u_k) = |A_{ik}|\, e_i(v_{-i}) = |A_{ik}|\, u_i$ and $f_ie_i(u_k) = |A_{ik}|\, f_i(v_i) = |A_{ik}|\, u_i$, so $[e_i, f_i]u_k = 0 = h_iu_k$. Step 2, on $v_i$. $f_i(v_i) = u_i$ and $e_i(u_i) = |A_{ii}|\, v_i = 2v_i$. Since $2\alpha$ is not a root, $e_i(v_i) = 0$. So $[e_i, f_i]v_i = 2v_i = h_iv_i$. Step 3, on $v_{-i}$. By the same computation with $e$ and $f$ exchanged, $[e_i, f_i]v_{-i} = -2v_{-i} = h_iv_{-i}$. Step 4, on $v_k$ with $\beta = \alpha_k \neq \pm\alpha$. The roots $\alpha, \beta$ are independent; let $q, r$ be the top and bottom coefficients of the $\alpha$-chain through $\beta$. If $r \geq 1$, $\beta - \alpha$ is a root with bottom coefficient $r - 1$ in the same chain, so $e_if_i(v_k) = (q + 1)r\, v_k$; if $r = 0$ both sides of this formula are $0$. Likewise $f_ie_i(v_k) = (r + 1)q\, v_k$. So $[e_i, f_i]v_k = (r - q)v_k = \langle \beta, \alpha^\vee \rangle v_k = h_iv_k$. Step 5. With the previous theorem, $[h_i, e_i] = 2e_i$, $[h_i, f_i] = -2f_i$ and $h_i \neq 0$ since $h_iv_i = 2v_i$. $\square$

**Theorem (Serre relation).** If $P$ is reduced and irreducible and $i \neq j$ in $\Delta$, then $[e_i, f_j] = 0$.

*Proof outline.* On a general $v_k$, both $e_if_j(v_k)$ and $f_je_i(v_k)$ are multiples of $v_n$ with $\alpha_n = \alpha_k + \alpha_i - \alpha_j$, and their coefficients are $(r_{i m} + 1)(q_{jk} + 1)$ and $(q_{j l} + 1)(r_{ik} + 1)$, where $\alpha_l = \alpha_k + \alpha_i$ and $\alpha_m = \alpha_k - \alpha_j$. Geck's Lemma 2.6 states that these are equal. Its proof shows that $P$ is not $G_2$ and uses that, outside $G_2$, $r_{ij} = 1$ if $p_{ij} = 0$ and $0$ otherwise when $\alpha_i + \alpha_j$ is a root. The case split, and the cases $u_k$ and $v_{\pm i}, v_{\pm j}$, are not reproduced here. $\square$

**Theorem (Semisimplicity).** Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic $0$, the Geck Lie algebra has trivial radical.

*Proof.* Step 1. Every element of $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$ has trace $0$ on $V$. The trace of $h_i$ is $\sum_l \langle \alpha_l, \alpha_i^\vee \rangle = 0$, since the roots come in pairs $\pm\alpha_l$. The diagonal entries of $e_i$ and $f_i$ vanish, and brackets have trace $0$. Step 2. $V$ is an irreducible $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$-module. This is Geck's Lemma 4.2, cited here: a nonzero submodule is not inside the zero weight space, spanned by the $u_k$, so it contains some $v_j$, and the $e_i$ and $f_i$ move $v_j$ to every basis vector. Step 3. $V$ is faithful, as $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta) \subseteq \operatorname{End}(V)$. Step 4. A Lie algebra with a faithful irreducible module of trace zero has trivial radical, by Chapter 5: by Lie's theorem and the invariance lemma the radical acts by scalars, and a scalar of trace zero in characteristic $0$ is $0$. $\square$

**Theorem (Completeness).** Let $K$ be algebraically closed of characteristic $0$ and $P$ reduced, irreducible and crystallographic with base $\Delta$. Then $\mathfrak{h}$ is a Cartan subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$, the root system of $(\mathfrak{g}(\Delta), \mathfrak{h})$ is isomorphic to $P$, and $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$ is simple. Every finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra $L$ with Cartan subalgebra $H$ and base $\Delta$ of its root system is isomorphic to $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$.

*Proof outline.* The theorems above say that $(A, h_i, e_i, f_i)$ is a basis of $\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)$ in the sense of the definitions. For any basis, $H$ and the subalgebras generated by the $e_i$ and by the $f_i$ add up to $L$ (Geck's Lemma 4.5), and lie in the zero, positive and negative root spaces, so $H$ is the zero root space and is a Cartan subalgebra. The roots of the $e_i$ form a base with Cartan matrix $A$; equal Cartan matrices give isomorphic systems (Section 2), and simplicity follows from irreducibility (Chapter 6). For the last claim, $L$ has a basis built from the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ triples of Chapter 6; simple algebras with bases of the same matrix are isomorphic, through the subalgebra of $L_1 \times L_2$ generated by the pairs $(e_i, e_i')$, $(f_i, f_i')$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example ($A_1$ by Geck).** Take $\iota = \{\alpha, -\alpha\}$ and $\Delta = \{\alpha\}$. Then $V$ has basis $u, v_+, v_-$ and $h = \operatorname{diag}(0, 2, -2)$. The maps are $e(v_-) = u$, $e(u) = 2v_+$, $e(v_+) = 0$, and $f(v_+) = u$, $f(u) = 2v_-$, $f(v_-) = 0$. Then $[e, f]v_+ = 2v_+$, $[e, f]u = 2u - 2u = 0$ and $[e, f]v_- = -f(u) = -2v_-$. The algebra is $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ in its three-dimensional representation of weights $2, 0, -2$, which is the adjoint representation.

**Example ($A_1$ by Serre).** For $A = (2)$ there is no Serre relation for $i \neq j$, and the relations are $[H, E] = 2E$, $[H, F] = -2F$, $[E, F] = H$. The span of the images of $H, E, F$ is closed under brackets, so the quotient is spanned by them. The map to $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ sending them to the standard triple respects the relations, so it is defined and onto; as $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ has dimension $3$, it is an isomorphism.

**Example ($G_2$).** Take the base $\alpha_1 = \alpha$ short and $\alpha_2 = \beta$ long, as in Sections 1 and 2. Then $\dim V = 2 + 12 = 14 = \dim \mathfrak{g}_2$. The $\alpha$-chain through $\beta$ is $\beta, \beta + \alpha, \beta + 2\alpha, \beta + 3\alpha$, so $r = 0$ and $q = 3$, and $e_1(v_\beta) = v_{\alpha + \beta}$. The $\alpha$-chain through $\alpha + \beta$ has $r = 1$ and $q = 2$, so $e_1(v_{\alpha + \beta}) = 2v_{2\alpha + \beta}$. In the Serre convention the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, and the relations are $\operatorname{ad}(E_1)^4(E_2) = 0$ and $\operatorname{ad}(E_2)^2(E_1) = 0$, matching the fact that $4\alpha + \beta$ and $\alpha + 2\beta$ are not roots.

Common errors of a learner: The first error is to use the same matrix in Serre and Geck. `Matrix.ToLieAlgebra` uses $[H_i, E_j] = A_{ij}E_j$, while `LieAlgebra.Basis` and the Geck construction use $[h_j, e_i] = A_{ij}e_i$; they differ by a transpose, which is why `CartanMatrix.G₂` is the transpose of `b.cartanMatrix` for $\alpha_1$ short. The second error is to expect `EmbeddedG2` to be a proposition; it carries a choice of roots, and `IsG2.toEmbeddedG2` makes one. The third error is to forget that Geck's space is indexed by `b.support ⊕ ι`, not by a basis of $M$. The fourth error is to apply `instHasTrivialRadical` without `IsAlgClosed`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Check the four coroot formulas for the roots of $G_2$. Hint: $(3\alpha + \beta)^\vee = s_\alpha^\vee(\beta^\vee) = \beta^\vee - \langle \alpha, \beta^\vee \rangle \alpha^\vee$.
2. (A) For $A_2$, write $h_1$ as a diagonal matrix of size $8$ and check that its trace is $0$. Hint: the weights on $v_j$ are $\langle \alpha_j, \alpha_1^\vee \rangle$ for the six roots.
3. (A) In $G_2$, show that the bottom coefficient of the $\alpha$-chain through $\alpha + \beta$ is $1$ although $\langle \alpha, (\alpha + \beta)^\vee \rangle \neq 0$. Hint: this is why `chainBotCoeff_if_one_zero` needs `IsNotG2`.
4. (B) Show that in a reduced crystallographic system, $p_{ij} = -3$ forces $p_{ji} = -1$, and deduce that the root with $p_{ij} = -3$ is the longer one. Hint: use the pair list and $p_{ij}B(\alpha_j, \alpha_j) = p_{ji}B(\alpha_i, \alpha_i)$.
5. (B) In the Geck construction for $B_2$ with $\alpha_1$ long, compute $e_2(v_{\alpha_1})$ and $e_2(v_{\alpha_1 + \alpha_2})$. Hint: the $\alpha_2$-chain through $\alpha_1$ is $\alpha_1, \alpha_1 + \alpha_2, \alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2$.
6. (C) Let $L$ be simple with a basis indexed by $\Delta$. Using Chapter 6, show that $\dim L = |\Delta| + |\Phi|$, so $\dim L = \dim V$ for the Geck construction. Hint: root spaces are lines and $H$ has dimension $|\Delta|$.

### Notes and history

$G_2$ was found by Killing in 1887 as the smallest exceptional simple Lie algebra, of dimension $14$. Serre's presentation by generators and relations appeared in his 1966 book on complex semisimple Lie algebras, which Mathlib cites. Geck's construction is from his 2017 paper on the construction of semisimple Lie algebras and Chevalley groups, and the Lean files follow it lemma by lemma. The header of `GeckConstruction.Basic` compares it with Serre's quotient and with a bracket on $H \oplus K^{|\Phi|}$ fixed by extraspecial pairs, and notes that no construction is known without a choice of base.

The Mathlib files are by these authors, read from their headers: `Finite.G2`, the `GeckConstruction` files and the `Lie.Basis` files by Oliver Nash; `SerreConstruction` by Oliver Nash and Jonathan Reich.
