## Normal extensions

### Overview

The field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ contains one root of $X^3 - 2$ and not the other two, which are not real. An automorphism of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ must send $\sqrt[3]{2}$ to a root of $X^3 - 2$ in the same field, so it is the identity. The field is too small to have symmetries. A normal extension is one that does not have this defect: if it contains one root of an irreducible polynomial over $K$, it contains all of them.

This section proves that a finite extension is normal if and only if it is a splitting field. It then proves that each embedding of a normal extension $L/K$ into a larger field maps $L$ onto itself. So automorphisms of a larger extension restrict to $L$, and two elements of $L$ with the same minimal polynomial differ by an automorphism. The section ends with the normal closure.

The section uses splitting fields and the theorem that an isomorphism of fields extends to their splitting fields (Chapter 3), and the embeddings of a simple extension (Chapter 4, Section 1).

### Definitions

**Definition (Normal extension).** An extension $L/K$ is normal if it is algebraic and the minimal polynomial over $K$ of each $\alpha \in L$ splits in $L$ into linear factors.

An equivalent form: $L/K$ is algebraic, and each irreducible $f \in K[X]$ that has a root in $L$ splits in $L$. Indeed such an $f$ is, up to a constant, the minimal polynomial of the root. The definition says nothing about separability: a normal extension may have repeated roots.

**Definition (Normal closure).** Let $L/K$ be algebraic and $\Omega$ an algebraically closed field that contains $L$. The normal closure of $L/K$ in $\Omega$ is the subfield of $\Omega$ generated by the roots in $\Omega$ of the minimal polynomials over $K$ of all the elements of $L$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Normal means splitting field).** A finite extension $L/K$ is normal if and only if $L$ is the splitting field over $K$ of some $f \in K[X]$.

*Proof.* Let $L/K$ be finite and normal, and let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ be a basis of $L$ over $K$. Let $f = m_1 \cdots m_n$ be the product of the minimal polynomials. Each $m_i$ splits in $L$, so $f$ splits in $L$, and $L$ is generated over $K$ by roots of $f$, namely the $\alpha_i$, and so by all the roots of $f$. So $L$ is a splitting field of $f$.

Conversely let $L$ be a splitting field of $f$ over $K$, with roots $\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_r$, so $L = K(\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_r)$. Let $\alpha \in L$ with minimal polynomial $m$, and let $N$ be a splitting field of $m$ over $L$. Let $\beta \in N$ be a root of $m$. It suffices to show $\beta \in L$.

The fields $K(\alpha)$ and $K(\beta)$ are both isomorphic to $K[X]/(m)$, so there is an isomorphism $\tau : K(\alpha) \to K(\beta)$ over $K$ with $\tau(\alpha) = \beta$. The field $L = K(\alpha)(\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_r)$ is a splitting field of $f$ over $K(\alpha)$. The field $L(\beta) = K(\beta)(\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_r)$ is a splitting field of $f$ over $K(\beta)$. The polynomial $f$ has its coefficients in $K$, so $\tau$ maps $f$ to $f$. By the extension theorem of Chapter 3, $\tau$ extends to an isomorphism $L \to L(\beta)$. In particular $[L : K(\alpha)] = [L(\beta) : K(\beta)]$. With the tower law and $[K(\alpha):K] = \deg m = [K(\beta):K]$:
$$[L(\beta) : K] = [L(\beta) : K(\beta)] \, [K(\beta) : K] = [L : K(\alpha)] \, [K(\alpha) : K] = [L : K].$$
Since $L \subseteq L(\beta)$ and the degrees over $K$ agree, $L(\beta) = L$, so $\beta \in L$. $\square$

**Corollary (Degree two).** Each extension $L/K$ of degree $2$ is normal.

*Proof.* Let $\alpha \in L \setminus K$. Then $L = K(\alpha)$, since $1 < [K(\alpha):K]$ divides $2$. The minimal polynomial is $X^2 + bX + c$ with $b, c \in K$. Its second root is $-b - \alpha \in L$. So $L$ is the splitting field of $X^2 + bX + c$. $\square$

Mathlib has this as the instance `Algebra.IsQuadraticExtension.normal`.

**Theorem (Upper part of a tower).** Let $L/K$ be normal and $K \subseteq M \subseteq L$. Then $L/M$ is normal.

*Proof.* Let $\alpha \in L$. Its minimal polynomial over $M$ divides its minimal polynomial over $K$, which splits in $L$. A divisor of a polynomial that splits also splits, by unique factorization in $L[X]$. $\square$

The lower part $M/K$ need not be normal: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ lies in the normal extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ of the first example, and it is not normal.

**Theorem (Embeddings of a normal extension).** Let $L/K$ be normal, let $E$ be an extension of $L$, and let $\sigma : L \to E$ be a $K$-embedding. Then $\sigma(L) = L$. In particular each $K$-embedding $L \to L$ is an automorphism.

*Proof.* Let $\alpha \in L$ with minimal polynomial $m$ over $K$. Since $\sigma$ fixes the coefficients of $m$, $m(\sigma(\alpha)) = 0$. Since $m$ splits in $L$, $m = c \prod_i (X - \alpha_i)$ with all $\alpha_i \in L$, and this factorization also holds in $E[X]$. So the roots of $m$ in $E$ are the $\alpha_i$, and $\sigma(\alpha) \in L$. So $\sigma(L) \subseteq L$.

For the other inclusion, let $\beta \in L$ with minimal polynomial $m$, and let $R \subseteq L$ be the finite set of the roots of $m$ in $L$. The map $\sigma$ is injective and maps $R$ into $R$, by the first part. An injective map of a finite set into itself is surjective, so $\beta = \sigma(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha \in R$. $\square$

**Corollary (Restriction).** Let $K \subseteq M \subseteq N$ with $M/K$ normal. Each $\sigma \in \operatorname{Aut}(N/K)$ maps $M$ onto $M$, and $\sigma \mapsto \sigma|_M$ is a group homomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(N/K) \to \operatorname{Aut}(M/K)$. If $N/K$ is normal and finite, the homomorphism is surjective.

*Proof.* The restriction $\sigma|_M : M \to N$ is a $K$-embedding, so $\sigma(M) = M$ by the theorem, and $\sigma|_M$ is an automorphism of $M$. Restriction respects composition. For the surjectivity, let $\tau \in \operatorname{Aut}(M/K)$. Since $N/K$ is normal and finite, $N$ is a splitting field over $K$ of some $f$, so also over $M$. By the extension theorem of Chapter 3, applied to $\tau : M \to M$ and the polynomial $f$, which $\tau$ fixes, $\tau$ extends to an isomorphism $\sigma : N \to N$. $\square$

**Theorem (Conjugates).** Let $L/K$ be finite and normal, and $x, y \in L$. Then $x$ and $y$ have the same minimal polynomial over $K$ if and only if $y = \sigma(x)$ for some $\sigma \in \operatorname{Aut}(L/K)$.

*Proof.* If $y = \sigma(x)$, then $y$ is a root of the minimal polynomial $m$ of $x$, and $m$ is irreducible, so it is the minimal polynomial of $y$. Conversely let $x$ and $y$ have the same minimal polynomial $m$. There is a $K$-isomorphism $\tau : K(x) \to K(y)$ with $\tau(x) = y$. The field $L$ is a splitting field over $K$ of some $f$, so it is a splitting field of $f$ over $K(x)$ and over $K(y)$. By Chapter 3, $\tau$ extends to an isomorphism $\sigma : L \to L$. $\square$

**Theorem (Normal closure).** Let $L = K(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ be finite over $K$, inside an algebraically closed field $\Omega$, and let $m_i$ be the minimal polynomial of $\alpha_i$. The normal closure $N$ of $L/K$ in $\Omega$ is the splitting field in $\Omega$ of $f = m_1 \cdots m_n$. It is finite and normal over $K$, and each normal extension of $K$ in $\Omega$ that contains $L$ contains $N$.

*Proof.* The splitting field $N'$ of $f$ in $\Omega$ is generated by the roots of the $m_i$, so $N' \subseteq N$. It is normal by the first theorem, so it contains all the roots of the minimal polynomial of each of its elements, in particular of each element of $L$. So $N \subseteq N'$, and $N = N'$. A normal extension $M$ of $K$ that contains $L$ contains the $\alpha_i$, so it contains all the roots of the $m_i$, and $N \subseteq M$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (A field with a missing root).** The extension $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal. The polynomial $X^3 - 2$ is irreducible (Chapter 2) and has the root $\sqrt[3]{2}$ in the field. Its other roots, $\sqrt[3]{2}\,\omega$ and $\sqrt[3]{2}\,\omega^2$ with $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, are not real, and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Its normal closure in $\mathbb{C}$ is the splitting field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$. Its degree is $6$: the tower law gives $[\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega) : \mathbb{Q}] = 3 \cdot [\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})(\omega) : \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})]$, and the second factor is $2$, since $\omega$ is a root of $X^2 + X + 1$ and is not real.

**Example (Normality is not transitive).** Let $a = \sqrt[4]{2} \in \mathbb{R}$. In the tower $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(a)$ each step has degree $2$, so each step is normal by the corollary. But $\mathbb{Q}(a)/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal: $X^4 - 2$ is irreducible by Eisenstein at $2$, and its root $i a$ is not real. So the hypothesis "$L/K$ normal" in the theorem on towers cannot be replaced by "$L/M$ and $M/K$ normal".

### Exercises

1. (A) Decide which of these extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$ are normal: $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[4]{2}, i)$, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{1 + \sqrt 2})$, $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ with $\zeta_5 = e^{2\pi i/5}$.
2. (A) Show that $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ is not normal, by a polynomial with one real root and two nonreal roots.
3. (A) Let $L/K$ be normal and $M$, $M'$ two normal intermediate fields. Show that $M \cap M'$ is normal over $K$.
4. (B) Show that the compositum of two finite normal extensions inside a common field is normal. Hint: use splitting fields.
5. (B) Let $L/K$ be finite and normal, and $\alpha \in L$ with minimal polynomial $m$ of degree $d$. Show that $\operatorname{Aut}(L/K)$ acts transitively on the roots of $m$ in $L$, and deduce that the number of roots is at most $d$ and divides $|\operatorname{Aut}(L/K)|$.
6. (B) Find the degree of the normal closure of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{1 + \sqrt 2})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. Hint: the conjugates are $\pm\sqrt{1 \pm \sqrt 2}$, and $\sqrt{1 + \sqrt 2}\sqrt{1 - \sqrt 2} = \sqrt{-1}$ up to sign.
7. (C) Let $L/K$ be algebraic. Show that $L/K$ is normal if and only if each $K$-embedding of $L$ into an algebraic closure of $L$ has the image $L$.

### Notes and history

The notion goes back to Galois, who worked with the field generated by all the roots of a polynomial. The name normal extension became standard with the abstract theory of fields after Steinitz.
