## Grading, involutions and the spin group

### Overview

The relation $\iota(x)^2 = Q(x)$ is not homogeneous for the grading by degree: the left side has degree $2$ and the right side has degree $0$. So the Clifford algebra has no $\mathbb{N}$-grading in general. The parity of the degree survives, and $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ is graded by $\mathbb{Z}/2$. This section proves this, defines two involutions, identifies the even part as a Clifford algebra, and ends with the Lipschitz, pin and spin groups.

The section uses the definition, the universal property and the rank theorem of Section 1 of this chapter, and the graded algebras of Chapter 3. Here $R$ is a commutative ring and $Q$ is a quadratic form on $M$, with polar form $B$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Even and odd parts).** For $i \in \mathbb{Z}/2$, let $\mathrm{Cl}^i(Q)$ be the span of all products $\iota(x_1) \cdots \iota(x_k)$ with $k \equiv i \pmod 2$. The even part $\mathrm{Cl}^0(Q)$ contains $1$ and is closed under products, so it is a subalgebra.

**Definition (Grade involution and reversion).** The grade involution $\alpha$ is the algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \to \mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ with $\alpha(\iota x) = -\iota x$. It exists by the universal property, since $(-\iota x)^2 = Q(x)$. The reversion is the linear map $\beta$ with $\beta(\iota(x_1) \cdots \iota(x_k)) = \iota(x_k) \cdots \iota(x_1)$. It is the lift of $\iota$ into the opposite algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)^{\mathrm{op}}$, where $(\iota x)^2 = Q(x)$ also holds. So $\beta(ab) = \beta(b)\beta(a)$. The Clifford conjugate is $\bar a = \beta(\alpha(a))$.

**Definition (Lipschitz, pin and spin groups).** The Lipschitz group $\Gamma(Q)$ is the subgroup of the units of $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ generated by the units of the form $\iota(u)$. The pin group is the set of $x \in \Gamma(Q)$ with $\bar x x = x \bar x = 1$. The spin group is the set of even elements of the pin group.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (The $\mathbb{Z}/2$ grading).** $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) = \mathrm{Cl}^0(Q) \oplus \mathrm{Cl}^1(Q)$, and $\mathrm{Cl}^i(Q) \, \mathrm{Cl}^j(Q) \subseteq \mathrm{Cl}^{i + j}(Q)$. So $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ is a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded algebra, with $\iota(M) \subseteq \mathrm{Cl}^1(Q)$.

*Proof.* The products of vectors span $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$, so the two parts span it. A product of $k$ vectors times a product of $l$ vectors is a product of $k + l$ vectors, which gives the product rule. For the directness, let $A = \mathrm{Cl}^0(Q) \oplus \mathrm{Cl}^1(Q)$ be the external direct sum, an algebra by the product rule (Chapter 3, Section 1). Let $\mathrm{of}_i$ be the inclusion of the summand $i$. The map $f(x) = \mathrm{of}_1(\iota x)$ satisfies $f(x)^2 = \mathrm{of}_0(\iota(x)^2) = \mathrm{of}_0(Q(x))$, the scalar $Q(x)$ in $A$. Let $\psi : \mathrm{Cl}(Q) \to A$ be its lift. Both $\psi$ and $\mathrm{of}$ are multiplicative, so $\psi(a) = \mathrm{of}_i(a)$ for each product $a$ of vectors of parity $i$, and so for each $a \in \mathrm{Cl}^i(Q)$. If $a_0 + a_1 = 0$ with $a_i \in \mathrm{Cl}^i(Q)$, then $\mathrm{of}_0(a_0) + \mathrm{of}_1(a_1) = \psi(0) = 0$, so $a_0 = a_1 = 0$. $\square$

The proof needs no hypothesis on $2$. When $2$ is a unit, the two parts are the eigenspaces of $\alpha$, since $\alpha = (-1)^i$ on $\mathrm{Cl}^i(Q)$.

**Theorem (The even part is a Clifford algebra).** Let $Q'$ be the form on $M \times R$ with $Q'(m, r) = Q(m) - r^2$. There is an algebra isomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \cong \mathrm{Cl}^0(Q')$ with $\iota(m) \mapsto e_0\, v(m)$, where $e_0 = \iota'(0, 1)$ and $v(m) = \iota'(m, 0)$.

*Proof, for $M$ free with an orthogonal basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$.* The vectors $(0,1)$ and $(m, 0)$ are orthogonal for $Q'$, so $e_0 v(m) = -v(m) e_0$, and $e_0^2 = Q'(0,1) = -1$. Let $f(m) = e_0 v(m)$, which lies in $\mathrm{Cl}^0(Q')$. Then
$$f(m)^2 = e_0 v(m) e_0 v(m) = -e_0^2 v(m)^2 = Q(m),$$
so $f$ lifts to $\varphi : \mathrm{Cl}(Q) \to \mathrm{Cl}^0(Q')$. The image of $\varphi$ is a subalgebra that contains each $f(m)$. Write $\iota'(m, r) = v(m) + r e_0$. The even part is spanned by products of pairs $\iota'(m_1, r_1)\iota'(m_2, r_2)$, and each pair expands into the four terms
$$v(m_1) v(m_2) = f(m_1) f(m_2), \quad v(m_1) e_0 = -f(m_1), \quad e_0 v(m_2) = f(m_2), \quad e_0^2 = -1.$$
So $\varphi$ is surjective. The basis $(e_i)$ with $(0, 1)$ added is an orthogonal basis of $M \times R$. By the rank theorem of Section 1, $\mathrm{Cl}(Q')$ has the basis $e_S$ over the subsets of $n + 1$ indices, and each $e_S$ lies in $\mathrm{Cl}^{|S|}(Q')$. By the grading theorem, the $2^n$ elements $e_S$ with $|S|$ even form a basis of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(Q')$. So $\varphi$ is a surjective linear map between free modules of the same finite rank $2^n$, and it is bijective (Volume 7). $\square$

**Theorem (Twisted conjugation by vectors).** Let $u \in M$ with $Q(u)$ a unit of $R$. Then $\iota(u)$ is a unit with inverse $Q(u)^{-1}\iota(u)$, and for all $v \in M$,
$$\iota(u)\, \iota(v)\, \iota(u)^{-1} = -\iota(s_u v), \qquad s_u v = v - \frac{B(u, v)}{Q(u)}\, u.$$
The map $s_u$ is an isometry of $Q$, the reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to $u$. So each $x$ in the group generated by such $\iota(u)$ satisfies $x\, \iota(M)\, x^{-1} = \iota(M)$.

*Proof.* The inverse follows from $\iota(u)^2 = Q(u)$. By the rule for two vectors, $\iota(u)\iota(v) = B(u, v) - \iota(v)\iota(u)$. Multiply on the right by $Q(u)^{-1}\iota(u)$:
$$\iota(u)\iota(v)\iota(u)^{-1} = \frac{B(u,v)}{Q(u)}\,\iota(u) - \iota(v) = -\iota(s_u v).$$
For the isometry, $Q(s_u v) = Q(v) - \frac{B(u,v)}{Q(u)} B(u, v) + \frac{B(u,v)^2}{Q(u)^2} Q(u) = Q(v)$. The set of units $x$ with $x\,\iota(M)\,x^{-1} \subseteq \iota(M)$ is closed under products, and the generators and their inverses $Q(u)^{-1}\iota(u)$ lie in it. Equality holds since $x^{-1}$ is in the group too. $\square$

So conjugation by $\iota(u)$ is $-s_u$ on $M$. The twisted action $\iota(v) \mapsto \alpha(x)\, \iota(v)\, x^{-1}$ is $s_u$ for $x = \iota(u)$, so a product of vectors acts by the product of their reflections. For an even $x$ the two actions agree.

### Examples

**Example (The spin group of the plane).** Let $Q(x, y) = -x^2 - y^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, so $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) = \mathbb{H}$ with $i = \iota(1,0)$ and $j = \iota(0,1)$, and the even part is $\{a + b k\}$. On $k = ij$, $\alpha(k) = k$ and $\beta(k) = ji = -k$, so $\overline{a + bk} = a - bk$ and $\overline{x} x = a^2 + b^2$. For $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ write $x = \cos\theta + \sin\theta\, k$. Then $x = (\cos\theta\, i + \sin\theta\, j)(-i)$, a product of two unit vectors, so $x \in \Gamma(Q)$ and $x$ lies in the spin group. The spin group is the circle $a^2 + b^2 = 1$. From $ki = j$ and $kj = -i$,
$$x\, i\, x^{-1} = \cos 2\theta\, i + \sin 2\theta\, j, \qquad x\, j\, x^{-1} = -\sin 2\theta\, i + \cos 2\theta\, j.$$
So $x$ acts on the plane by the rotation through $2\theta$, and $x$ and $-x$ give the same rotation.

**Counterexample (The norm condition).** Let $Q(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ and $e = \iota(1)$, so $e^2 = 1$ and $e$ is a unit in $\Gamma(Q)$. But $\bar e = -e$, so $\bar e e = -1 \neq 1$. So $e$ is in the Lipschitz group but not in the pin group. With the convention of Mathlib, over a field, a vector $u$ lies in the pin group exactly when $Q(u) = -1$.

**Counterexample (The reversion is not an algebra map).** In the quaternions as above, $\beta(ij) = ji = -k$, but $\beta(i)\beta(j) = ij = k$. So $\beta$ reverses products and is not an algebra homomorphism. For a product of $p$ orthogonal vectors, $\beta$ changes the sign by $(-1)^{p(p-1)/2}$, since the reversal needs $p(p-1)/2$ swaps.

### The library around it

From the contraction by a linear form, the file `Contraction` builds `CliffordAlgebra.changeForm`, a linear map $\mathrm{Cl}(Q) \to \mathrm{Cl}(Q')$ for each bilinear form $B'$ with $B'(x, x) = Q'(x) - Q(x)$. It is bijective. When $2$ is a unit, the choice $B' = -\frac12 B$ gives $Q' = 0$ and the linear isomorphism `CliffordAlgebra.equivExterior` with the exterior algebra of Chapter 4.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\alpha \circ \alpha = \mathrm{id}$ and $\beta \circ \beta = \mathrm{id}$. Hint: check on products of vectors.
2. (A) Show that $\alpha \circ \beta = \beta \circ \alpha$. Hint: both send $\iota(x_1)\cdots\iota(x_k)$ to $(-1)^k \iota(x_k) \cdots \iota(x_1)$.
3. (A) In $\mathrm{Cl}(Q)$ for $Q = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, compute $\alpha$, $\beta$ and the conjugate of $e_1 e_2 e_3$. Hint: use the sign $(-1)^{p(p-1)/2}$.
4. (B) For the dual numbers $\mathrm{Cl}(0)$ on $R$, find the even and odd parts and the Lipschitz group. Hint: $\iota(u)^2 = 0$, so no $\iota(u)$ is a unit.
5. (B) Show that $\mathrm{Cl}^0(Q) \cong \mathrm{Cl}^0(-Q)$ when $M$ has an orthogonal basis. Hint: send $e_S$ to $(-1)^{|S|/2} e_S$, and check the product rule of the model of Section 1.
6. (B) For $Q = -x^2 - y^2 - z^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, use the theorem on the even part to show that $\mathrm{Cl}^0(Q) \cong \mathbb{H}$. Hint: apply the theorem to $Q = -x^2 - y^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
7. (C) Let $Q = -x^2 - y^2 - z^2$. Show that the unit quaternions in $\mathrm{Cl}^0(Q) \cong \mathbb{H}$ form the spin group, and that each acts on $\mathbb{R}^3$ by a rotation. Hint: write a unit quaternion as a product of two unit vectors.

### Notes and history

Clifford introduced the algebras in 1878, as Section 1 records. The quaternions of Hamilton, from 1843, give the case of rotations of space, as Exercise 7 shows. Mathlib proves the even part theorem for every module, through a universal property of the even part.
