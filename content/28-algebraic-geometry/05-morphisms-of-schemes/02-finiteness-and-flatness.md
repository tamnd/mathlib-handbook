## Finiteness and flatness

### Overview

A morphism of finite type is the relative form of a finitely generated algebra, and a finite morphism is the relative form of a finite module. These notions control the size of fibers. Flatness is of a different kind. A flat morphism is a family whose fibers vary continuously, and a faithfully flat morphism is a cover along which properties descend. This section gives the definitions, compares finite and integral morphisms, proves the stalk criterion for flatness, proves descent of isomorphisms, and studies the rank of a finite flat morphism.

All properties here come from properties of ring homomorphisms by the construction `affineLocally` of Section 1. So each is local on the target and on the source, and on $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ it is the property of $A \to B$.

### Definitions

Let $f : X \to Y$ be a morphism of schemes. For affine opens $U \subseteq Y$ and $V \subseteq f^{-1}(U)$, write $f^\sharp_{U,V} : \mathcal{O}_Y(U) \to \mathcal{O}_X(V)$ as in Section 1.

**Definition (Locally of finite type).** The morphism $f$ is *locally of finite type* when every $f^\sharp_{U,V}$ makes $\mathcal{O}_X(V)$ a finitely generated $\mathcal{O}_Y(U)$-algebra. It is *locally of finite presentation* when every $\mathcal{O}_X(V)$ is a finitely presented $\mathcal{O}_Y(U)$-algebra: a quotient of a polynomial ring in finitely many variables by a finitely generated ideal.

Over a Noetherian $Y$ the two notions agree, since every ideal of a polynomial ring over a Noetherian ring is finitely generated by the Hilbert basis theorem.

**Definition (Finite and integral).** The morphism $f$ is *finite* when it is affine and, for every affine open $U \subseteq Y$, the ring $\mathcal{O}_X(f^{-1}U)$ is a finitely generated $\mathcal{O}_Y(U)$-module. It is *integral* when it is affine and every $\mathcal{O}_X(f^{-1}U)$ is integral over $\mathcal{O}_Y(U)$.

**Definition (Flat).** The morphism $f$ is *flat* when every $f^\sharp_{U,V}$ is flat: the functor $- \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_Y(U)} \mathcal{O}_X(V)$ is exact.

**Definition (Locally quasi-finite).** A ring homomorphism $A \to B$ is *quasi-finite* when every fiber ring $B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is a finite-dimensional $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$-algebra. The Stacks project also asks $A \to B$ to be of finite type; Mathlib does not. The morphism $f$ is *locally quasi-finite* when every $f^\sharp_{U,V}$ is quasi-finite. The morphism is *quasi-finite at* $x$ when the stalk map at $x$ is quasi-finite.

**Definition (Rank).** For $s \in Y$, the *rank* of $f$ at $s$ is the rank, as a module over the stalk of an affine neighbourhood of $s$, of the ring of $f$ over that neighbourhood. When $f$ is finite, flat and locally of finite presentation, the ring over an affine open $\operatorname{Spec} A \ni s$ is a finite projective $A$-module $B$, and the rank at $s = \mathfrak{p}$ is $\dim_{\kappa(\mathfrak{p})} B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Finite is integral of finite type).** A morphism is finite if and only if it is integral and locally of finite type.

*Proof.* All three properties are local on the target and an integral or finite morphism is affine, so it is enough to prove the claim for a ring homomorphism $A \to B$. Let $B$ be a finite $A$-module with generators $b_1, \dots, b_n$. The same elements generate $B$ as an $A$-algebra, so $A \to B$ is of finite type. Each $b \in B$ is integral over $A$ by the determinant trick (Volume 10, Chapter 1): write $b\, b_i = \sum_j a_{ij} b_j$, so the matrix $bI - (a_{ij})$ kills the vector $(b_j)$, its determinant kills each $b_j$ and so kills $1$, and the determinant is a monic polynomial in $b$. Conversely, let $B = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$ with each $b_i$ integral, say a root of a monic polynomial of degree $d_i$. Then $b_i^{d_i}$ is an $A$-combination of $1, b_i, \dots, b_i^{d_i - 1}$. By induction on the degree, every monomial in the $b_i$ is an $A$-combination of the monomials $b_1^{e_1} \cdots b_n^{e_n}$ with $e_i < d_i$. These are finitely many, so $B$ is a finite $A$-module. $\square$

**Proposition (Finite fibers).** A finite morphism has finite fibers, and each point of a fiber is closed in the fiber.

*Proof.* The claim is local on the target, so let $f$ be $\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ with $B$ a finite $A$-module. The fiber over $\mathfrak{p}$ is $\operatorname{Spec}(B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p}))$ (Chapter 3). The ring $C = B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is a finite-dimensional $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$-vector space, so every chain of ideals in $C$ is finite and $C$ is Artinian. An Artinian ring has finitely many primes, and all of them are maximal (Volume 9, Chapter 4). $\square$

So a finite morphism is locally quasi-finite. The converse fails, as the main example below shows.

**Theorem (Flatness on stalks).** A morphism $f$ is flat if and only if every stalk map $\mathcal{O}_{Y, f(x)} \to \mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ is flat.

*Proof.* Both sides are local on the target and the source, so let $f$ come from $\varphi : A \to B$. Let $\mathfrak{q} \subseteq B$ be prime and $\mathfrak{p} = \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{q})$. Suppose $\varphi$ is flat. Then $B_{\mathfrak{p}} = B \otimes_A A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is flat over $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, since flatness is stable under base change. The ring $B_{\mathfrak{q}}$ is a localization of $B_{\mathfrak{p}}$, and a localization is flat, so $B_{\mathfrak{q}}$ is flat over $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Conversely, suppose every $A_{\mathfrak{p}} \to B_{\mathfrak{q}}$ is flat. Let $N \to N'$ be an injective map of $A$-modules and $K$ the kernel of $N \otimes_A B \to N' \otimes_A B$. For every prime $\mathfrak{q}$ of $B$, localization at $\mathfrak{q}$ is exact and $(N \otimes_A B)_{\mathfrak{q}} = N_{\mathfrak{p}} \otimes_{A_{\mathfrak{p}}} B_{\mathfrak{q}}$. So $K_{\mathfrak{q}}$ is the kernel of $N_{\mathfrak{p}} \otimes B_{\mathfrak{q}} \to N'_{\mathfrak{p}} \otimes B_{\mathfrak{q}}$, which is $0$ because $N_{\mathfrak{p}} \to N'_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is injective and $B_{\mathfrak{q}}$ is flat. A $B$-module with all localizations at primes equal to $0$ is $0$, so $K = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem (Faithfully flat).** For a ring homomorphism $\varphi : A \to B$, the morphism $\operatorname{Spec} \varphi$ is flat and surjective if and only if $\varphi$ is faithfully flat.

*Proof.* Let $\varphi$ be faithfully flat: flat, and $M \otimes_A B \neq 0$ for every $A$-module $M \neq 0$. For a prime $\mathfrak{p}$, the fiber ring $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) \otimes_A B$ is nonzero, so it has a prime. The fiber over $\mathfrak{p}$ is not empty, and $\operatorname{Spec} \varphi$ is surjective. Conversely, let $\varphi$ be flat with surjective $\operatorname{Spec} \varphi$. For a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ of $A$, a prime $\mathfrak{q}$ of $B$ lies over $\mathfrak{m}$, so $\mathfrak{m} B \subseteq \mathfrak{q} \neq B$. A flat $\varphi$ with $\mathfrak{m} B \neq B$ for every maximal $\mathfrak{m}$ is faithfully flat (Volume 10, Chapter 8). $\square$

**Lemma (Descent of bijectivity).** Let $A \to A'$ be faithfully flat and $\psi : M \to N$ a map of $A$-modules. If $\psi \otimes_A A'$ is bijective, then $\psi$ is bijective.

*Proof.* Let $K = \ker \psi$ and $C = \operatorname{coker} \psi$. The sequence $0 \to K \to M \to N \to C \to 0$ is exact. Tensor with $A'$; flatness keeps it exact, so $K \otimes_A A'$ and $C \otimes_A A'$ are the kernel and cokernel of $\psi \otimes_A A'$. Both are $0$. Faithful flatness gives $K = 0$ and $C = 0$. $\square$

The index turns such lemmas into scheme statements. If a property $Q$ of ring maps codescends along faithfully flat maps, then the corresponding $P$ descends along morphisms that are surjective, flat and quasi-compact. With the lemma for $Q$ = bijective, this gives descent of isomorphisms.

**Theorem (Flat monomorphisms).** Let $f : X \to Y$ be flat, quasi-compact, surjective and a monomorphism. Then $f$ is an isomorphism.

*Proof.* Form the pullback $X \times_Y X$ with projections $p_1, p_2$. The morphism $f$ is a monomorphism, so $p_1 = p_2$: indeed $f \circ p_1 = f \circ p_2$. The diagonal $\Delta : X \to X \times_Y X$ satisfies $p_1 \circ \Delta = \mathrm{id}_X$. Also $\Delta \circ p_1$ and $\mathrm{id}$ agree after composition with $p_1$ and with $p_2 = p_1$, so $\Delta \circ p_1 = \mathrm{id}$ by the universal property. Hence $p_1$ is an isomorphism. Now $p_1 : X \times_Y X \to X$ is the base change of $f$ along $f$. The morphism $f$ is surjective, flat and quasi-compact, and isomorphisms descend along such morphisms. So $f$ is an isomorphism. $\square$

**Proposition (Rank and isomorphisms).** Let $f$ be finite, flat and locally of finite presentation. Then the rank of $f$ is locally constant, $f$ is surjective if and only if the rank is at least $1$ everywhere, and $f$ is an isomorphism if and only if the rank is $1$ everywhere.

*Proof.* Work over $\operatorname{Spec} A$ with $B$ a finite projective $A$-module. A finite projective module is locally free: each $\mathfrak{p}$ has $a \notin \mathfrak{p}$ with $B_a \cong A_a^n$, so the rank is $n$ on $D(a)$. By Nakayama's lemma, $B \otimes \kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = 0$ exactly when $B_{\mathfrak{p}} = 0$, so the fiber over $\mathfrak{p}$ is empty exactly when the rank at $\mathfrak{p}$ is $0$. For the last claim, let the rank be $1$. Over each $\mathfrak{p}$ the map $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) \to B \otimes \kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is a nonzero map of one-dimensional spaces, so it is bijective. Nakayama's lemma makes $A_{\mathfrak{p}} \to B_{\mathfrak{p}}$ surjective, and a surjection of free modules of equal finite rank is bijective. By the stalk criterion of Section 1, $f$ is an isomorphism. The converse is clear. $\square$

### Examples

**Counterexample (The hyperbola over the line).** Let $f : \operatorname{Spec} k[x, y]/(xy - 1) \to \operatorname{Spec} k[x]$ come from $x \mapsto x$. The ring $B = k[x, y]/(xy - 1)$ is $k[x, x^{-1}]$, the localization of $k[x]$ at $x$. So $f$ is the open immersion of $D(x)$, and it is of finite type, flat and locally quasi-finite. Its fiber over $(x)$ is empty, and its fiber over any other prime is one point. The morphism $f$ is not finite. Suppose $x^{-1}$ were integral over $k[x]$, with $x^{-n} + a_{n-1} x^{-(n-1)} + \dots + a_0 = 0$ and $a_i \in k[x]$. Multiply by $x^n$: $1 + a_{n-1} x + \dots + a_0 x^n = 0$. This is an equation in $k[x]$, since $k[x] \to k[x, x^{-1}]$ is injective. It says $1 = -x(a_{n-1} + \dots + a_0 x^{n-1})$, so $x$ is a unit in $k[x]$, which is false. So $B$ is not integral over $k[x]$ and $f$ is not finite. Geometrically, the points of the hyperbola run off to infinity as $x \to 0$. In Section 3 the same picture shows that $f$ is not universally closed.

**Example (The Gaussian integers).** The map $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}[i]$ makes $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ free with basis $1, i$. So $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}[i] \to \operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}$ is finite and flat of rank $2$. The fiber over $(p)$ is $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{F}_p[x]/(x^2 + 1)$. For $p = 2$: $x^2 + 1 = (x + 1)^2$ in $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$, so the fiber ring is $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x+1)^2$, one point with a nonzero nilpotent. For $p = 3$: the squares mod $3$ are $0$ and $1$, so $x^2 + 1$ has no root, it is irreducible, and the fiber ring is the field $\mathbb{F}_9$, one point. For $p = 5$: $x^2 + 1 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$ since $4 + 1 = 5$, and the Chinese remainder theorem gives $\mathbb{F}_5 \times \mathbb{F}_5$, two points. The generic fiber is $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Q}(i)$. In each case the fiber ring has dimension $2$ over the residue field, as the proposition on rank predicts. The rank is constant, but the number of points changes.

**Example (The squaring map).** Let $k[t] \to k[x]$ send $t$ to $x^2$. Then $k[x]$ is free over $k[t]$ with basis $1, x$, since every polynomial is $g(x^2) + x\, h(x^2)$ in a unique way. So $\mathbb{A}^1 \to \mathbb{A}^1$, $x \mapsto x^2$, is finite and flat of rank $2$. The fiber over $t = a$ is $k[x]/(x^2 - a)$. Section 4 studies where this map is étale.

**Counterexample (The origin is not flat).** The closed immersion $\operatorname{Spec} k \to \mathbb{A}^1_k$ of the origin comes from $k[x] \to k[x]/(x)$. Multiplication by $x$ on $k[x]$ is injective. After $- \otimes_{k[x]} k$ it becomes multiplication by $0$ on $k$, which is not injective. So $k$ is not flat over $k[x]$, and the closed immersion is finite but not flat.

Common errors of a learner: `LocallyOfFiniteType` has no quasi-compactness; the morphism of finite type of the literature is `LocallyOfFiniteType f ∧ QuasiCompact f`. `IsFinite` includes `IsAffineHom`, so it is not only a condition on fibers. The rank `Scheme.Hom.finrank` is defined for every morphism, but it has meaning only for finite, flat and finitely presented ones. Flatness descent needs quasi-compactness of the cover; surjective and flat is not enough in general.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that a closed immersion is finite. *Hint:* A quotient $A/I$ is generated by $1$ as an $A$-module.
2. (A) Show that $\operatorname{Spec} k[x] \to \operatorname{Spec} k$ is of finite type and flat but not finite. *Hint:* The powers of $x$ are linearly independent over $k$.
3. (B) Compute the fiber of $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}[i] \to \operatorname{Spec} \mathbb{Z}$ over $(13)$ and over $(7)$. *Hint:* Decide whether $-1$ is a square mod $p$; the rule is $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.
4. (B) Let $f : \operatorname{Spec} k[x, y]/(y^2 - x^3) \to \operatorname{Spec} k[x]$. Show that $f$ is finite and flat of rank $2$ and describe the fibers when $k$ is algebraically closed of characteristic not $2$. *Hint:* The basis is $1, y$; the fiber over $x = a$ has two points for $a \neq 0$ and one point for $a = 0$.
5. (B) Show that $k[x] \to k[x, y]/(xy)$ is not flat. *Hint:* Multiplication by $x$ on $k[x]$ is injective, but $x \cdot y = 0$ in the target.
6. (C) Show that the morphism $\coprod_{n \geq 1} \operatorname{Spec} k \to \operatorname{Spec} k$ is flat and surjective but not quasi-compact, and that the target has the quotient topology anyway. Then find a flat surjective morphism that is not a quotient map. *Hint:* For the second part, take the disjoint union of $\operatorname{Spec} \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{A}^1, \mathfrak{m}}$ over the closed points $\mathfrak{m}$ of $\mathbb{A}^1_k$, with $k$ infinite, and look at the generic point.

### Notes and history

Grothendieck made flatness and faithfully flat descent basic tools of algebraic geometry in EGA and SGA 1, 1960 to 1967. Chevalley proved that the image of a constructible set is constructible. The Stacks project is the source of the tags in the Mathlib docstrings.
