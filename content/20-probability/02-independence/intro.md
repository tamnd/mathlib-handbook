# Independence

Independence is the notion that separates probability from the general theory of measures. Two events are independent when the probability of their intersection is the product of their probabilities. This one equation, extended to families of events, to σ-algebras and to random variables, gives probability its own theorems: the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem and the zero-one laws all start from it.

Section 1 defines independence of events and of σ-algebras, shows by an example that pairwise independence is weaker than independence, and proves the two structural tools of the chapter: the π-system criterion, which reduces independence of σ-algebras to independence of generators, and the grouping theorem, which says that disjoint blocks of an independent family are independent. Section 2 defines independent random variables, proves that independence is the factorisation of the joint law into the product of the laws, and computes the law of a sum as a convolution. It then builds an independent family with given laws on the infinite product space, the space of all later limit theorems. Section 3 proves the two Borel-Cantelli lemmas and Kolmogorov's zero-one law, and applies them to coin tosses, to long runs of heads and to random series.

The chapter needs Chapter 1 for probability spaces and laws, and Volume 18, Measure and integration, for the π-λ theorem, the uniqueness of measures, product measures and Fubini's theorem. In Mathlib, all the notions of independence live in the namespace `ProbabilityTheory` and are special cases of independence with respect to a kernel, a choice that the text explains in Section 1. Chapter 3 uses independence to compute expectations and variances of sums, and Chapter 5 uses all three sections.
