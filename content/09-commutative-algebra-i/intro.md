# Commutative algebra I: ideals, polynomials, factorization

This volume teaches the first half of commutative algebra and its form in Mathlib. Volume 6 gave a first view of ideals, quotient rings, polynomials and factorization: the isomorphism theorems, prime and maximal ideals, the field of fractions, division with remainder in $R[X]$, and the chain from Euclidean domain to principal ideal domain to unique factorization domain. This volume starts from there. It studies the ideals of an arbitrary commutative ring with the tools that the rest of algebra and algebraic geometry use: radicals, localization, chain conditions, primary decomposition, Nakayama's lemma, polynomials in several variables and power series. Each main theorem has a complete proof on paper, the Mathlib name of the result, and examples that compute.

Chapter 1 studies operations on ideals: the lattice of ideals, products and ideal quotients, extension and contraction along a ring homomorphism, the radical of an ideal, the nilradical and the Jacobson radical, the Chinese remainder theorem for finitely many ideals and the prime avoidance lemma. Chapter 2 builds the localization $S^{-1}R$ of a ring at a multiplicative set, describes its ideals and its prime ideals, defines local rings, and gives the first facts on the prime spectrum $\operatorname{Spec} R$ with its Zariski topology. Chapter 3 defines Noetherian rings and modules, proves the Hilbert basis theorem, and proves that each ideal of a Noetherian ring has a primary decomposition. Chapter 4 proves Nakayama's lemma and studies Artinian rings, up to the theorem that a ring is Artinian if and only if it is Noetherian of dimension zero. Chapter 5 returns to factorization: Gauss's lemma, the theorem that $R[X]$ is a unique factorization domain when $R$ is one, the criteria of Eisenstein and of reduction modulo a prime, derivatives and multiple roots, and the construction $R[X]/(f)$ that adjoins a root. Chapter 6 studies polynomials in several variables, their ideals, monomial orders and the division algorithm, and ends with Hilbert's Nullstellensatz. Chapter 7 studies formal power series in one and several variables, Laurent series and Hahn series. Chapter 8 is a guide to the Mathlib library of polynomials in one variable, from coefficients and degrees to roots, derivatives and modules over $R[X]$; it needs only Volume 6, Chapter 5, and a reader can open it at any point.

The volume needs Volume 6 in full, and modules, submodules, quotient modules and finitely generated modules from Volume 7. Zorn's lemma and the lattices of Volume 4 appear in the existence proofs of Chapters 1 and 3. Chapter 2, Section 3 uses only the definition of a topological space from Volume 13. Read Chapters 1 to 3 in order. Chapter 4 needs Chapters 1 and 3, Chapter 5 needs Chapter 1 and Volume 6, Chapter 6 needs Chapters 3 and 5, and Chapter 7 needs Chapters 1, 2, 3 and 6. A course on polynomials can read Chapter 5 right after Chapter 1.

The examples of the spec of the volume run through the text. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is Noetherian but not a unique factorization domain (Chapter 3). The polynomial $X^4 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ but reducible modulo every prime (Chapter 5). The ring $\mathbb{C}[x, y]/(xy)$, the ring of the two coordinate axes in the plane, has zero divisors and two minimal primes, and its zero set is the union of two lines (Chapter 6).

Later volumes build on this one. Volume 10 continues with valuations, Dedekind domains, dimension theory and local algebra, and it holds the Mathlib theory of local rings and of the prime spectrum, which this volume uses in its first form. Volume 11 on fields and Galois theory uses the irreducibility criteria and the construction of $R[X]/(f)$ of Chapter 5. Volume 12 on number theory uses localization and unique factorization. In Mathlib the objects of the volume are spread over the folder `Mathlib/RingTheory` and the folders `Mathlib/Algebra/Polynomial` and `Mathlib/Algebra/MvPolynomial`. Many of them are stated for a semiring or for a module where the textbook states them for a ring, and each section says where the library is more general than the text and why. The volume has no Lean companion yet, so the examples are on paper, and the Mathlib boxes give the names and the signatures of the library.
