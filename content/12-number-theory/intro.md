# Number theory

This volume teaches elementary and algebraic number theory and its form in Mathlib. Number theory studies the integers: which numbers divide which, which equations have integer solutions, how the primes are distributed. Volume 2 proved the first theorems of the subject: Euclid's algorithm, unique factorization, the Chinese remainder theorem, and the theorems of Fermat, Euler and Wilson. Volume 6 proved that the Gaussian integers form a Euclidean domain and deduced that a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ is a sum of two squares. This volume does not prove these results again. It refers to them by volume and chapter, and it goes further.

Chapter 1 treats the $p$-adic valuation, divisors and primality tests. Chapter 2 makes the arithmetic functions into a ring under Dirichlet convolution and proves Möbius inversion. Chapter 3 proves quadratic reciprocity and treats the Legendre and Jacobi symbols. Chapter 4 treats Pythagorean triples, Fermat's last theorem for the exponent $4$, sums of two and four squares, and Pell's equation. Chapter 5 builds $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and proves Hensel's lemma. Chapter 6 defines Dirichlet characters and their L-series. Chapter 7 gives the results on the distribution of primes that Mathlib has, up to the prime number theorem and Dirichlet's theorem. Chapter 8 is a first view of number fields, rings of integers, the class group and the unit theorem. Chapter 9 describes the modular forms of the library: the modular group, the slash action, $q$-expansions, Eisenstein series and the forms of level one. Chapter 10 treats irrational and transcendental numbers: the golden ratio, Niven's theorem, Liouville numbers, metric Diophantine approximation and Siegel's lemma.

The volume needs Volume 2 for the integers and `ZMod n`, Volume 5 for finite groups and cyclic groups, and Volume 6 for rings, ideals, quotients and the Gaussian integers. Chapter 5 uses the metric spaces and the completions of Volume 13. Chapters 6 and 7 use the series, the logarithm and the asymptotic notation of Volume 15. Chapter 8 uses the bases and the free modules of Volume 7.

Read Chapters 1 to 3 in order. Chapter 4 uses Chapter 3 only for the Legendre symbol, and Chapter 5 needs only Chapter 1. Chapter 7 uses the arithmetic functions of Chapter 2 and, for Dirichlet's theorem, the characters of Chapter 6. Chapter 8 can follow Chapter 4.

The Mathlib form of number theory mixes three styles. Elementary statements are about `ℕ` and `ℤ`, often with `Nat.Prime p` as a hypothesis and `[Fact p.Prime]` as an instance argument, so that `ZMod p` is a field. Statements about residues live in `ZMod n`, where a congruence becomes an equation. Algebraic and analytic statements use the general machinery: `ArithmeticFunction R` is a ring, a Dirichlet character is a `MulChar` on `ZMod n`, and a number field is a class `NumberField K`. The volume has no Lean companion yet, so the examples are on paper, and the Mathlib boxes give the names and the signatures of the library.
