# Cofinality and regular cardinals

Chapter 5 measured the size of a set. This chapter measures how fast an order reaches its top. The cofinality of an order is the least size of a cofinal subset, that is, a subset that has an element above each element of the order. The ordinal $\omega_\omega$ is very large, but the sequence $\omega_0, \omega_1, \omega_2, \dots$ climbs to it in countably many steps, so its cofinality is $\aleph_0$. The ordinal $\omega_1$ has no countable cofinal subset, so its cofinality is $\aleph_1$.

Section 1 defines the cofinality for each preorder, as Mathlib does, and specializes it to ordinals. It computes the cofinality of successors, sums and limits, and it proves the consequences of the theorem of König: $\kappa < \kappa^{\operatorname{cf} \kappa}$, and the continuum $2^{\aleph_0}$ is not $\aleph_\omega$. Section 2 separates the infinite cardinals into two classes. A cardinal is regular when its cofinality is itself, and singular otherwise. Each successor cardinal is regular, $\aleph_\omega$ is singular, and a regular strong limit cardinal above $\aleph_0$ is inaccessible. The universes of Lean give an inaccessible cardinal at once. The section also proves the infinite pigeonhole principle. Section 1 also gives the library of closed unbounded sets and stationary sets, up to the pressing down lemma of Fodor.

The chapter uses the ordinals of Chapter 2, the normal functions and fixed points of Chapter 4, and the alephs and the arithmetic of Chapter 5. The theorem of König comes from Chapter 1.
