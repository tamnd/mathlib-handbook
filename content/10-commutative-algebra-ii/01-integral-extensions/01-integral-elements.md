## Integral elements

### Overview

Over a field $K$, an element of an extension is algebraic if it is a root of a nonzero polynomial over $K$. Over a ring this notion is too weak. The number $1/2$ is a root of $2X - 1 \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, but it does not behave like an integer. The right notion over a ring asks for a monic polynomial: $x$ is integral over $R$ if $x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0$ with all $a_i \in R$. The integers are integral over $\mathbb{Z}$, and so are $\sqrt{2}$, $i$ and $(1 + \sqrt{5})/2$, but $1/2$ is not.

The main theorem of the section is a criterion by finite modules: $x$ is integral over $R$ if and only if $x$ lies in a subring of $A$ that is finitely generated as an $R$-module. The criterion turns a statement about one polynomial into a statement about a module, and modules are easy to combine. As a consequence, sums and products of integral elements are integral, and integrality is transitive in a tower $R \subseteq B \subseteq C$. After this section the reader can prove that an element is integral without finding its polynomial.

In the whole chapter, $R$ is a commutative ring and $A$ is a commutative $R$-algebra with structure map $\varphi : R \to A$. For $r \in R$ and $x \in A$ the product $rx$ means $\varphi(r)x$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Integral element).** An element $x \in A$ is integral over $R$ if there is a monic polynomial $p = X^n + a_{n-1}X^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 \in R[X]$ with $p(x) = 0$ in $A$. Such an equation is an equation of integral dependence of $x$.

**Definition (Integral algebra).** The algebra $A$ is integral over $R$, or an integral extension of $R$ when $\varphi$ is injective, if every element of $A$ is integral over $R$.

**Definition (Finite algebra).** The algebra $A$ is finite over $R$ if $A$ is finitely generated as an $R$-module.

For $x \in A$, write $R[x]$ for the subalgebra of $A$ generated by $x$: the set of the values $p(x)$ with $p \in R[X]$.

*Remarks.* The polynomial must be monic. If any nonzero leading coefficient is allowed, every rational number is a root of a polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, and the notion says nothing new over a domain. Over a field $K$ the two notions agree: divide by the leading coefficient. So an element of a field extension of $K$ is integral over $K$ if and only if it is algebraic over $K$.

The structure map need not be injective. For an ideal $I$ of $R$, every element of $R/I$ is integral over $R$: the class $\bar r$ is a root of $X - r$. The definition is about $\varphi$, not about a subring.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (Finiteness criterion).** For $x \in A$ the following are equivalent:

1. $x$ is integral over $R$;
2. $R[x]$ is a finitely generated $R$-module;
3. $x$ lies in a subring $C$ of $A$ that contains $\varphi(R)$ and is a finitely generated $R$-module;
4. there is an $R[x]$-module $M$ that is faithful (only $0 \in R[x]$ acts as zero on $M$) and finitely generated as an $R$-module.

*Proof.* The plan is $1 \Rightarrow 2 \Rightarrow 3 \Rightarrow 4 \Rightarrow 1$. Only the last step needs an idea.

$1 \Rightarrow 2$. Let $x^n = -(a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0)$. Let $N$ be the $R$-submodule of $A$ spanned by $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$. The power $x^n$ lies in $N$. If $x^{n+k} \in N$, then $x^{n+k+1} = x \cdot x^{n+k}$ is an $R$-combination of $x, \dots, x^n$, and each of these lies in $N$. By induction every power of $x$ lies in $N$, so $R[x] = N$, and $N$ has $n$ generators.

$2 \Rightarrow 3$. Take $C = R[x]$.

$3 \Rightarrow 4$. Take $M = C$. It is an $R[x]$-module because $R[x] \subseteq C$, and it is faithful: if $y \in R[x]$ and $yC = 0$, then $y = y \cdot 1 = 0$.

$4 \Rightarrow 1$. Let $m_1, \dots, m_n$ generate $M$ over $R$. For each $i$, the element $xm_i$ lies in $M$, so there are $a_{ij} \in R$ with
$$x m_i = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} m_j.$$
Let $T$ be the $n \times n$ matrix with entries $T_{ij} = \delta_{ij}x - a_{ij}$ in the commutative ring $R[x]$, and let $m$ be the column vector $(m_1, \dots, m_n)$. The equations say $Tm = 0$. Multiply on the left by the adjugate matrix $\operatorname{adj}(T)$. Since $\operatorname{adj}(T)\,T = \det(T)\,I$ over any commutative ring (Volume 7, Chapter 4), the result is $\det(T)\,m_j = 0$ for all $j$. The $m_j$ generate $M$, so $\det(T)$ acts as zero on $M$, and faithfulness gives $\det(T) = 0$. Expand the determinant: $\det(\delta_{ij}X - a_{ij}) \in R[X]$ is the characteristic polynomial of the matrix $(a_{ij})$, a monic polynomial of degree $n$. Its value at $x$ is $\det(T) = 0$. So $x$ is integral. $\square$

The argument of the last step is the determinant trick. It reappears in the proof of Nakayama's lemma in Chapter 2.

**Lemma (Finite over finite).** Let $R \to B \to C$ be ring maps. If $B$ is a finite $R$-module and $C$ is a finite $B$-module, then $C$ is a finite $R$-module.

*Proof.* Let $b_1, \dots, b_k$ generate $B$ over $R$ and $c_1, \dots, c_l$ generate $C$ over $B$. An element $c \in C$ is $\sum_j \beta_j c_j$ with $\beta_j \in B$, and each $\beta_j = \sum_i r_{ij} b_i$ with $r_{ij} \in R$. So $c = \sum_{i,j} r_{ij}\, b_i c_j$, and the $kl$ products $b_i c_j$ generate $C$ over $R$. $\square$

**Theorem (Sums and products).** If $x, y \in A$ are integral over $R$, then $x + y$, $x - y$ and $xy$ are integral over $R$.

*Proof.* The ring $R[x]$ is finite over $R$ by the criterion. The element $y$ is integral over $R$, so it is integral over $R[x]$ by the same equation. So $R[x][y] = R[x, y]$ is finite over $R[x]$, by the criterion over the base ring $R[x]$. By the lemma, $R[x, y]$ is finite over $R$. It is a subring of $A$ that contains $x + y$, $x - y$ and $xy$, so these elements are integral by condition 3. $\square$

Note what the proof avoids. It gives no equation for $x + y$. Such an equation exists, and it is the determinant of the proof of the criterion, but the proof does not need to compute it.

**Theorem (Transitivity).** Let $R \to B \to C$ be ring maps. If $B$ is integral over $R$ and $c \in C$ is integral over $B$, then $c$ is integral over $R$. In particular, if $B$ is integral over $R$ and $C$ is integral over $B$, then $C$ is integral over $R$.

*Proof.* Let $c^n + b_{n-1}c^{n-1} + \cdots + b_0 = 0$ with $b_i \in B$. Put $B_0 = R[b_0, \dots, b_{n-1}]$, the subring of $B$ generated by the image of $R$ and the $b_i$. Each $b_i$ is integral over $R$, so $R[b_0]$ is finite over $R$, $R[b_0, b_1]$ is finite over $R[b_0]$, and so on. By the lemma, applied $n$ times, $B_0$ is finite over $R$. The equation of $c$ has coefficients in $B_0$, so $B_0[c]$ is finite over $B_0$, and so finite over $R$. It contains $c$, and condition 3 of the criterion gives that $c$ is integral over $R$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Quadratic irrationals).** The number $\sqrt{2}$ is a root of $X^2 - 2$, so it is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$. The golden ratio $\tau = (1 + \sqrt{5})/2$ satisfies $\tau^2 = (6 + 2\sqrt{5})/4 = \tau + 1$, so it is a root of $X^2 - X - 1$, and it is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ even though it has a denominator. A root of unity $\zeta$ with $\zeta^n = 1$ is a root of $X^n - 1$. The imaginary unit $i$ is a root of $X^2 + 1$.

**Example (A sum, with its equation).** The theorem on sums says that $s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$. The equation is easy to find here. Here $s^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}$, so $(s^2 - 5)^2 = 24$, and $s^4 - 10s^2 + 1 = 0$. The proof of the criterion gives the same polynomial. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}]$ has the $\mathbb{Z}$-basis $1, \sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, \sqrt{6}$, and multiplication by $s$ has, in this basis, the matrix whose characteristic polynomial is $X^4 - 10X^2 + 1$.

**Example (One half is not integral).** Assume $(1/2)^n + a_{n-1}(1/2)^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0$ with $a_i \in \mathbb{Z}$. Multiply by $2^n$:
$$1 + 2\left(a_{n-1} + 2a_{n-2} + \cdots + 2^{n-1}a_0\right) = 0.$$
The left side is odd, so it is not zero. So $1/2$ is not integral over $\mathbb{Z}$. In the language of the criterion: $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ is not a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module, since a finite set of elements of $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$ has a common denominator $2^k$ and spans only a subset of $2^{-k}\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example (Faithfulness is needed).** Condition 4 of the criterion fails without the word faithful. Let $x = 1/2 \in \mathbb{Q}$ and let $M = \mathbb{Z}[1/2]/\mathbb{Z}[1/2] = 0$. Then $M$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module and an $R[x]$-module, but $x$ is not integral. In the proof, $\det(T)$ acts as zero on $M = 0$, and nothing follows. A less trivial case: $M = \mathbb{Z}[1/2]/\mathbb{Z}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}[1/2]$-module on which $2^k$ kills the class of $2^{-k}$, but $M$ is not finitely generated over $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Example (Quotients and polynomial rings).** For every ideal $I$ of $R$, the quotient $R/I$ is integral over $R$, since $\bar r$ is a root of $X - r$. A finite algebra is integral, by condition 3. The polynomial ring $R[X]$ is not integral over $R$ when $R \ne 0$: if $X$ were integral, $R[X]$ would be generated by $1, X, \dots, X^{n-1}$ as an $R$-module, and $X^n$ is not in their span.

### The library around it

The files `Mathlib/RingTheory/IntegralClosure/IsIntegral/` hold the definition and the closure lemmas. The naming pattern is `IsIntegral.op` for a closure property, with the hypotheses on the elements as explicit arguments: `IsIntegral.add hx hy`, `IsIntegral.pow hx n`, `IsIntegral.map f hx` for an algebra map `f`. The lemmas `isIntegral_zero`, `isIntegral_one`, `isIntegral_algebraMap`, `isIntegral_natCast` and `isIntegral_intCast` give the base cases. The lemma `IsIntegral.of_pow` says that $x$ is integral if a power $x^n$ with $n > 0$ is. The lemma `IsIntegral.of_finite` says that every element of a finite algebra is integral, and `Algebra.IsIntegral.of_finite` is the class form, an instance.

The lemma `isIntegral_iff_isIntegral_closure_finite` reduces integrality over $R$ to integrality over a subring generated by finitely many elements. It is the formal form of the step "the equation has only finitely many coefficients" in the proof of transitivity.

Common errors of a learner:

- `IsIntegral R x` takes the base ring first. The algebra is implicit and found from the type of `x`.
- In a tower, `IsIntegral R x` and `IsIntegral A x` are different statements, and `IsScalarTower R A B` must be in the context to move between them.
- `Algebra.IsIntegral R A` is a class. To use it on an element, write `Algebra.IsIntegral.isIntegral x`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Find an equation of integral dependence over $\mathbb{Z}$ for $\sqrt[3]{2} + 1$ and for $i\sqrt{2}$.
2. (A) Show that a rational number is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if it is an integer. Hint: write $x = a/b$ in lowest terms and argue as for $1/2$.
3. (A) Let $A = R[X]/(f)$ with $f$ monic of degree $n$. Show that $A$ is a free $R$-module of rank $n$ and that $A$ is integral over $R$.
4. (B) Let $x \in A$ be a unit. Show that $x^{-1}$ is integral over $R$ if and only if $x^{-1} \in R[x]$. Hint: multiply an equation of $x^{-1}$ by $x^{n-1}$.
5. (B) Let $B$ be integral over $R$ and $J$ an ideal of $B$. Show that $B/J$ is integral over $R/(J \cap R)$, where $J \cap R$ means the inverse image of $J$ in $R$.
6. (B) Let $S$ be a multiplicative subset of $R$ and $B$ integral over $R$. Show that $S^{-1}B$ is integral over $S^{-1}R$. Hint: divide an equation of $b$ by $s^n$.
7. (B) Use the proof of the criterion to compute an equation of $\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt[3]{3}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. Hint: the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}, \sqrt[3]{3}]$ has six basis elements, but a shorter route is $(x^6) = 8 \cdot 9$.
8. (C) Let $A$ be a ring, not necessarily commutative, and $x, y \in A$ integral over a central subring $R$ with $xy \ne yx$. Show that $x + y$ need not be integral. Hint: take $A = M_2(\mathbb{Q})$, $R = \mathbb{Z}$, and two idempotent matrices whose sum has a non-integral eigenvalue.

### Notes and history

Integral elements over $\mathbb{Z}$ go back to Dedekind, who defined the algebraic integers of a number field as the roots of monic integer polynomials and proved that they form a ring. The criterion by finitely generated modules, and the determinant trick in its proof, are the standard route in Atiyah and Macdonald, *Introduction to Commutative Algebra*, Proposition 5.1 and Corollary 5.3, and in Matsumura, *Commutative Ring Theory*, Theorem 9.1.

In Mathlib the predicate is stated for a ring homomorphism and for an algebra, and the base ring and the algebra need not be domains. The proof of the closure under sums goes through the finiteness of `Algebra.adjoin R {x, y}`, as in the text.
