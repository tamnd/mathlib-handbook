## Ring congruences

### Overview

A congruence relation on a ring $R$ is an equivalence relation $\sim$ that is compatible with both operations: if $w \sim x$ and $y \sim z$, then $w + y \sim x + z$ and $w y \sim x z$. The quotient set $R/{\sim}$ then has a unique ring structure for which the projection $R \to R/{\sim}$ is a ring homomorphism. For a ring with negation, the congruences correspond to the two-sided ideals: the class of $0$ is a two-sided ideal $I$, and $x \sim y$ holds exactly when $x - y \in I$. For a semiring the correspondence fails, because a semiring has no subtraction. So the congruence is the more general notion, and it gives quotients of semirings such as $\mathbb{N}$.

The six modules of this section build the type `RingCon R` of ring congruences, the quotient ring by a congruence, the complete lattice of congruences, and the homomorphism theorems. Volume 5 treats the same constructions for monoids with `Con`, and a ring congruence is a monoid congruence for the multiplication that is also an additive congruence. Section 4 uses `RingCon R` as the data of a two-sided ideal. In a commutative ring, the rest of this volume uses `Ideal R` and the quotient `R ⧸ I` of Section 7. The ring congruences are the tool for semirings, for noncommutative rings, and for the general constructions under the ideal API.

### The library around it

#### The type of congruences and the quotient ring

The module `Mathlib.RingTheory.Congruence.Defs` defines the structure `RingCon R` for a type with `Add R` and `Mul R`. It extends `Con R` with the field `add'`, so a congruence `c` gives `c.toCon : Con R` and `c.toAddCon : AddCon R`. The structure has a coercion to a function `R → R → Prop`, so `c a b` is the statement $a \sim b$. The lemmas `RingCon.refl`, `RingCon.symm` and `RingCon.trans` give the equivalence, and `RingCon.add` and `RingCon.mul` give the compatibility: from `c w x` and `c y z` they give `c (w + y) (x + z)` and `c (w * y) (x * z)`. Two congruences with the same relation are equal by `RingCon.ext`.

The quotient is the type `RingCon.Quotient c`, written `c.Quotient`. An element `a : R` gives its class `(a : c.Quotient)` through the coercion `RingCon.instCoeTCQuotient`, and `RingCon.toQuotient` is the same map as a function. The basic fact is `RingCon.eq`:

$$(a : R/c) = (b : R/c) \iff c\ a\ b.$$

This is the rule that turns an equation in the quotient into a statement about the relation. The module then gives the quotient every algebraic structure that $R$ has. For each class of the hierarchy there is an instance: `RingCon.instSemiringQuotient`, `RingCon.instCommSemiringQuotient`, `RingCon.instRingQuotient`, `RingCon.instCommRingQuotient`, and the non-unital and non-associative variants. The `simp` lemmas `RingCon.coe_add`, `RingCon.coe_mul`, `RingCon.coe_zero`, `RingCon.coe_one`, `RingCon.coe_neg`, `RingCon.coe_sub`, `RingCon.coe_pow` and `RingCon.coe_natCast` push the coercion through the operations, in the direction from `↑(x + y)` to `↑x + ↑y`. The ring homomorphism form of the projection is `RingCon.mk' c : R →+* c.Quotient`, and `RingCon.mk'_surjective` says that it is onto. The `simp` lemma `RingCon.coe_mk'` rewrites `c.mk'` to the coercion, so the simp normal form of a class is `↑a`, not `c.mk' a`.

The same module defines the congruence generated by a relation. For a relation $r$ on $R$, the inductive predicate `RingConGen.Rel r` has the constructors `RingConGen.Rel.of`, `RingConGen.Rel.refl`, `RingConGen.Rel.symm`, `RingConGen.Rel.trans`, `RingConGen.Rel.add` and `RingConGen.Rel.mul`. It is the least relation that contains $r$ and that is closed under these rules. The congruence `ringConGen r` packs this relation into a `RingCon R`. Pullback along a map that preserves both operations is `RingCon.comap`, and `RingCon.comap_rel` says that `J.comap f` relates $x$ and $y$ exactly when `J` relates $f(x)$ and $f(y)$.

#### The lattice of congruences

The module `Mathlib.RingTheory.Congruence.Basic` orders the congruences by inclusion of relations: `RingCon.le_def` says that $c \le d$ means that $c\ x\ y$ implies $d\ x\ y$. The instance `RingCon.instCompleteLattice` makes `RingCon R` a complete lattice. The least congruence is equality (`RingCon.coe_bot`) and the greatest relates all pairs (`RingCon.coe_top`). The infimum is the intersection of relations: `RingCon.inf_iff_and` says that $(c \sqcap d)\ x\ y$ holds exactly when $c\ x\ y$ and $d\ x\ y$, and `RingCon.coe_sInf` gives the same fact for a set of congruences. The supremum is not the union, because a union of two equivalence relations is not transitive in general. The library describes it through generation: `RingCon.sup_eq_ringConGen` says that $c \sqcup d$ is the congruence generated by the union, and `RingCon.sSup_eq_ringConGen` gives the version for a set.

The key fact about generation is `RingCon.ringConGen_le`:

$$\mathrm{ringConGen}(r) \le c \iff r \le c.$$

Proof sketch. If `ringConGen r ≤ c`, then $r \le c$ because $r$ is contained in the generated congruence (`RingCon.le_ringConGen`). Conversely, if $r \le c$, then induction on `RingConGen.Rel r x y` gives $c\ x\ y$: the constructor `of` uses $r \le c$, and the other constructors use that $c$ is a congruence. So `ringConGen` is left adjoint to the coercion to relations. The library packs this as the Galois insertion `RingCon.gi`. The lemmas `RingCon.ringConGen_idem`, `RingCon.ringConGen_of_ringCon` and `RingCon.ringConGen_mono` are consequences.

The module also treats the quotient as an algebra. If $R$ is an algebra over a commutative semiring $\alpha$, then `RingCon.instAlgebraQuotient` makes `c.Quotient` an $\alpha$-algebra, and `RingCon.mkₐ α c : R →ₐ[α] c.Quotient` is the projection as an algebra map. The scalar actions on the quotient come with `RingCon.instSMulQuotient` and the tower and commutation instances. Two lemmas detect the trivial cases: `RingCon.subsingleton_quotient` says that the quotient is a single point exactly when $c = \top$, and `RingCon.nontrivial_quotient` says that it is nontrivial exactly when $c \ne \top$.

#### Finite sums and products

The module `Mathlib.RingTheory.Congruence.BigOperators` extends the compatibility rules to finite families. The lemma `RingCon.finsetSum` says that if $c\ (f\ i)\ (g\ i)$ for each $i \in s$, then

$$c\ \Big(\sum_{i \in s} f(i)\Big)\ \Big(\sum_{i \in s} g(i)\Big).$$

The proof is an induction on the finset $s$ with `RingCon.add` in the step and `RingCon.refl` for the empty sum. The same pattern gives `RingCon.finsetProd`, `RingCon.listSum`, `RingCon.listProd`, `RingCon.multisetSum`, `RingCon.multisetProd`, `RingCon.finsuppSum`, `RingCon.finsuppProd`, `RingCon.dfinsuppSum` and `RingCon.dfinsuppProd`. The companion `coe_` lemmas push the coercion into the quotient through the big operator: `RingCon.coe_finsetSum` states $\overline{\sum_i f(i)} = \sum_i \overline{f(i)}$, and `RingCon.coe_listProd` and `RingCon.coe_multisetSum` are the other forms.

#### Homomorphisms and the isomorphism theorems

The module `Mathlib.RingTheory.Congruence.Hom` connects congruences with ring homomorphisms. The kernel of $f : M \to P$ is the congruence `RingCon.ker f`, and `RingCon.ker_apply` says that it relates $x$ and $y$ exactly when $f(x) = f(y)$. The kernel of the projection is the congruence itself: `RingCon.ker_mk'_eq`.

The universal property is `RingCon.lift`. For a ring homomorphism $f : M \to P$ and a proof of $c \le \ker f$, it gives a ring homomorphism $\bar f : M/c \to P$ with $\bar f(\bar x) = f(x)$, and this equation is `RingCon.lift_mk'`. The map is well defined because $c\ x\ y$ implies $f(x) = f(y)$. The lemma `RingCon.lift_unique` says that $\bar f$ is the only homomorphism with this property, and `RingCon.Quotient.hom_ext` says that two homomorphisms out of the quotient are equal when they agree after composition with the projection. The algebra version is `RingCon.liftₐ`, and `RingCon.liftₐEquiv` states the universal property as an equivalence between the algebra maps that kill $c$ and the algebra maps out of the quotient.

The first isomorphism theorem has several forms. The map `RingCon.kerLift f` is the lift of $f$ to the quotient by its kernel, and `RingCon.kerLift_injective` says that it is injective. Proof: if $\bar f(\bar x) = \bar f(\bar y)$, then $f(x) = f(y)$, so $x$ and $y$ are related by `RingCon.ker f`, and `RingCon.eq` gives $\bar x = \bar y$. The image of `RingCon.kerLift f` is the image of $f$ (`RingCon.kerLift_range_eq`). This gives three equivalences, by the kind of image:

| Lean name | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| `RingCon.quotientKerEquivRangeS` | $M/\ker f \cong f(M)$, with the image as a subsemiring |
| `RingCon.quotientKerEquivRange` | $M/\ker f \cong f(M)$, with the image as a subring, for rings |
| `RingCon.quotientKerEquivRangeₐ` | $M/\ker f \cong f(M)$ as $R$-algebras |
| `RingCon.quotientKerEquivOfSurjective` | $M/\ker f \cong P$ when $f$ is surjective |
| `RingCon.quotientKerEquivOfRightInverse` | $M/\ker f \cong P$ when $f$ has a right inverse |

For two congruences with $c \le d$ on the same ring, `RingCon.map c d h` is the induced homomorphism $M/c \to M/d$, and `RingCon.factorₐ` is its algebra form. The third isomorphism theorem is `RingCon.quotientQuotientEquivQuotient`: the quotient of $M/c$ by the kernel of this map is isomorphic to $M/d$. Its algebra form is `RingCon.quotientQuotientEquivQuotientₐ`. The correspondence theorem is the order isomorphism `RingCon.correspondence`: the congruences on $M/c$ correspond to the congruences on $M$ that are at least $c$. The proof sends a congruence $d \ge c$ to the relation $\bar x \sim \bar y \iff d\ x\ y$, which is well defined because $c \le d$. The inverse pulls a congruence on $M/c$ back along the projection.

A congruence can also move along a function. For $f : M \to N$, `RingCon.mapGen f` is the congruence on $N$ generated by the pairs $(f(x), f(y))$ with $c\ x\ y$. In general this relation is larger than the image of $c$. When $f$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with $\ker f \le c$, `RingCon.mapGen_eq_map_of_surjective` says that no generation is needed: the congruence relates $f(x)$ and $f(y)$ exactly when $c\ x\ y$. The equivalences `RingCon.comapQuotientEquivOfSurj` and `RingCon.congr` compare quotients of rings that are related by a surjection or by an isomorphism.

#### Opposite rings and star rings

The module `Mathlib.RingTheory.Congruence.Opposite` moves a congruence to the opposite ring. The map `RingCon.op` sends a congruence on $R$ to a congruence on $R^{\mathrm{mop}}$, and `RingCon.op_iff` says that `c.op x y` holds exactly when `c (unop y) (unop x)` holds. The order of the arguments changes, but this is harmless because a congruence is symmetric. Proof that `c.op` is a congruence: the addition is the same in both rings, and the product $x \cdot y$ in $R^{\mathrm{mop}}$ is $y x$ in $R$, so the multiplication rule of $c$ applies with the factors exchanged. The inverse map is `RingCon.unop`, and `RingCon.opOrderIso` packs the two maps as an order isomorphism. The two-sided ideals of Section 4 use this to pass between left and right.

The module `Mathlib.RingTheory.Congruence.Star` has two lemmas for a star ring. If a relation $r$ satisfies $r\ a\ b \Rightarrow r\ a^*\ b^*$, then `RingConGen.Rel.star` and `ringConGen_star` say that the generated congruence has the same property. The proof is an induction on `RingConGen.Rel`. The star reverses products, $(x y)^* = y^* x^*$, but the multiplication rule of a congruence holds for factors in either order, so the step for `mul` works.

#### Common errors

1. A learner uses `RingCon` for a commutative ring where `Ideal R` is the right tool. The quotient `c.Quotient` and the quotient `R ⧸ I` are different types, and the lemmas of Section 7 do not apply to `c.Quotient`. For a commutative ring, take the quotient by an ideal. For a two-sided ideal of a noncommutative ring, use `TwoSidedIdeal`, which stores a `RingCon` but has its own API.
2. A learner tries to prove `↑a = ↑b` in `c.Quotient` with `rfl` or `simp`. The two sides are classes in a quotient type, and their equality is not a definitional equality of the representatives. The step is `(RingCon.eq c).mpr h`, with a proof `h : c a b`, or `rw [RingCon.eq]` to turn the goal into the relation.
3. A learner reads `c ⊔ d` as the union of the two relations. The union is in general not transitive. The supremum is `ringConGen` of the union, by `RingCon.sup_eq_ringConGen`, and a proof about it needs an induction on `RingConGen.Rel`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Prove that `RingCon.ker f = ⊥` holds exactly when the ring homomorphism $f$ is injective, with `RingCon.ker_apply` and `RingCon.coe_bot`.
2. (A) Use `RingCon.finsetSum` and `RingCon.finsetProd` to show that if $c\ a\ b$, then $c\ (a^n + a)\ (b^n + b)$ for each $n$.
3. (B) On the semiring $\mathbb{N}$, define the congruence that relates $m$ and $n$ when both are at least $2$ or when $m = n$, and show that its quotient has three elements. Show that the class of $0$ is $\{0\}$, as for equality, so the class of $0$ does not determine a congruence of a semiring.
4. (B) Prove `RingCon.ringConGen_le` from the definition of `RingConGen.Rel` by induction, without `RingCon.gi`.
5. (B) For a relation $r$ on $R$, prove that `(ringConGen r).Quotient` has the universal property: a ring homomorphism $f : R \to P$ with $f(x) = f(y)$ for each pair with $r\ x\ y$ factors uniquely through the quotient.
6. (C) Let $R$ be a ring with negation. Show that $c \mapsto \{x \mid c\ x\ 0\}$ is an order isomorphism from `RingCon R` to the two-sided ideals of $R$, and compare the result with `TwoSidedIdeal.orderIsoRingCon`.
