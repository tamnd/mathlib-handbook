## Derivations, envelopes and free Lie algebras

### Overview

A derivation of a Lie algebra $L$ is a linear map $D$ with $D[a, b] = [Da, b] + [a, Db]$. The Jacobi identity says that each $\operatorname{ad} x$ is a derivation, and the derivations of $L$ form a Lie algebra $\operatorname{Der}(L)$ in which the inner derivations $\operatorname{ad}(L)$ are an ideal. Derivations give the semidirect products, the simplest extensions of Lie algebras, and a nilpotent derivation in characteristic $0$ exponentiates to an automorphism.

The commutator turns each associative algebra into a Lie algebra. The universal enveloping algebra $U(L)$ goes the other way: it is the associative algebra that is freest among those that receive a Lie morphism from $L$. A representation of $L$ is the same as a module over $U(L)$. The free Lie algebra on a set $X$ has the corresponding universal property for maps of sets, and its enveloping algebra is the free associative algebra on $X$. The section ends with tensor products of modules and with base change.

In the whole section $R$ is a commutative ring, $L$ and $K$ are Lie algebras over $R$, and $M$ and $N$ are Lie modules over $L$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Derivation).** A derivation of $L$ into $M$ is an $R$-linear map $D : L \to M$ with
$$D[a, b] = [a, D b] - [b, D a] \quad \text{for all } a, b \in L.$$
For $M = L$ this is $D[a, b] = [D a, b] + [a, D b]$. The derivations of $L$ into itself form the module $\operatorname{Der}(L)$, with the bracket $[D_1, D_2] = D_1 D_2 - D_2 D_1$.

**Definition (Inner derivation).** For $x \in L$, the inner derivation $\operatorname{ad} x$ is $y \mapsto [x, y]$. The map $\operatorname{ad} : L \to \operatorname{Der}(L)$ is the adjoint map with values in the derivations. A derivation that is not of the form $\operatorname{ad} x$ is an outer derivation.

**Definition (Universal enveloping algebra).** Let $T(L)$ be the tensor algebra of the module $L$, with $\iota_T : L \to T(L)$. The universal enveloping algebra is the quotient
$$U(L) = T(L) / \big( \iota_T(x)\iota_T(y) - \iota_T(y)\iota_T(x) - \iota_T([x, y]) \big),$$
the quotient by the two-sided ideal generated by these elements for all $x, y \in L$. The map $\iota : L \to U(L)$ is the composite of $\iota_T$ and the quotient map.

**Definition (Free Lie algebra).** For a set $X$, the free Lie algebra $F(X)$ over $R$ is a Lie algebra with a map $\mathrm{of} : X \to F(X)$ such that each map $g : X \to L$ to a Lie algebra extends to a unique morphism $\tilde g : F(X) \to L$ with $\tilde g \circ \mathrm{of} = g$. It is the quotient of the free non-associative algebra on $X$ by the relations of a Lie algebra.

**Definition (Tensor product of modules).** The tensor product $M \otimes_R N$ is a Lie module over $L$ with
$$[x, m \otimes n] = [x, m] \otimes n + m \otimes [x, n].$$

**Definition (Base change).** For a commutative $R$-algebra $A$, the module $A \otimes_R L$ is a Lie algebra over $A$ with $[s \otimes x, t \otimes y] = st \otimes [x, y]$.

**Definition (Semidirect product).** Let $\psi : L \to \operatorname{Der}(K)$ be a morphism. The semidirect product $K \rtimes_\psi L$ is the module $K \times L$ with the bracket
$$[(k_1, l_1), (k_2, l_2)] = \big([k_1, k_2] + \psi(l_1) k_2 - \psi(l_2) k_1, \ [l_1, l_2]\big).$$

**Definition (Extension).** An extension of $L$ by $K$ is a Lie algebra $E$ with morphisms $i : K \to E$ and $p : E \to L$ such that $i$ is injective, $p$ is surjective and $\ker p = i(K)$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Proposition (Derivations form a Lie algebra).** If $D_1, D_2$ are derivations of $L$, then $[D_1, D_2] = D_1 D_2 - D_2 D_1$ is a derivation. So $\operatorname{Der}(L)$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(L)$.

*Proof.* Apply $D_1$ to $D_2[a, b] = [D_2 a, b] + [a, D_2 b]$:
$$D_1 D_2 [a, b] = [D_1 D_2 a, b] + [D_2 a, D_1 b] + [D_1 a, D_2 b] + [a, D_1 D_2 b].$$
Exchange $D_1$ and $D_2$ and subtract. The two middle terms are symmetric in $D_1, D_2$, so they cancel, and what remains is $[D_1, D_2][a, b] = [[D_1, D_2] a, b] + [a, [D_1, D_2] b]$. $\square$

**Theorem (Inner derivations form an ideal).** Each $\operatorname{ad} x$ is a derivation, and $\operatorname{ad} : L \to \operatorname{Der}(L)$ is a morphism with kernel the center of $L$. For each derivation $D$ and $x \in L$,
$$[D, \operatorname{ad} x] = \operatorname{ad}(D x).$$
So $\operatorname{ad}(L)$ is an ideal of $\operatorname{Der}(L)$.

*Proof.* The Leibniz rule $[x, [a, b]] = [[x, a], b] + [a, [x, b]]$ says that $\operatorname{ad} x$ is a derivation. The map $\operatorname{ad}$ is a morphism by the previous section, and $\operatorname{ad} x = 0$ if and only if $[x, y] = 0$ for all $y$, so its kernel is the center. For $y \in L$,
$$[D, \operatorname{ad} x](y) = D[x, y] - [x, D y] = [D x, y] + [x, D y] - [x, D y] = [D x, y].$$
So $[D, \operatorname{ad} x] = \operatorname{ad}(D x)$ lies in $\operatorname{ad}(L)$, and $\operatorname{ad}(L)$ is an ideal. $\square$

**Proposition (Powers of a derivation).** For a derivation $D$ of $L$ and $n \geq 0$,
$$D^n [a, b] = \sum_{i + j = n} \binom{n}{i} [D^i a, D^j b].$$

*Proof.* Induction on $n$. The case $n = 0$ is clear. Apply $D$ to the formula for $n$; each term $[D^i a, D^j b]$ gives $[D^{i+1} a, D^j b] + [D^i a, D^{j+1} b]$. Collect the terms with $i + j = n + 1$ and use $\binom{n}{i - 1} + \binom{n}{i} = \binom{n+1}{i}$. $\square$

**Theorem (The exponential of a nilpotent derivation).** Suppose that $L$ is a module over $\mathbb{Q}$ and that $D$ is a nilpotent derivation of $L$. Then $\exp D = \sum_{n \geq 0} D^n / n!$, a finite sum, is an automorphism of $L$.

*Proof.* By the previous proposition,
$$\exp(D)[a, b] = \sum_{n} \sum_{i + j = n} \frac{[D^i a, D^j b]}{i! \, j!} = [\exp(D) a, \exp(D) b],$$
and the sums are finite because $D^N = 0$ for some $N$. The derivation $-D$ is also nilpotent and commutes with $D$, so $\exp(D) \exp(-D) = \exp(0) = 1$, and $\exp D$ is a bijection. $\square$

**Theorem (Universal property of the enveloping algebra).** The map $\iota : L \to U(L)$ is a morphism of Lie algebras for the commutator bracket on $U(L)$. For each associative $R$-algebra $A$ and each morphism $f : L \to A$ of Lie algebras, there is a unique algebra morphism $\tilde f : U(L) \to A$ with $\tilde f \circ \iota = f$.

*Proof.* In $U(L)$ the relation $\iota(x)\iota(y) - \iota(y)\iota(x) = \iota([x, y])$ holds by construction, so $\iota$ is a morphism. Let $f : L \to A$ be a morphism. The universal property of the tensor algebra gives an algebra morphism $F : T(L) \to A$ with $F \circ \iota_T = f$. It sends each generator of the ideal to
$$f(x) f(y) - f(y) f(x) - f([x, y]) = 0,$$
so it vanishes on the ideal and induces $\tilde f$ on $U(L)$. The algebra $U(L)$ is generated by $\iota(L)$, so two algebra morphisms that agree on $\iota(L)$ are equal, and $\tilde f$ is unique. $\square$

**Corollary (Representations and modules over the envelope).** A Lie module structure on an $R$-module $M$ is the same as a $U(L)$-module structure on $M$.

*Proof.* A Lie module structure is a morphism $L \to \operatorname{End}_R(M)$ of Lie algebras, by the previous section. By the theorem, these are in bijection with algebra morphisms $U(L) \to \operatorname{End}_R(M)$, which are the $U(L)$-module structures. $\square$

**Theorem (The envelope of a free Lie algebra).** The algebra $U(F(X))$ is isomorphic to the free associative algebra $R\langle X \rangle$, by an isomorphism that sends $\iota(\mathrm{of}(x))$ to $x$.

*Proof.* For each associative algebra $A$ there are natural bijections
$$\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Alg}}(U(F(X)), A) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Lie}}(F(X), A) \cong \operatorname{Map}(X, A) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Alg}}(R\langle X \rangle, A),$$
by the universal properties of $U$, of $F(X)$ and of $R\langle X \rangle$. Two objects that represent the same functor are isomorphic, and the isomorphism matches the elements that correspond to the identity maps. $\square$

**Proposition (The tensor product is a Lie module).** The bracket on $M \otimes N$ satisfies $[[x, y], t] = [x, [y, t]] - [y, [x, t]]$.

*Proof.* Take $t = m \otimes n$ by additivity. Then
$$[x, [y, m \otimes n]] = [x, [y, m]] \otimes n + [y, m] \otimes [x, n] + [x, m] \otimes [y, n] + m \otimes [x, [y, n]].$$
Exchange $x$ and $y$ and subtract. The two middle terms cancel against their images, and the module rule in $M$ and in $N$ gives $[[x, y], m] \otimes n + m \otimes [[x, y], n] = [[x, y], m \otimes n]$. $\square$

**Proposition (Semidirect products).** The bracket on $K \rtimes_\psi L$ makes it a Lie algebra. The inclusions $k \mapsto (k, 0)$ and $l \mapsto (0, l)$ are morphisms, $K \times 0$ is an ideal, and the projection to $L$ is a surjective morphism with kernel $K \times 0$. So $K \rtimes_\psi L$ is an extension of $L$ by $K$ with a section.

*Proof.* In the Jacobi identity for three elements, sort the terms by the number of entries from $L$. The terms with only entries from $K$ give the Jacobi identity of $K$. The terms with one entry $l$ from $L$ give the derivation rule for $\psi(l)$. The terms with two entries $l_1, l_2$ give $\psi([l_1, l_2]) = [\psi(l_1), \psi(l_2)]$, which is the morphism property of $\psi$. The terms with only entries from $L$ give the Jacobi identity of $L$. The formula for the bracket shows directly that the two inclusions and the projection preserve it, and the kernel of the projection is $K \times 0$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (All derivations are inner).** Let $L$ have basis $x, y$ with $[x, y] = y$. A linear map $D$ with $D x = a x + b y$ and $D y = c x + d y$ is a derivation if and only if $D[x, y] = [D x, y] + [x, D y]$, that is, $c x + d y = a y + d y$. So $a = c = 0$, and $\operatorname{Der}(L)$ is two-dimensional, with $D x = b y$, $D y = d y$. Here $\operatorname{ad} x$ has $b = 0, d = 1$, and $\operatorname{ad} y$ has $b = -1, d = 0$. So $\operatorname{Der}(L) = \operatorname{ad}(L)$, and $\operatorname{ad}$ is injective because the center is $0$. The next chapter proves this for semisimple algebras in characteristic $0$.

**Counterexample (Outer derivations of an abelian algebra).** If $L = R^n$ is abelian, then each linear map is a derivation, because both sides of the derivation rule are $0$. So $\operatorname{Der}(L) = \mathfrak{gl}_n(R)$, while $\operatorname{ad}(L) = 0$. Each nonzero derivation is outer.

**Example (The Heisenberg algebra).** Let $\mathfrak{h}$ have basis $x, y, z$ with $[x, y] = z$ and $z$ central. Take $K = R y + R z$, an abelian algebra, and $L = R x$, with $\psi(x)$ the derivation $y \mapsto z$, $z \mapsto 0$. In $K \rtimes_\psi L$, the bracket of $(0, x)$ and $(y, 0)$ is $(\psi(x) y, 0) = (z, 0)$, so $\mathfrak{h} \cong K \rtimes_\psi L$. It is also an extension of the abelian algebra $R x + R y$ by the central line $R z$, and this extension has no section that is a morphism, because the image of a section would be abelian and could not contain lifts of both $x$ and $y$.

**Example (The envelope of an abelian algebra).** If $L$ is free with basis $x_1, \dots, x_n$ and abelian, then $U(L)$ is the polynomial ring $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. A morphism of Lie algebras $L \to A$ is a linear map whose images commute pairwise, and these are in bijection with the algebra morphisms $R[x_1, \dots, x_n] \to A$. So the polynomial ring has the universal property of $U(L)$.

**Example (The free Lie algebra on one generator).** For $X = \{x\}$, the free Lie algebra is the free module $R \cdot \mathrm{of}(x)$ with zero bracket. The line $R \cdot \mathrm{of}(x)$ is a subalgebra, since $[\mathrm{of}(x), \mathrm{of}(x)] = 0$, and it contains the image of $X$, so it is all of $F(X)$ by the uniqueness in the universal property. The morphism to the abelian algebra $R$ with $x \mapsto 1$ shows that $r \cdot \mathrm{of}(x) \neq 0$ for $r \neq 0$.

Common errors of a learner: The first error is to apply a `LieDerivation` with the textbook Leibniz rule directly; the field `leibniz'` has the form $[a, D b] - [b, D a]$, and for `M = L` the lemma `apply_lie_eq_add` gives the usual form. The second error is to confuse `LieDerivation.inner` with `LieDerivation.ad`; for `M = L` the first sends `y` to `x ↦ ⁅x, y⁆`, which is $-\operatorname{ad} y$. The third error is to expect `ι` into `UniversalEnvelopingAlgebra R L` to be injective by a lemma; over a field it is, but the proof needs the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem, which the library does not have.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that the set of derivations $D$ of $L$ with $D(L) \subseteq Z(L)$ and $D([L, L]) = 0$ is an ideal of $\operatorname{Der}(L)$. Hint: compute $[D', D](y)$ for an arbitrary derivation $D'$.
2. (A) Let $D$ be a derivation of $L$ and $I$ an ideal. Show that $D(I)$ need not be contained in $I$, and that $D([L, L]) \subseteq [L, L]$. Hint: for the first claim use an abelian $L$; for the second apply the derivation rule to a bracket.
3. (A) Compute $\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{h})$ for the Heisenberg algebra over a field and find its dimension. Hint: a derivation is fixed by $D x$ and $D y$, and $D z = [D x, y] + [x, D y]$; check which conditions $D z$ must satisfy.
4. (B) Show that $\exp(\operatorname{ad} e)$ on $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{Q})$ sends $f$ to $f + h - e$ and $h$ to $h - 2e$. Hint: $(\operatorname{ad} e)^3 = 0$; compute $[e, f] = h$ and $[e, h] = -2e$.
5. (B) Show that a morphism $f : L \to L'$ induces an algebra morphism $U(f) : U(L) \to U(L')$, and that $U(f \circ g) = U(f) \circ U(g)$. Hint: apply the universal property to $\iota' \circ f$.
6. (C) Show that $U(L_1 \times L_2) \cong U(L_1) \otimes_R U(L_2)$. Hint: the images of $L_1$ and $L_2$ in the tensor product commute; use the universal property in both directions.

### Notes and history

The theorem that all derivations of a semisimple Lie algebra are inner is a classical consequence of the Killing form. The universal enveloping algebra and the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem, which gives a basis of ordered monomials, are named after Poincaré, Birkhoff and Witt. Free Lie algebras and their relation to free associative algebras were studied by Witt.

The Mathlib files are by these authors, read from their headers: `UniversalEnveloping`, `Free`, `TensorProduct` and `BaseChange` (all under `Mathlib.Algebra.Lie`) by Oliver Nash; `Derivation.Basic` and `AdjointAction.Derivation` by Frédéric Marbach; `Derivation.BaseChange` and `SemiDirect` by Leonid Ryvkin; `AdjointAction.Basic` by Janos Wolosz; and `Extension` by Scott Carnahan.
