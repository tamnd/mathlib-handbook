## Computing Galois groups

### Overview

Section 1 gave two general tools: the Galois group of an irreducible separable polynomial is a transitive group of permutations of its roots, and the discriminant tells whether it lies in the alternating group. This section adds two more and applies them. The first is the fundamental theorem used in reverse: known subfields of the splitting field give quotients and subgroups of the group, and the degree of the splitting field gives its order. The second is complex conjugation: for a polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$, it acts on the complex roots and gives an element of order $1$ or $2$ in the group.

The section computes the group of $X^4 - 2$, the dihedral group of order $8$, with its full lattice of subfields. It proves that an irreducible polynomial of prime degree $p$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ with exactly two nonreal roots has the group $S_p$, and applies this to $X^5 - 4X + 2$. It ends with abelian extensions and a theorem of Isaacs on embeddings.

The section uses Section 1 and the fundamental theorem (Chapter 5), and from Volume 5 the symmetric groups and the theorem of Cauchy on elements of prime order (Chapters 7 and 8).

### Definitions

**Definition (Abelian extension).** A Galois extension $L/K$ is abelian if $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is abelian, and cyclic if $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is cyclic.

By the fundamental theorem, each subextension $M/K$ of an abelian extension is Galois, since each subgroup of an abelian group is normal, and $\operatorname{Gal}(M/K)$ is a quotient of an abelian group, so it is abelian. The extension $L/M$ is abelian too, with a subgroup as its group. The compositum of two abelian extensions is abelian, since its group embeds into the product of their groups (Section 1, theorem on factors).

### Theorems and proofs

**Lemma (A $p$-cycle and a transposition).** Let $p$ be prime. A $p$-cycle and a transposition generate $S_p$.

*Proof.* Let $\sigma$ be a $p$-cycle and $\tau = (a\ b)$ a transposition. Since $\sigma$ is a single cycle through all $p$ points, there is $k$ with $\sigma^k(a) = b$, and $0 < k < p$. Since $p$ is prime, $\sigma^k$ is again a $p$-cycle, and it generates the same subgroup as $\sigma$. Number the points so that $\sigma^k = (1\ 2\ \cdots\ p)$ and $\tau = (1\ 2)$. Then $\sigma^{ki} \tau \sigma^{-ki} = (i+1\ \ i+2)$ for $0 \leq i \leq p - 2$. These are the transpositions of neighbouring points, and they generate $S_p$ (Volume 5, Chapter 7), since each transposition is a product of them and each permutation is a product of transpositions. $\square$

**Theorem (Prime degree with two nonreal roots).** Let $f \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$ be irreducible of prime degree $p$, with exactly two roots in $\mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$. Then $\operatorname{Gal}(f) \cong S_p$.

*Proof.* Let $L \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ be the splitting field, generated by the $p$ distinct complex roots; they are distinct since $f$ is irreducible in characteristic $0$ (Chapter 4). View $G = \operatorname{Gal}(f)$ as a subgroup of $S_p$ through its action on the roots. By Section 1, $p$ divides $|G|$. By Cauchy's theorem (Volume 5, Chapter 8), $G$ has an element of order $p$. An element of order $p$ in $S_p$ is a product of disjoint cycles whose lengths have least common multiple $p$, so it is a $p$-cycle.

Complex conjugation maps $L$ into itself, since it permutes the roots of $f$, which has real coefficients. So it restricts to an element $c \in G$. It fixes the $p - 2$ real roots and swaps the two nonreal roots, which are conjugate to each other. So $c$ is a transposition. By the lemma, $G = S_p$. $\square$

**Theorem (Isaacs).** Let $E/F$ be algebraic and $K$ an extension of $F$. If the minimal polynomial of each element of $E$ has a root in $K$, then there is an $F$-embedding $E \to K$.

*Proof for a finite separable $E/F$.* By the primitive element theorem (Chapter 4, Section 3), $E = F(\gamma)$. The minimal polynomial $m$ of $\gamma$ has a root $\beta \in K$, and $\gamma \mapsto \beta$ defines an $F$-embedding $E \cong F[X]/(m) \to K$ (Chapter 2). $\square$

The general theorem needs a reduction to finite subextensions, and in characteristic $p$ a separate treatment of inseparable elements; this section does not give it. A consequence: an algebraic extension $E$ of $F$ in which each irreducible polynomial over $F$ has a root is an algebraic closure of $F$.

::: mathlib

If two algebraic extensions have the same set of minimal polynomials, they are isomorphic, `Field.nonempty_algEquiv_of_range_minpoly_eq`. The consequence above is `IsAlgClosure.of_exists_root`.

### Examples

**Example (The group of $X^4 - 2$).** Let $a = \sqrt[4]{2} > 0$. The roots of $X^4 - 2$ are $a, ia, -a, -ia$, so the splitting field is $L = \mathbb{Q}(a, i)$. The polynomial is irreducible by Eisenstein at $2$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(a):\mathbb{Q}] = 4$, and $i \notin \mathbb{Q}(a) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, so $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = 8$. An automorphism sends $a$ to one of four roots and $i$ to $\pm i$, and there are $8$ automorphisms, so each of the $8$ choices occurs. Let $r$ send $a \mapsto ia$, $i \mapsto i$, and $s$ be complex conjugation, $a \mapsto a$, $i \mapsto -i$. Then $r$ has order $4$, $s$ has order $2$, and $srs = r^{-1}$: both sides send $a$ to $-ia$ and fix $i$. So $G \cong D_4$, the symmetry group of a square whose corners are the four roots.

The ten subgroups of $D_4$ and their fixed fields are as follows. Order $8$: $\mathbb{Q}$. Order $4$: $\langle r \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(i)$, $\langle r^2, s \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$, $\langle r^2, rs \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(i\sqrt 2)$. Order $2$: $\langle r^2 \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, i)$, $\langle s \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(a)$, $\langle r^2 s \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}(ia)$, $\langle rs \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}((1+i)a)$, $\langle r^3 s \rangle \leftrightarrow \mathbb{Q}((1-i)a)$. Order $1$: $L$. To check one line: $rs(a) = r(a) = ia$ and $rs(i) = r(-i) = -i$, so $rs(ia) = (-i)(ia) = a$ and $rs$ fixes $a + ia$. The element $(1+i)a$ has degree $4$, since $((1+i)a)^4 = (1+i)^4 \cdot 2 = -8$, and $X^4 + 8$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. The normal subgroups are $\langle r^2 \rangle$ and the three of order $4$, so the Galois subextensions are $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, i)$ and the three quadratic fields. The four fields of degree $4$ besides $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, i)$ are not Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Example (A quintic with the group $S_5$).** Let $f = X^5 - 4X + 2$. It is irreducible by Eisenstein at $2$. The values $f(-2) = -22$, $f(0) = 2$, $f(1) = -1$, $f(2) = 26$ change sign three times, so $f$ has at least three real roots. Its derivative $5X^4 - 4$ has only two real zeros, so by Rolle's theorem $f$ has at most three real roots. So $f$ has exactly three real roots and two nonreal ones, and $\operatorname{Gal}(f) \cong S_5$, of order $120$. Chapter 8 deduces that the roots of $f$ cannot be written with radicals.

**Example (The real roots are needed).** The polynomial $g = X^5 - X - 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, and its group is $S_5$, but it has one real root and four nonreal roots, so the theorem does not apply to it. Its group needs another argument, such as reduction modulo primes. The irreducible polynomial $\Phi_5 = X^4 + X^3 + X^2 + X + 1$ also has no real root, and its group is cyclic of order $4$ (Chapter 8), not $S_4$. So without the condition on the real roots, complex conjugation may be a product of two transpositions, and the conclusion fails. The condition that the degree is prime is needed too: $X^4 - 2$ has exactly two nonreal roots, but its group $D_4$ is not $S_4$.

### The library around it

The file `Mathlib.FieldTheory.Galois.Abelian` defines abelian extensions. The file `Mathlib.FieldTheory.Isaacs` holds the theorem on embeddings. The two files on differential fields begin a differential Galois theory. `Mathlib.FieldTheory.Differential.Basic` defines the logarithmic derivative `Differential.logDeriv` $a \mapsto a'/a$ and extends a derivation to finite extensions, and `Mathlib.FieldTheory.Differential.Liouville` defines Liouville extensions, the setting of the theorem of Liouville on elementary integrals.

::: mathlib
`IsLiouville` · `Mathlib.FieldTheory.Differential.Liouville`

The class `IsLiouville F K` for differential fields says: if $a \in F$ can be written as $v + \sum c_i\, u_i'/u_i$ with $u_i, v \in K$ and constants $c_i$, then it can be written so with $u_i, v \in F$. Liouville's theorem says that each finite extension is of this kind in characteristic $0$, and it is the reason why $\int e^{-x^2}\,dx$ is not an elementary function.

### Exercises

1. (A) Show that $\operatorname{Gal}(X^4 - 5X^2 + 6)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is the Klein four group.
2. (A) Show that $X^5 - 6X + 3$ has the group $S_5$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.
3. (A) Find the group of $X^4 + 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. Hint: its roots are the primitive $8$th roots of unity, and $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8) = \mathbb{Q}(i, \sqrt 2)$.
4. (B) Show that a transitive subgroup of $S_p$, $p$ prime, that contains a transposition is $S_p$.
5. (B) For each prime $p \geq 5$, find an irreducible polynomial of degree $p$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ with exactly two nonreal roots. Hint: let $g = (X^2 + 2)(X - 2)(X - 4)\cdots(X - 2(p-2))$, take $N g + 2$ for a large odd integer $N$, and use Eisenstein at $2$.
6. (B) Find the group of $X^4 - 2$ over $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ and over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$, with the fundamental theorem and the lattice above.
7. (C) Let $f$ be an irreducible quartic over $\mathbb{Q}$ whose group is $D_4$. Show that the splitting field contains exactly three quadratic fields, and that exactly one of them is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta(f)})$.

### Notes and history

The criterion with a $p$-cycle and a transposition is classical and appears in many textbooks of the twentieth century. The theorem on embeddings appears in a paper of I. M. Isaacs in the American Mathematical Monthly. Liouville proved in the 1830s that some integrals are not elementary; the algebraic form with differential fields comes from the twentieth century.
