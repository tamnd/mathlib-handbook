## Operations on ideals

### Overview

Volume 6, Chapter 2 defined the sum, the intersection and the product of two ideals. This section shows that the ideals of a ring form a complete lattice and, with the product, a commutative semiring. It adds the ideal quotient $(I : J)$, extension and contraction along a ring homomorphism, and coprime ideals. Rings are commutative with $1$.

### Definitions

**Definition (Sum of a family).** The sum $\sum_\lambda I_\lambda$ of a family of ideals of $R$ is the set of the finite sums $x_1 + \dots + x_n$ with each $x_k$ in some $I_{\lambda_k}$.

**Definition (Ideal quotient).** For ideals $I, J$ of $R$, the ideal quotient is
$$(I : J) = \{ r \in R : r J \subseteq I \}.$$
The ideal $(0 : J)$ is the annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(J)$.

**Definition (Extension and contraction).** Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism. The extension $I^e$ of an ideal $I$ of $R$ is the ideal of $S$ generated by $f(I)$: the set of the sums $\sum_k s_k f(a_k)$ with $s_k \in S$, $a_k \in I$. The contraction $J^c$ of an ideal $J$ of $S$ is $f^{-1}(J)$, an ideal by Volume 6, Chapter 2.

**Definition (Coprime ideals).** Ideals $I$ and $J$ are coprime if $I + J = R$, that is, $1 = a + b$ with $a \in I$, $b \in J$.

### Theorems and proofs

**Theorem (The lattice of ideals).** The ideals of $R$, ordered by inclusion, form a complete lattice. The greatest lower bound of a family is its intersection and the least upper bound is its sum.

*Proof.* An intersection of ideals is an ideal, and it contains each ideal that lies in all $I_\lambda$. The sum $T$ contains $0$ and is closed under differences and under $r (x_1 + \dots + x_n) = r x_1 + \dots + r x_n$. So $T$ is an ideal that contains each $I_\lambda$, and each such ideal contains $T$. $\square$

**Theorem (Rules for products).** Let $I, J, K$ be ideals of $R$.

1. If $I \subseteq I'$ and $J \subseteq J'$, then $I J \subseteq I' J'$.
2. $I (J + K) = I J + I K$.
3. (Modular law) If $J \subseteq I$, then $I \cap (J + K) = J + (I \cap K)$.

*Proof.* (1) Each generator $a b$ of $I J$ lies in $I' J'$.

(2) $I (J + K)$ is generated by the products $a (b + c) = a b + a c$, which lie in $I J + I K$. Conversely $I J$ and $I K$ lie in $I(J + K)$ by (1).

(3) The ideals $J$ and $I \cap K$ lie in $I$ and in $J + K$, which gives $\supseteq$. Conversely let $x \in I$ with $x = b + c$, $b \in J$, $c \in K$. Then $c = x - b \in I$, since $b \in J \subseteq I$. So $c \in I \cap K$ and $x \in J + (I \cap K)$. $\square$

The full distributive law fails in general (Exercise 5).

**Theorem (Ideal quotients).** Let $I, J, K$ and $I_\lambda, J_\lambda$ be ideals of $R$.

1. $(I : J)$ is an ideal, $I \subseteq (I : J)$, and $(I : J) J \subseteq I$.
2. $(I : J) = R$ if and only if $J \subseteq I$.
3. $((I : J) : K) = (I : J K)$.
4. $(\bigcap_\lambda I_\lambda : J) = \bigcap_\lambda (I_\lambda : J)$ and $(I : \sum_\lambda J_\lambda) = \bigcap_\lambda (I : J_\lambda)$.

*Proof.* (1) If $r J, r' J \subseteq I$, then $(r - r') b \in I$ and $(t r) b = t (r b) \in I$ for $b \in J$, $t \in R$. For $a \in I$, $a J \subseteq I$. Each generator $r b$ of $(I : J) J$ lies in $I$.

(2) $1 \in (I : J)$ if and only if $J \subseteq I$.

(3) $r \in ((I : J) : K)$ means $r c b \in I$ for all $c \in K$ and $b \in J$. Since $J K$ is generated by the products $b c$, this says $r (J K) \subseteq I$.

(4) $r J \subseteq \bigcap_\lambda I_\lambda$ if and only if $r J \subseteq I_\lambda$ for each $\lambda$. And $r (b_1 + \dots + b_n) = r b_1 + \dots + r b_n$, so $r$ multiplies the sum into $I$ if and only if it multiplies each $J_\lambda$ into $I$. $\square$

**Theorem (Galois connection).** Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism, $I$ an ideal of $R$ and $J$ an ideal of $S$. Then $I^e \subseteq J$ if and only if $I \subseteq J^c$. Consequently:

1. $I \subseteq I^{ec}$ and $J^{ce} \subseteq J$;
2. $I^{ece} = I^e$ and $J^{cec} = J^c$;
3. $I \mapsto I^e$ is a bijection from the contracted ideals of $R$ onto the extended ideals of $S$, with inverse $J \mapsto J^c$.

*Proof.* If $I^e \subseteq J$ and $x \in I$, then $f(x) \in J$, so $x \in J^c$. If $I \subseteq J^c$, then $f(I) \subseteq J$, so the ideal generated by $f(I)$ lies in $J$.

(1) Apply the equivalence to $J = I^e$ and to $I = J^c$.

(2) Both operations preserve inclusion. So $I \subseteq I^{ec}$ gives $I^e \subseteq I^{ece}$, and (1) for $I^e$ gives $I^{ece} \subseteq I^e$. Dually $J^c \subseteq J^{cec}$ by (1), and $J^{ce} \subseteq J$ gives $J^{cec} \subseteq J^c$.

(3) If $I = J^c$, then $I^{ec} = J^{cec} = I$ by (2). If $K = I^e$, then $K^{ce} = I^{ece} = K$. $\square$

**Theorem (Extension and contraction of operations).** Let $f : R \to S$ be a ring homomorphism. For ideals $I_1, I_2$ of $R$ and $J_1, J_2$ of $S$:

1. $(I_1 + I_2)^e = I_1^e + I_2^e$ and $(I_1 I_2)^e = I_1^e I_2^e$;
2. $(J_1 \cap J_2)^c = J_1^c \cap J_2^c$;
3. if $J_1$ is prime, then $J_1^c$ is prime.

*Proof.* (1) Both sides of the first equation are the least ideal of $S$ that contains $f(I_1) \cup f(I_2)$. The generators $f(\sum_k a_k b_k) = \sum_k f(a_k) f(b_k)$ of $(I_1 I_2)^e$ lie in $I_1^e I_2^e$. Conversely $I_1^e I_2^e$ is generated by products $u v$ with $u = \sum_i s_i f(a_i)$ and $v = \sum_j t_j f(b_j)$, and $u v = \sum_{i, j} s_i t_j f(a_i b_j) \in (I_1 I_2)^e$.

(2) $f(x) \in J_1 \cap J_2$ if and only if $f(x) \in J_1$ and $f(x) \in J_2$.

(3) See Volume 6, Chapter 2, Section 3. $\square$

**Theorem (Surjective homomorphisms).** Let $f : R \to S$ be surjective. Then $J^{ce} = J$ for each ideal $J$ of $S$, and $I^{ec} = I + \ker f$ for each ideal $I$ of $R$. If $P$ is a prime ideal with $\ker f \subseteq P$, then $P^e = f(P)$ is prime.

*Proof.* Since $f$ is surjective, $I^e = f(I)$ and $f(f^{-1}(J)) = J$. Next, $x \in I^{ec}$ means $f(x) = f(a)$ for some $a \in I$, that is, $x - a \in \ker f$. So $I^{ec} = I + \ker f$, and $P^{ec} = P$. If $f(x) f(y) \in f(P)$, then $x y \in P^{ec} = P$, so $x \in P$ or $y \in P$. And $f(P) \ne S$, since otherwise $1 \in P^{ec} = P$. $\square$

**Theorem (Coprime ideals).** Let $I, J, K$ be ideals of $R$.

1. If $I + J = R$ and $I + K = R$, then $I + J K = R$.
2. If $I + J = R$, then $I^m + J^n = R$ for all $m, n \ge 1$.

*Proof.* (1) Write $1 = a + b$ and $1 = a' + c$ with $a, a' \in I$, $b \in J$, $c \in K$. Then
$$1 = (a + b)(a' + c) = (a a' + a c + b a') + b c,$$
where the first three terms lie in $I$ and $b c \in J K$.

(2) Part (1) with $K = J$ and induction on $n$ give $I + J^n = R$. The same argument for the coprime pair $J^n, I$ gives $J^n + I^m = R$. $\square$

### Examples

**Example (Ideal quotients in $\mathbb{Z}$).** Let $m, n \ge 1$ and $d = \gcd(m, n)$. Then $((m) : (n)) = (m/d)$. Indeed $m \mid r n$ means $(m/d) \mid r (n/d)$, and since $m/d$ and $n/d$ are coprime, this holds if and only if $(m/d) \mid r$. So $((12) : (8)) = (3)$ and $((12) : (5)) = (12)$.

**Counterexample (The extension of a prime need not be prime).** Let $f : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}[i]$ be the inclusion. The prime $(5)$ extends to $5 \mathbb{Z}[i]$, which is not prime: $(2 + i)(2 - i) = 5$, but $2 + i \notin 5 \mathbb{Z}[i]$. The contraction of $(2 + i)$ is a prime ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ that contains $5$, so it is $(5)$. Hence $(5)^e = 5 \mathbb{Z}[i] \subsetneq (2 + i)$.

### The library around it

Products and coprimality are in `Mathlib.RingTheory.Ideal.Operations`, `Ideal.map` and `Ideal.comap` in `Mathlib.RingTheory.Ideal.Maps`, the ideal quotient in `Mathlib.RingTheory.Ideal.Colon`. Common errors:

- `Ideal.map f I` is the extension, not the image set `f '' I`. They agree only for surjective `f`.
- `I.colon J` takes `J` as a set. For `Ideal.span {x}` use `Ideal.mem_colon_span_singleton`.

### Exercises

1. (A) Compute $((18) : (12))$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ and the annihilator of $[6]$ in $\mathbb{Z}/18$.
2. (A) Show that $(I : J)$ is the largest ideal $K$ with $K J \subseteq I$.
3. (A) Show that $3 \mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a prime ideal. Hint: $\mathbb{Z}[i]/(3) \cong \mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x^2 + 1)$, and $-1$ is not a square modulo $3$.
4. (B) Let $f : R \to S$ be a homomorphism and $J_1, J_2$ ideals of $S$. Show $(J_1 : J_2)^c \subseteq (J_1^c : J_2^c)$. Hint: for a strict inclusion take $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}[x]$, $J_1 = (2x)$, $J_2 = (x)$.
5. (B) In $k[x, y]$ let $I = (x + y)$, $J = (x)$, $K = (y)$. Show $I \cap (J + K) = I$ but $(I \cap J) + (I \cap K) \ne I$. Hint: $I \cap J = (x (x + y))$, so the sum lies in $(x, y)(x + y)$.
6. (C) Let $f : R \to S$ be a homomorphism and $P$ a prime ideal of $R$. Show that $P$ is the contraction of a prime ideal of $S$ if and only if $P^{ec} = P$. Hint: apply the theorem of Section 2 on primes that avoid a multiplicative set to $P^e$ and $f(R \setminus P)$.

### Notes and history

Dedekind introduced products of ideals in rings of algebraic integers. The terms extension and contraction follow Atiyah and Macdonald, *Introduction to Commutative Algebra* (1969), Chapter 1.
